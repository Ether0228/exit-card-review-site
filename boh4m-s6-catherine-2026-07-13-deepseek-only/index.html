补救模块

err-individual-ethics

Students may believe that individual ethics come from company codes, government regulations, or are the same for everyone in a culture. In reality, individual ethics are personal core beliefs about right and wrong, shaped by family, school, peers, media, and life experiences, and they evolve over time.

Explain: Individual ethics are your personal core beliefs about right and wrong. They are not the same as organizational ethics (company culture) or laws. Your ethics are shaped by many influences: family, school, peers, social media, and unexpected events. They can change as you gain experience. For example, Catherine's personal ethics reject arbitrary gender/age norms, and she values honest feedback. Sun Ruijia feels that staying within legal bounds is sufficient, but that is a personal ethical stance. Vivian (Zhu Hongyu) lists honesty, responsibility, and respect as her core ethics, influenced by parents, teachers, and social norms.

Example: Consider school dress codes: In Chinese K-12 schools, uniforms are required with no makeup or dyed hair; Canadian high schools allow personal styling. A student from China might personally believe uniforms are important for discipline (individual ethics shaped by culture), while a Canadian student might value personal expression. Neither is wrong; they reflect different social norms that shape individual ethics.

err-organizational-ethics

Students often confuse organizational ethics with individual ethics or legal compliance, failing to recognize that they are rooted in company culture and stakeholder priorities.

Explain: Organizational ethics are not just the personal ethics of leaders or compliance with laws; they are shaped by the company's internal culture and the priorities of all stakeholders (customers, employees, communities, creditors, environment). They guide how the organization conducts itself beyond legal minimums.

Example: A company may have a culture that values inclusivity, so its ethics would guide it to avoid discriminatory dress codes, even if not legally required. This reflects organizational ethics rooted in culture and stakeholder considerations.

err-ethical-behavior

Students often equate ethical behavior with legal behavior, personal preferences, or profit maximization, rather than understanding that ethical behavior aligns with widely accepted social norms, which can vary across cultures.

Explain: Ethical behavior is defined as behavior that aligns with widely accepted social norms – the shared values of a large group. This is different from legality: some actions may be legal but unethical (e.g., exploiting a legal loophole to harm others), and some unethical actions may not be illegal. Social norms vary across cultures, so what is considered ethical in one context may differ in another. A useful self-check: if you can comfortably disclose a decision to your family, see it published in the news, and sleep guilt-free, it is likely ethical.

Example: In many Canadian high schools, students can choose their own clothes and hairstyles; in many Chinese K-12 schools, uniforms are required and makeup/dyed hair are banned. Both norms are ethical within their respective cultural contexts because they follow the accepted social standards of that group.

err-unethical-behavior

Students often confuse unethical behavior with illegal behavior, not realizing that unethical actions violate social norms which may or may not be illegal.

Explain: Unethical behavior is action that violates widely accepted social norms. It is not necessarily illegal. Social norms are shared standards of acceptable behavior that vary across cultures. For example, school dress codes differ between Chinese K-12 (uniforms required, no makeup or dyed hair) and Canadian high schools (personal styling allowed). Breaking a school's dress code is unethical (violates norm) but not illegal.

Example: In a Canadian high school, wearing a T-shirt with a controversial slogan may be allowed by law but could be considered unethical if it disrupts the learning environment. Similarly, using a cheat sheet during a test is unethical (violates academic integrity norms) but may not be illegal in many jurisdictions.

err-social-norms

Student fails to recognize that social norms are shared standards of acceptable behavior that vary across cultures and are not necessarily the same as laws.

Explain: Social norms are the shared standards of acceptable behavior within a group. They vary across different cultures and geographic scales. For example, ethical behavior aligns with widely accepted social norms, while unethical behavior violates those norms. It's important to note that not all unethical behavior is illegal, and social norms can differ significantly between societies.

Example: Consider school dress codes: In Chinese K-12 schools, students are required to wear uniforms, no makeup, and no dyed hair. In Canadian high schools, students are allowed personal styling. This illustrates how social norms about appearance differ across cultures.

err-factors-shaping-individual-ethics

Students often think that individual ethics are static and solely influenced by family, ignoring the role of school, peers, media, and life experiences in shaping and evolving personal ethics.

Explain: Individual ethics are personal core beliefs about right and wrong that are shaped by multiple factors: family upbringing, school education, peer influence, social media exposure, and unexpected situational events. These ethics evolve over time as you gain new experiences. For example, a person's view on honesty might be influenced by a teacher's praise or a friend's betrayal, not just their parents.

Example: Consider Zhu Hongyu (Vivian) from the lesson: her core ethics of honesty, responsibility, and respect came from her parents, teachers, and social norms. This shows multiple sources.
