Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students often think scarcity only happens during emergencies or when prices rise, confusing it with shortage.

Remediation → err-scarcity-vs-shortage | Retest → err-scarcity-vs-shortage

  1. A. A temporary situation where a product is unavailable due to supply chain disruption.
    That describes a shortage, not scarcity. Scarcity is a permanent condition of unlimited wants vs limited resources.
  2. B. A condition that exists when demand exceeds supply for a particular good at a specific time.
    That's a shortage again. Scarcity is universal and ongoing.
  3. ✓ C. The fundamental economic problem that wants exceed the available supply of resources at any time.
    Correct. Scarcity is the basic condition that forces us to make choices.
  4. D. Only occurs for natural resources like oil and diamonds.
    Scarcity applies to all resources, not just natural ones. Time, labor, and capital are also scarce.

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students often include past payments (like tuition) in opportunity cost even though they are sunk and not affected by the current decision.

Remediation → err-opportunity-cost-including-sunk-cost | Retest → err-opportunity-cost-including-sunk-cost

  1. A. $20,000 (tuition) + $2,000 (books) = $22,000
    This only includes explicit costs and ignores the foregone salary. Also, it includes all costs without considering what is truly given up.
  2. ✓ B. $16,000 (foregone salary) + $20,000 (tuition) + $2,000 (books) = $38,000
    Correct. Opportunity cost includes both explicit costs (tuition+books) and implicit costs (foregone salary).
  3. C. $16,000 (foregone salary) only
    This ignores explicit costs. The tuition and books are also part of the cost of attending university.
  4. D. $16,000 + $20,000 + $2,000 + any other costs already incurred, like application fees paid last month
    Application fees are sunk costs and should not be included in opportunity cost of the current decision.

    补救模块

    err-scarcity-vs-shortage

    Thinks scarcity is a temporary condition that only arises when supplies run low, rather than a permanent condition of limited resources.

    Explain: Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem that human wants for goods and services exceed the available supply of resources. It is a permanent condition that exists at all times, regardless of price or availability. In contrast, a shortage is a temporary situation where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price, often due to disruptions or hoarding. For example, during the pandemic, masks were in short supply, but the underlying resource (labor to produce masks) is not permanently scarce in the same sense as oil or time. Scarcity forces choices; shortage can be resolved by price adjustments or production increases.

    Example: Gold is scarce because there is a limited quantity on Earth. Even if no gold is being sold at a given moment, it remains scarce. By contrast, a shortage of gold jewelry during a holiday rush is temporary and can be alleviated by increasing supply or raising prices.

    err-opportunity-cost-including-sunk-cost

    Sunk costs, which are already incurred and cannot be recovered, are mistakenly included in the opportunity cost calculation.

    Explain: Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative foregone when making a choice. It only includes future costs and benefits, not past expenses that cannot be changed. Sunk costs are irrelevant to current decisions.

    Example: A student deciding between college and work: opportunity cost includes foregone salary and current tuition, but not application fees already paid.

    err-explicit-implicit-confusion

    Students often believe that only direct monetary payments count as costs, failing to recognize the value of resources they own (e.g., their own time or property) as implicit costs.

    Explain: In economics, costs are divided into explicit and implicit costs. Explicit costs are direct, out-of-pocket monetary payments made to others (e.g., wages, rent, supplies). Implicit costs are the opportunity costs of using resources that you already own; they represent forgone income or benefits from the next best alternative use of those resources. For example, if you quit a job to start a business, your foregone salary is an implicit cost. Understanding both types is essential for calculating economic profit.

    Example: Consider a manager who leaves a job paying $16,000 per year to open her own restaurant. She uses her own building (which could rent for $24,000 per year) and her own savings (which could earn $20,000 in interest). Her restaurant has total revenue of $80,000 and explicit costs of $20,000 (supplies, wages). The implicit costs are: foregone salary ($16,000), foregone rent ($24,000), and foregone interest ($20,000). Total implicit = $60,000. Accounting profit = $80,000 - $20,000 = $60,000. Economic profit = $80,000 - $20,000 - $60,000 = $0. This shows that even though accounting profit is positive, economic profit is zero because all resources are earning exactly their opportunity cost.

    err-accounting-profit-as-true-profit

    Students think accounting profit is the complete measure of profitability and overlook the opportunity costs of using own resources.

    Explain: Accounting profit = total revenue − explicit costs. Economic profit = total revenue − explicit costs − implicit costs (opportunity costs). A business can have positive accounting profit but zero or negative economic profit if implicit costs are high. Economic profit tells whether the firm is truly earning more than the next best alternative.

    Example: Consider a manager who leaves a $12,000 salary to open a restaurant. She uses her own building (foregone rent $24,000). Annual revenue $80,000, explicit costs (wages, supplies) $44,000. Accounting profit = $80,000 − $44,000 = $36,000. But implicit costs = $12,000 (salary) + $24,000 (rent) = $36,000. Economic profit = $80,000 − $44,000 − $36,000 = $0. The business earns exactly what she gave up, so it is not a superior use of resources.

    err-ppc-constant-vs-increasing

    Students believe that the production possibilities curve is always a straight line, and they do not understand that a concave (bowed-outward) shape reflects increasing marginal opportunity cost as more of one good is produced.

    Explain: The production possibilities curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce. Its shape reveals how opportunity cost behaves. A straight-line PPC means constant marginal opportunity cost: each additional unit of one good always costs the same amount of the other good. A concave (bowed-outward) PPC means increasing marginal opportunity cost: as you produce more of one good, you must give up increasing amounts of the other good. This typically occurs because resources are not equally suited to producing both goods—first you use the most efficient resources, then less efficient ones, raising the opportunity cost.

    Example: Consider an economy that can produce either robots or wheat. With a straight-line PPC, producing every 10 robots costs exactly 20 tons of wheat. With a concave PPC, producing the first 10 robots costs 20 tons of wheat, but producing the next 10 robots costs 24 tons of wheat because the best wheat land must be converted, giving up more wheat per robot.

    err-ppc-shift-direction

    The student confuses which events increase or decrease an economy's production capacity, leading to incorrect inward/outward PPC shifts.

    Explain: The production possibilities curve (PPC) shifts outward (to the right) when the economy's productive capacity increases due to factors like technological advancements, increases in natural resources, or beneficial government policies. It shifts inward (to the left) when capacity decreases due to disasters, resource depletion, or harmful policies. The key is whether the event expands or contracts the total amount of goods/services that can be produced.

    Example: A new farming technology that increases crop yields per hectare allows the economy to produce more food and other goods with the same resources, shifting the PPC outward. Conversely, a drought that destroys farmland reduces output capacity, shifting the PPC inward.
