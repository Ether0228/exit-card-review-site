Q1. Which statement best distinguishes process theories of motivation from content theories?

errorId: err-process-theories-confuse-with-content | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Compare theories of how to motivate individuals and teams in a productive work e

Official: C3 | Node: BOH4M-C-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/business1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Confusing process theories (how motivation works) with content theories (what motivates).

Remediation → err-process-theories-confuse-with-content | Retest → err-process-theories-confuse-with-content

  1. A. Process theories focus on what motivates people, while content theories focus on how motivation occurs.
    This reverses the roles. Remember: content theories identify the 'what' (needs like Maslow's), while process theories explain the 'how' (decision-making).
  2. ✓ B. Process theories explain how people decide to exert effort, while content theories identify the needs that drive behavior.
    Correct! Process theories like equity and expectancy describe the cognitive process behind motivation, whereas content theories like Maslow's focus on internal needs.
  3. C. Process theories are based on reinforcement, while content theories are based on goal setting.
    Reinforcement is a process theory, and goal setting is also a process theory. This classification is inaccurate.
  4. D. Process theories are only used in business, while content theories are only used in psychology.
    Both sets of theories are applied across fields, including business management.

Q2. A manager gives all team members the same bonus regardless of performance, explaining 'I treat everyone equally.' According to equity theory, this approach may cause problems because:

errorId: err-equity-eq-vs-eq | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Compare theories of how to motivate individuals and teams in a productive work e

Official: C3 | Node: BOH4M-C-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/business1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Confusing equity (fairness) with equality (sameness).

Remediation → err-equity-eq-vs-eq | Retest → err-equity-eq-vs-eq

  1. A. Employees always prefer unequal pay distributions.
    Equity theory does not say people always want unequal pay; it says they want rewards proportional to their inputs.
  2. ✓ B. Equity theory suggests that fairness means tailoring rewards to individual contributions, not giving everyone the same.
    Correct! Equity theory focuses on a fair balance between what employees give (inputs) and what they receive (outputs). Identical rewards for unequal contributions create perceived inequity.
  3. C. Equality always leads to guilt among high performers.
    Guilt can occur in over-reward situations, but equality alone isn't guaranteed to cause guilt; the theory focuses on the perception of fairness relative to others.
  4. D. Equity theory states that identical rewards increase motivation because they uphold equality.
    Equity theory values fairness over equality. Identical rewards can demotivate high performers who feel under-rewarded.

Q3. An employee believes that if they work hard, they can meet their sales target (high expectancy), and they trust that meeting the target will lead to a bonus (high instrumentality). However, the employee is not motivated because the bonus is only $10. Which component of expectancy theory is low?

errorId: err-expectancy-ignoring-valence | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: analyze | OSSD: Compare theories of how to motivate individuals and teams in a productive work e

Official: C3 | Node: BOH4M-C-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/business1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Believing that high expectancy alone ensures motivation without considering instrumentality or valence.

Remediation → err-expectancy-ignoring-valence | Retest → err-expectancy-ignoring-valence

  1. A. Expectancy
    Expectancy is high because the employee believes effort will lead to performance. The problem isn't expectancy.
  2. B. Instrumentality
    Instrumentality is high because the employee trusts that performance leads to the bonus. The issue is with the value of the bonus itself.
  3. ✓ C. Valence
    Correct! Valence refers to the value an individual places on the expected outcome. Even if expectancy and instrumentality are high, low valence (the $10 bonus feels worthless) will undermine motivation.
  4. D. Equity
    Equity is part of a different theory. Expectancy theory's three components are expectancy, instrumentality, and valence.

补救模块

err-process-theories-confuse-with-content

The student treats process theories as lists of needs or rewards and treats content theories as explanations of how motivation is calculated. They cannot distinguish 'what motivates' from 'how motivation happens.'

Explain: Process theories describe how individuals assign value to rewards and decide on work effort based on personal preferences, available support, and expected outcomes. They focus on the mental sequence that leads to motivation. Content theories, by contrast, identify the specific needs that drive behavior (for example, Maslow's hierarchy). The key difference: content theories answer 'WHAT do people need?' while process theories answer 'HOW do people decide to put in effort?' Equity theory, expectancy theory, goal setting theory, and reinforcement theory are all process theories because they explain the mechanism of motivation.

Example: An employee who learns that a coworker with less experience received the same raise feels unmotivated. Equity theory explains this by analyzing how the employee compares their inputs and outputs to others—that is a process. If instead we say 'the employee has a need for recognition,' we are using a content theory because we are naming a need, not explaining the calculation.

err-equity-eq-vs-eq

Believing that equity means treating everyone the same (equality), rather than providing individualized support based on needs and contributions.

Explain: Equity theory focuses on fairness perceptions in the workplace. Equity is not the same as equality. Equality means giving everyone identical resources, while equity means tailoring support to each person's individual needs. When rewards are equal but contributions differ, employees compare their input to their output and the output of others. Underpayment leads to anger; overpayment leads to guilt. To be fair, managers must balance inputs and outcomes so each employee feels valued relative to their effort.

Example: A manager gives every employee the same annual bonus, thinking this is fair. According to equity theory, high performers who contribute more to the team will perceive the equal bonus as underpayment, because the reward does not reflect their higher input. This anger may reduce their motivation. Equity, instead, would tailor the bonus to each person's performance and responsibilities, making rewards feel fair even though they are not identical.

err-expectancy-ignoring-valence

The student mistakenly believes that high expectancy (confidence that effort leads to performance) alone guarantees motivation, without considering instrumentality (performance leads to outcomes) and valence (the value of the outcome).

Explain: Expectancy theory says motivation depends on three variables: expectancy (belief that effort leads to performance), instrumentality (belief that performance leads to outcomes), and valence (value placed on the outcome). All three must be high for strong motivation. If any one is low, motivation drops. A student who only looks at expectancy misses the fact that employees need to also see a clear link between performance and rewards, and they need to actually value those rewards.

Example: An employee is confident that working harder will help them reach their sales target (high expectancy) and trusts that reaching the target will earn a bonus (high instrumentality). But the bonus is only a $15 gift card. The employee doesn't care about that reward, so valence is low. Even with high expectancy and instrumentality, motivation is weak because the outcome isn't valuable to the employee.

err-goal-setting-vague-goals

The student thinks motivation comes from merely having a goal, without considering goal setting theory's requirements: goals must be specific, challenging, and prioritized, with timely midpoint feedback and rewards for completion. Vague goals fail because they do not focus effort or signal what success looks like.

Explain: Goal setting theory explains that vague goals like 'do your best' are weak motivators. Effective goals are specific (so the employee knows exactly what to do), challenging (to push performance), prioritized (so attention goes to the most important target), and supported with timely midpoint feedback and rewards for completion. Without these elements, goals do not direct effort or create commitment.

Example: Compare 'Try to improve sales if possible' with 'Increase sales by 5% within the next quarter.' The second goal is specific, measurable, time-bound, and challenging. It gives a clear target and a deadline, so it motivates effort more powerfully.

err-reinforcement-punish-vs-neg

The student believes that negative reinforcement and punishment are the same because both involve unpleasant stimuli, failing to notice that negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus to increase desired behavior, while punishment adds an unpleasant stimulus to reduce undesired behavior.

Explain: In reinforcement theory, behavior can be shaped in three ways: positive reinforcement adds a pleasant stimulus to increase a behavior, negative reinforcement removes an unpleasant stimulus to increase a behavior, and punishment adds an unpleasant stimulus to decrease a behavior. The confusion often arises because both negative reinforcement and punishment involve unpleasant situations, but they work in opposite directions: negative reinforcement increases the frequency of a desired behavior by taking away something the person does not like, while punishment decreases the frequency of an undesired behavior by adding something the person does not like. To classify correctly, always ask two questions: Is a stimulus being added or removed? Is the behavior increasing or decreasing? Removing an unpleasant condition to reward a good behavior is negative reinforcement; adding an unpleasant condition to stop a bad behavior is punishment.

Example: A manager removes a strict daily progress report requirement after an employee consistently meets deadlines, and the employee is more likely to keep meeting deadlines. Because the manager removed an unpleasant requirement (the daily report) and the desired behavior (meeting deadlines) increased, this is negative reinforcement. If, instead, the manager gave the employee a written warning for missing a deadline, the warning would add an unpleasant stimulus to reduce the behavior of missing deadlines — that is punishment.

err-incentive-plan-confusion

The student thinks profit sharing and gain sharing are interchangeable terms, not recognizing that profit sharing distributes company-wide profits, while gain sharing rewards specific operational gains like reduced waste or increased efficiency.

Explain: Profit sharing and gain sharing are both incentive plans, but they differ in what they reward. Profit sharing gives employees a share of the company's overall profits. Gain sharing rewards employees for specific improvements in performance, such as reducing waste, improving quality, or cutting costs. The key is: profit = company-wide results, gain = specific improvements.

Example: Company A gives employees 15% of its annual total profits—that's profit sharing because it depends on overall company success. Company B gives employees 3% of the savings from reduced material waste—that's gain sharing because it rewards a specific operational improvement.
