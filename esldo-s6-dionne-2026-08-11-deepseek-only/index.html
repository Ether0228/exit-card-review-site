Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Confusing autobiography with biography, thinking a biography is written by the subject.

Remediation → err-autobio-vs-bio | Retest → err-autobio-vs-bio

  1. ✓ A. The person themselves
    Correct! An autobiography is a life story written by the author about their own life.
  2. B. A close friend
    No, that would be a biography written by someone else. An autobiography is written by the subject.
  3. C. A professional writer
    A professional writer might write a biography, but an autobiography is self-written. Think 'auto' = self.
  4. D. A family member
    This would be a biography or memoir written by a relative, not an autobiography. The author must be the subject.

补救模块

err-autobio-vs-bio

The student confuses who writes an autobiography versus a biography, thinking a biography is self-written or an autobiography is written by a third party.

Explain: An autobiography is a life story written by the author themselves, while a biography is written by a third party. To write an autobiography, sort personal information, organize into coherent paragraphs, and integrate the paragraphs into a complete work.

Example: When you write about your own hometown (e.g., Shanghai) and your hobby (e.g., piano), that is part of an autobiography because you are telling your own story. If a friend wrote about your life, that story would be a biography.

err-forms-fields

The student believes every official form asks for identical fields, not realizing that while common provincial forms share core personal details, forms vary based on their purpose.

Explain: In Ontario, common official forms like the Ontario Health Card application, driver’s license application, and OSAP student financial aid application all require the same basic personal information: full name, date of birth, residential address, and contact information. However, not every form asks for every possible field. Forms are designed for different purposes, so they may add or omit fields accordingly. Always read each form carefully to see exactly what is being requested.

Example: An Ontario Health Card application typically asks for your full name, date of birth, residential address, and contact information. It does NOT normally require a passport number. In contrast, an OSAP application may ask for additional financial information because it is used to determine student financial aid. The core fields stay the same, but extra fields depend on the form’s goal.

err-safe-topics-religion

The student believes religion is an appropriate casual conversation topic for initial meetings in Canada.

Explain: In Canada, some topics are safe for casual first meetings, such as weather, sports, pets, food, movies, hobbies, travel, and weekend plans. Other topics are sensitive and should be avoided because they are considered too personal. Religion is one of these sensitive topics. Asking about someone's religion can make them feel uncomfortable or judged, so it is not a good idea for small talk.

Example: When meeting a new classmate, you could say, 'The weather is nice today, have you tried any good food in Toronto?' This is safe and friendly. You should NOT say, 'What religion do you follow?' because that is too personal for a first meeting.

err-polite-modals-informal

The student thinks 'can you' and 'could you' are equally polite, or uses direct commands like 'Pass me the salt' without any polite modal. They do not yet understand that 'could you + base verb' is the expected polite request structure in Canadian English.

Explain: In English, we use different modal verbs to show politeness. For polite requests, use 'could you + base verb' (e.g., 'Could you pass the salt?'). Use 'would you like to + base verb' for invitations and 'would you mind + verb-ing' for polite requests. 'Can you' is less formal and can sound direct; commands like 'Give me the salt' are even more abrupt. In Canadian social settings, using 'could' sounds more respectful.

Example: Instead of 'Pass me the salt.' or 'Can you pass me the salt?', say 'Could you please pass me the salt?'

err-softening-omission

The student thinks direct negative statements are always clear and acceptable, but in Canadian culture, delivering bad news or corrections without a softening phrase can sound harsh or rude. They do not yet understand that words like 'actually', 'unfortunately', and 'I'm afraid that' help maintain politeness.

Explain: In Canadian social interactions, soften negative or corrective statements with 'actually', 'unfortunately', or 'I'm afraid that' to avoid sounding harsh. These phrases signal consideration and keep the conversation comfortable.

Example: Instead of saying 'I can't come to the party,' say 'Unfortunately, I can't come to the party.' Or instead of 'You're wrong,' say 'Actually, I think the meeting is at 3 p.m.'

err-body-language-misinterpret

Closed body language such as crossed arms or frowning is misinterpreted as friendly, while open body language like smiling or relaxed posture is seen as unfriendly.

Explain: In Canada, open body language (smiling, eye contact, relaxed posture, open palms) signals friendliness. Closed body language (crossed arms, frown, turned away) signals disinterest or discomfort. Recognizing these cues helps you respond appropriately in social interactions.

Example: When you meet someone new, if they smile and keep eye contact, they feel friendly. If they cross their arms and look away, they may feel uncomfortable or uninterested.

err-personal-space-close

They think standing too close or touching is acceptable or even friendly in Canada and do not notice the other person's discomfort cues.

Explain: In Canada, keep a comfortable personal space between yourself and unfamiliar people. Do not touch without asking permission. If the other person shows discomfort—such as stepping back, crossing arms, or turning away—adjust your distance to give them more room. Open body language, like smiling and relaxed posture, signals friendliness, but closed body language can mean they feel uncomfortable.

Example: At orientation, you greet a new classmate. You step in very close, but your classmate frowns and takes a step back. That is a non-verbal cue that you are too close. Move back and give them more space.
