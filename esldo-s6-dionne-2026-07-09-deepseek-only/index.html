补救模块

err-narrative-fiction-only

Students may think narratives must be fictional; they need to understand non-fictional narratives (biographies, etc.) also count.

Explain: A narrative is a structured story that can be based on real events (non-fictional) or imagined events (fictional). Both types include characters, setting, plot, conflict, point of view, and theme. Examples of non-fictional narratives include biographies, autobiographies, news articles, and travelogues. Fictional narratives include novels, short stories, and films. Understanding that narratives can be non-fiction helps you read and write a wider range of stories.

Example: A biography of Marie Curie is a narrative because it tells the story of her life with a beginning, middle, and end, even though it is based on real events. Similarly, the fairy tale 'Cinderella' is a fictional narrative because it is imagined.

err-plot-structure-order

Students may think the climax comes before rising action or confuse the placement of conflict and rising action. They need to understand that exposition sets the scene, conflict is introduced, rising action builds tension, climax is the turning point, falling action leads to resolution.

Explain: The standard plot structure follows a specific order to build a compelling story. It begins with exposition (introducing characters and setting), then conflict (the problem), rising action (events that build tension), climax (the peak moment), falling action (events after the climax), and resolution (how the story ends). This order ensures logical progression.

Example: In the sample narrative 'Meeting a New Friend', the exposition introduces the main character and the park setting. The conflict arises when the character feels lonely. Rising action includes approaching a stranger. The climax is the first conversation. Falling action shows them laughing. Resolution is planning to meet again.

err-show-dont-tell-overuse

Students may think 'show, don't tell' means never state any emotion directly, leading to overly vague descriptions.

Explain: The 'show, don't tell' principle encourages writers to use descriptive actions and sensory details to let readers infer characters' emotions and traits. However, direct telling can be used for clarity or emphasis. Overusing showing can make writing vague or confusing. The goal is balance: show most of the time, but tell when necessary.

Example: For instance, instead of 'He was nervous' (tell), you could write 'He tapped his foot and avoided eye contact.' (show). But if clarity is needed, 'He felt nervous' is acceptable.

err-missing-essential-elements

The student may omit one or more of the six core narrative elements (characters, setting, plot, central conflict, point of view, theme) from their narrative.

Explain: A complete narrative must include all six core elements: characters (who), setting (when/where), plot (what happens), central conflict (the problem), point of view (who tells the story), and theme (the message).

Example: In the narrative 'Meeting a New Friend', the characters include the narrator and the new friend, the setting is a park, the plot involves meeting and talking, the conflict is overcoming shyness, the point of view is first-person, and the theme is the value of friendship.
