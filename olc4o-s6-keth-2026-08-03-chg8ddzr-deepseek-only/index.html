补救模块

err-reflective-definition-misconception

They think reflective writing is the same as a diary or a simple narrative of what happened.

Explain: Reflective writing is a deep response to an experience, opinion, or event. It goes beyond simply describing what happened. Instead, it focuses on exploring learning paths, enhancing self-awareness, and strengthening writing skills. A diary entry records daily events without analysis; reflective writing extracts themes, analyzes influences, and connects to past experiences and future actions.

Example: Diary entry: 'I failed my math test and felt sad.' Reflective writing: 'When I failed my math test, I realized I had been skipping homework. This showed me that consistent practice is essential. I will now set aside 30 minutes daily to review, and I plan to ask my teacher for help.' The reflective version examines causes and plans change.

err-reflective-vs-academic-formal

The student believes that because reflective writing shares formal and structured characteristics with academic writing, it must remain impersonal and avoid any expression of personal feelings or 'I'. In fact, reflective writing uniquely requires linking personal emotions and actions and using first-person perspective, while still maintaining formal style and logical coherence.

Explain: Reflective writing does share important similarities with academic writing—both require research, focus on core issues, use critical thinking, have structured paragraphs, use a formal style, and maintain logical coherence. However, reflective writing is not the same as a standard academic paper. It uniquely requires you to link personal emotions and actions, use a first-person perspective, integrate theory and practice, and sometimes use less formal expression. The key is not to choose between formality and personal voice, but to combine them: keep the clear structure and rigorous thinking of academic writing, while adding your own genuine reflection and insights.

Example: In an academic report, you might write: 'The data show a significant decrease in participant motivation.' In a reflective piece, you would instead write: 'I noticed that my own motivation dropped when I did not receive feedback, and I now understand how important timely responses are for maintaining engagement.' Both are written in a formal, structured style, but the reflective version adds personal experience and first-person perspective.

err-reflective-process-skip-analysis

The student thinks that simply recounting what happened is enough for reflective writing, so they skip the essential steps of theme extraction and factor analysis.

Explain: Reflective writing is a deep response to experiences, opinions, or events, focusing on exploring learning paths, enhancing self-awareness, and strengthening writing skills. It is not the same as a factual description or a simple retelling. The reflective writing process includes: detailed recording of the experience with noted moments of confidence or difficulty; theme extraction; factor analysis of influences; linking to past experiences and planning future responses. If you skip the theme extraction or factor analysis steps, you are just recounting events, not reflecting.

Example: Using the Gibbs reflective cycle from the lesson, if you wrote about a presentation, after describing what happened, you should extract themes (e.g., 'I feel anxious when I am not prepared') and analyze factors that influenced the experience (e.g., 'I did not practice out loud'). Then link to past experiences and plan future responses. Without these steps, the writing is only a story, not a reflection.

err-reflective-structure-intro-length

The student does not understand that the introduction in reflective writing should be about 10% of the total length, and instead writes an introduction that is either too long, too detailed, or too brief.

Explain: In reflective writing, the introduction should be about 10% of the total length. Its purpose is to state the theme and purpose of the reflection. For example, if your total piece is 1000 words, the introduction should be approximately 100 words. Keeping the introduction short and focused sets up the body, where topic sentences and examples carry the main analysis, and leads to a conclusion that summarizes viewpoints and improvement directions.

Example: If you write a 1000-word reflective piece, aim for a 100-word introduction. A good introduction briefly states what experience you are reflecting on and the theme you will explore, without diving into details or analysis. Details belong in the body paragraphs.

err-reflective-critical-separate

The student believes reflective writing and critical thinking are two distinct processes that do not influence each other, rather than recognizing that they are intertwined and jointly support behavior judgment, problem solving, and self-awareness improvement.

Explain: Reflective and critical thinking are not separate; they are intertwined. Reflection involves looking back at an experience, but doing it well requires critical thinking to analyze why things happened, what influenced the outcomes, and how to improve. Critical thinking, in turn, is deepened by reflection because personal experience provides concrete material for evaluation. Together they support behavior judgment, problem solving, and self-awareness improvement.

Example: Using the Gibbs reflective cycle, after describing a group project, you ask: 'What was I feeling? Why did I avoid contributing? What factors in the group led to that? How can I act differently next time?' The 'why' and 'how can I change' steps are critical thinking applied to your own experience. Without them, reflection is just a diary.

err-writing-foundation-mismatch

The student does not understand that effective writing starts by accurately identifying the audience and purpose, and then matching the content framework and genre to those elements. As a result, they may pick a genre that is too formal, too informal, or not aligned with the goal.

Explain: Before you write, ask: Who is my audience? What is my purpose? Then choose the genre that fits that audience and purpose. For example, if the audience is teenagers and the purpose is to convince them to make healthy choices, persuasive writing is the right genre. A formal academic report may be too distant, while a fictional story may entertain but not persuade.

Example: You are asked to write about the importance of healthy eating for teenagers. Audience: teenagers. Purpose: to convince them to eat healthily. Best genre: persuasive article. An academic research report does not match this audience and purpose, and a reflective essay focuses on personal experience instead of direct persuasion.

err-prewriting-no-outline

I don't need to outline or list ideas before writing; I can just start and figure it out as I go.

Explain: Pre-writing strategies help you organize your thoughts before drafting. They include background knowledge review, outlining, listing key points, relationship webs, and building three-dimensional frameworks. These strategies ensure your writing is logical, complete, and matches your audience and purpose. Without them, you may miss important ideas or lose focus. For a reflective piece, an outline would include an introduction (about 10% of the total length) stating theme and purpose, body paragraphs with topic sentences and examples, and a conclusion summarizing key viewpoints and improvements.

Example: Before writing a persuasive article for teenagers, you would first identify the audience and purpose, list your key points, and then create an outline with an introduction, body, and conclusion. This planning step makes the writing process smoother and the final piece more effective.

err-narrative-fictional

Student believes that a narrative can be fictional or can embellish with imaginary details, not realizing that non-fiction narrative writing must be based on true experiences.

Explain: Non-fiction narrative writing tells a true story from real life. It must use complete sentences, correct tense, subject-verb agreement, proper punctuation, and answer the 5W1H questions (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How). The most important rule is: it must be based on real experience—no invented events, characters, or settings.

Example: Instead of writing 'I flew to Mars on a rocket,' which is fictional, a non-fiction narrative would be 'I visited the Ontario Science Centre with my class last Tuesday. We saw the Mars exhibit and I learned how rockets work.' This is a real experience that actually happened.

err-interview-no-closing

The student thinks an interview write-up can simply end after the questions and answers, without a polite farewell or informing the interviewee where it will be published.

Explain: A complete interview write-up must include a polite closing that thanks the interviewee and tells them where the interview will be published. This follows the interview writing convention: polite welcome, valuable questions, and closing with publication information.

Example: After the final answer, you might write: 'Thank you for sharing your experiences. This interview will appear in the school newspaper next week.'

err-practical-writing-missing-format

Students believe a cover letter or resume is complete without standard format elements, but cover letters require date, place, contact information, clear statement of purpose, and closing thanks, and resumes require personal background, education, experience, and contact information.

Explain: Practical writing like cover letters and resumes has strict conventions. A cover letter must use a standard format with date, place, contact information, a clear statement of purpose, and a closing expression of thanks. A resume must include personal background, education, experience, and contact information. Missing any of these elements makes the writing incomplete and unprofessional. Always check the format checklist before submitting.

Example: A cover letter that begins with 'Dear Sir/Madam' but has no date, no sender address, no contact information, and no statement of purpose is missing required elements. A complete cover letter starts with date and place, includes contact information, states the purpose clearly, and ends with thanks.
