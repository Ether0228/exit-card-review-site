补救模块

err-myth-definition

Student thinks a myth is simply a false or fictional story with no cultural value, rather than a traditional story that explains natural phenomena, human experiences, or social norms.

Explain: A myth is not a fake story. It is a traditional story passed down through generations in a culture. Myths help explain natural events (like why the sun rises), human experiences (like love or jealousy), and social norms (like rules or values). For example, the Greek myth of Narcissus explains the origin of the word 'narcissist' and warns against vanity. Mythology is the collection of all myths from a culture.

Example: In the lesson, we learned that the Greek creation myth begins with Chaos, a formless void, and explains how the world and gods came to be. This is not a historical fact, but a story that ancient Greeks used to understand their world.

err-polytheism-monotheism

Student may think polytheism means belief in one god (monotheism) or no god (atheism), rather than belief in many gods.

Explain: Polytheism is the belief in many gods. The word 'poly' means many, and 'theism' means belief in gods. This is different from monotheism (belief in one god) and atheism (belief in no gods). Ancient Greece was polytheistic, with each god governing a distinct domain such as the sky, sea, love, or war.

Example: The ancient Greeks believed in many gods, including Zeus (king of the gods, sky), Poseidon (sea), and Athena (wisdom). This is polytheism.

err-chaos-chronos

The student confuses Chaos, the formless void that began the Greek creation myth, with Cronus, a Titan who later overthrew his father. They may also confuse it with Chronos, the personification of time.

Explain: In the Greek creation myth, the very first being was Chaos, a formless void. From Chaos emerged Gaia (Earth) and Uranus (Sky). Cronus was a Titan, born much later, who overthrew Uranus. Zeus was even later. Chronos is a different figure (the god of time).

Example: Think of the famous statue of Cronus swallowing his children; that was long after Chaos.

err-creation-order

Student may misorder the creation sequence: placing Zeus before Cronus or confusing Gaia and Uranus.

Explain: The Greek creation myth follows a specific sequence: 1) Chaos (formless void) appears first. 2) Gaia (Earth) and Uranus (Sky) emerge from Chaos. 3) They give birth to the Titans, Cyclopes, and Hecatoncheires. 4) Cronus (a Titan) overthrows Uranus and rules. 5) To prevent a prophecy, Cronus swallows his children. 6) Rhea saves Zeus by giving Cronus a stone. 7) Zeus grows up and leads a 10-year war (Titanomachy) against Cronus. 8) With help from the Cyclopes and Hecatoncheires, Zeus wins. 9) Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades divide the cosmos among themselves. 10) The 12 Olympian gods, led by Zeus, become the new rulers.

Example: A common error is putting Zeus before Cronus. But Cronus ruled first; Zeus was his son who later overthrew him after the Titanomachy.

err-hero-definition

Student thinks heroes are only superhuman or gods, not ordinary humans with brave traits.

Explain: In Greek mythology, heroes are brave and persistent characters who complete difficult journeys to solve collective problems. They can be mortal or demigod, but they are not necessarily gods. Their defining traits are courage, determination, and using their abilities to help others. For example, Hercules was a demigod (half-god, half-human) who became a hero through his 12 impossible trials, not because he was a god.

Example: Hercules is a Greek hero known for his strength and bravery, but he was not a full god—he was a demigod. His heroism came from completing 12 incredibly difficult tasks to protect people.

err-olympian-number

The student may think there are fewer or more than 12 major Olympian gods, or may confuse the domains of specific gods.

Explain: In Greek mythology, there are exactly 12 major Olympian gods. They are the most important deities who lived on Mount Olympus, each ruling a different domain. Knowing this helps you understand Greek culture and mythology.

Example: The 12 major Olympian gods are: Zeus (sky, king), Hera (marriage), Poseidon (sea), Demeter (agriculture), Athena (wisdom), Apollo (music, prophecy), Artemis (hunting), Ares (war), Aphrodite (love), Hephaestus (fire, forge), Hermes (messenger, travel), and Dionysus (wine, theater).
