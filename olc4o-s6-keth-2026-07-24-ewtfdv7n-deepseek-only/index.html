    补救模块

    err-OLC4O-interview-goal

    The student thinks the primary goal of a journalistic interview is to collect a generic list of facts about the interviewee, rather than to uncover a specific, emotion-driven personal narrative.

    Explain: In journalistic interviewing, the main objective is not to gather a dry list of biographical details (like age, job, hometown). Instead, it is to discover a unique, emotional story that reveals something personal about the interviewee. Think of it like 'Humans of New York': each interview captures a single, heartfelt moment, not a full biography. The W5H questions are just a starting point—the real gold comes from listening and asking follow-up questions that dig into feelings and personal experiences.

    Example: In the 'Humans of New York' project, one subject said: 'Here it's very hard to know if someone is genuine... Back in Ghana a smile means: "Welcome," or "How are you?" But here it's very hard for me to tell where it's coming from.' That is an emotion-driven narrative about culture and trust—far more compelling than listing where he was born or his job.

    err-OLC4O-w5h-framework

    The student believes the W5H framework must be used as a fixed list of questions asked in order, rather than as a flexible starting roadmap to guide the conversation.

    Explain: The W5H framework (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) is a structured starting roadmap for generating initial questions, but it is not a rigid script. The goal of an interview is to uncover an emotion-driven personal narrative, and the best details come from unplanned follow-up questions that arise naturally from the conversation. Using W5H as a checklist limits your ability to listen actively and adapt.

    Example: If you start with a W5H question like 'What happened?' and the interviewee says, 'I lost my job,' instead of moving to the next W5H ('When?'), you should follow up with 'How did that make you feel?' to get the emotional story. The W5H got you started, but the follow-up unlocked the narrative.

    err-OLC4O-followup-questions

    Students think pre-written questions are the main source of good material, but unplanned follow-up questions yield richer, emotion-driven details.

    Explain: In journalistic interviews, the most valuable story details come not from pre-written questions but from unplanned, context-driven follow-ups. Pre-written questions (like the 6 W5H questions) are a starting roadmap to open the conversation, but they often produce generic, surface-level answers. By actively listening and asking spontaneous follow-ups based on what the interviewee says, you dig deeper into personal, emotional narratives. The Question Engine Workflow shows this loop: Start → Planned Questions → Subject's Answer (listen carefully) → Unplanned Questions (follow-up) → back to listening. Let the conversation flow naturally; your best details will come from unplanned follow-up questions.

    Example: In the 'Humans of New York' project, an interviewer might start with 'Where are you from?' (a W5H question). If the person says 'Ghana,' a pre-written follow-up might be 'How long have you been here?' But an unplanned follow-up listening for emotion could be: 'You said 'back in Ghana a smile means welcome.' What does a smile mean to you in New York?' That leads to the powerful line: 'Here it's very hard to know if someone is genuine.'

    err-OLC4O-active-listening

    Students think that taking detailed verbatim notes is a recommended active listening technique, when in fact simplified keyword notes are preferred to maintain engagement.

    Explain: Active listening during an interview means staying fully present with the speaker. You should maintain steady eye contact to show engagement, take only simplified keyword notes so you can focus on the conversation, never interrupt the speaker, and avoid staring at your notes. This helps you pick up on emotional cues and generate meaningful follow-up questions.

    Example: Imagine a student interviewing a classmate who says, 'I moved here last year and it was really hard to make friends.' An active listener would maintain eye contact, jot down just 'moved last year, hard to make friends,' and then ask a follow-up like 'What was the hardest part?' instead of writing every word.
