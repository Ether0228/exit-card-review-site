Learning Goal: Distinguish between explicit and implicit bias.

Misconception: Students may think bias only appears as explicitly opinionated language.

Remediation → err-bias-explicit-only | Retest → err-bias-explicit-only

  1. A. Using words like "disastrous" and "horrifying" to describe an event.
    This is explicit bias because the language is openly opinionated and inflammatory.
  2. ✓ B. Presenting only data that supports one side of an argument while omitting contrary evidence.
    Correct. Implicit bias often works through omission or one-sided framing rather than obvious emotional words.
  3. C. Stating "The mayor made a terrible decision" without providing reasons.
    This is an explicit opinion, not implicit bias. The author directly states the judgment.
  4. D. Including a headline that says "Shocking Revelation Uncovered!"
    Sensational headlines may grab attention but don't necessarily indicate implicit bias; they are often explicit.

      补救模块

      err-bias-explicit-only

      Bias only exists when the author uses openly opinionated, inflammatory language; neutral-sounding texts are automatically unbiased.

      Explain: Bias is not limited to emotional language. Explicit bias is obvious: it uses openly opinionated or inflammatory words. Implicit bias is subtle: it may omit facts or present a one-sided framing that steers the reader. To catch implicit bias, you must verify against other sources and ask what information might be missing.

      Example: An article says: 'The new plaza will bring jobs and shoppers.' It quotes only the developer, never local residents. No harsh words appear, but leaving out the other side makes it implicitly biased. A cross-source check would reveal resident complaints about traffic and noise.

      err-denotation-connotation-confusion

      Students may conflate denotation and connotation, treating all word meanings as purely literal and ignoring emotional associations.

      Explain: Denotation is a word's literal dictionary meaning, while connotation is its emotional or cultural association. Authors choose words to shape reader perception, so a single word can carry an implicit judgment that goes beyond its dictionary definition.

      Example: Consider 'thrifty' versus 'cheap'. Both denote carefulness with money, but 'thrifty' implies a positive trait, while 'cheap' suggests stinginess. An author describing a character as 'thrifty' creates a favorable impression, whereas 'cheap' creates a negative one.

      err-text-structure-mix-up

      Thinks 'sequence' always means cause-effect, or that any text mentioning a problem uses problem-solution, without checking signal words and logical relationships.

      Explain: Informational texts use different organizational structures for different purposes. Chronological order arranges events by time, using words like 'first,' 'next,' 'then,' and 'finally.' Cause and effect explains why something happened and its result, using words like 'because,' 'so,' and 'therefore.' Problem-solution states a problem and proposes a solution. To identify structure, ask: Is the text mainly showing a time sequence, an explanation of cause/result, or a problem with a fix? Parentheses can add extra context but do not change the main structure.

      Example: Using the WWI case study: a chronological structure would list 'alliances formed → trench warfare developed → mechanized warfare used → Treaty of Versailles signed.' A cause-effect structure would say 'opposing alliances and mechanized warfare caused a deadly stalemate, leading to the Treaty of Versailles.' The same topic can be presented in different structures depending on the author's purpose.

      err-objectivity-assumption

      Students believe informational texts are simply neutral reports of facts and therefore do not look for the author's perspective, selective evidence, or emotional language.

      Explain: Objective reporting uses verified facts and a neutral tone. Perspective-driven prose includes personal value judgments and selective evidence. To critically read, identify the author's credentials, target audience, and core communicative purpose rather than assuming neutrality. Even factual-looking text can be one-sided; implicit bias works through omission or one-sided framing and requires cross-source verification.

      Example: In the WWI case study, a text that says 'the war was a senseless slaughter caused entirely by one nation's aggression' is not objective: it makes a value judgment ('senseless slaughter') and selects only one cause, ignoring that two opposing alliances contributed to the conflict. An objective report would present verified facts about both alliances and the trench warfare stalemate in a neutral tone.

      err-ai-misuse

      Students may believe AI can be used to generate entire assignments or fabricate evidence without academic integrity consequences.

      Explain: AI tools can be used as learning partners for structured analysis but must not generate fake evidence or unoriginal student work. Using AI to explain concepts is appropriate, but submitting AI-generated work as your own is plagiarism and academically dishonest.

      Example: Appropriate: asking AI to explain the difference between explicit and implicit bias so you can write your own analysis. Inappropriate: asking AI to write a full essay or create fake citations.
