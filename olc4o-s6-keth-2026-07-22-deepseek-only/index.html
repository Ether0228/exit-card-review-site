Q1. Which of the following best explains the main purpose of pre-writing in the writing process?

errorId: err-prewriting-is-optional | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Official: B1 | Node: OLC4O-B-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/english12curr.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students may think pre-writing is unnecessary for experienced writers or can be skipped.

Remediation → err-prewriting-is-optional | Retest → err-prewriting-is-optional

  1. A. It is an optional step that can be skipped if you are a good writer.
    Pre-writing is not optional — even experienced writers use it to organize thoughts and avoid writer's block.
  2. ✓ B. It helps overcome writer's block and creates a structured plan for drafting.
    Correct. Pre-writing provides a blueprint that makes drafting more efficient.
  3. C. It is only useful for beginners, not experienced writers.
    Pre-writing benefits writers at all levels by ensuring ideas are organized before drafting.
  4. D. It replaces the need for revising and editing.
    Pre-writing is a planning stage; revising and editing are separate later steps in the process.

Q2. What is the key difference between Listing and Outlining as pre-writing strategies?

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students think Listing and Outlining are the same or that Listing is just a rough outline.

Remediation → err-confusing-listing-outlining | Retest → err-confusing-listing-outlining

  1. ✓ A. Listing generates random ideas without structure, while Outlining organizes ideas hierarchically.
    Correct. Listing is a free-association brainstorm; Outlining imposes a logical structure.
  2. B. Listing is used for narrative writing, Outlining for academic essays.
    Both strategies can be used for any type of writing; the choice depends on the writer's goal.

  4. D. They are the same, just different names.
    They are distinct strategies: Listing is unstructured, Outlining is structured.

补救模块

err-prewriting-is-optional

Student believes pre-writing is unnecessary and can skip straight to drafting, underestimating its role in overcoming writer's block and organizing ideas.

Explain: Pre-writing is not optional; it is a crucial first step in the writing process. It overcomes 'blank page inertia' and improves drafting efficiency by providing a structured blueprint (fact bank). Without it, you risk writer's block and disorganized drafts.

Example: Consider a student who skips pre-writing and starts drafting a news report. They struggle to organize facts and get stuck after the first paragraph. With pre-writing (e.g., outlining), they could have created a clear structure and written more smoothly.

err-confusing-listing-outlining

Student believes Listing and Outlining are the same or that Listing is a rough version of Outlining, not understanding that Listing is an unstructured idea generation strategy and Outlining is a structured organization strategy.

Explain: Listing (also called Inventorying) is a pre-writing strategy where you rapidly list all ideas related to a topic without filtering or organizing. Then you group similar terms into categories. Outlining, on the other hand, takes pre-generated ideas and arranges them into a logical hierarchical structure with a thesis, supporting points, and evidence. Listing helps generate raw material; Outlining helps structure that material for drafting.

Example: If you are writing about climate change, Listing might produce: greenhouse gases, rising temperatures, melting ice, polar bears, policy, electric cars. Then you group them (e.g., causes, effects, solutions). Outlining would then organize these groups into a hierarchy: I. Introduction (thesis), II. Causes (greenhouse gases), III. Effects (rising temperatures, melting ice, polar bears), IV. Solutions (policy, electric cars), V. Conclusion.

err-cubing-only-description

The student believes Cubing requires only describing the topic, ignoring the other five perspectives (association, comparison, analysis, application, argumentation), leading to shallow content.

Explain: Cubing is a pre-writing strategy where you examine a topic from six different perspectives: description, association, comparison, analysis, application, and argumentation. Each perspective adds depth and ensures a comprehensive exploration. Using only description limits your ideas and results in superficial writing. To fully benefit from cubing, you must address all six perspectives.

Example: For the topic 'school uniforms': - Description: Uniforms are standardized clothing worn by students. - Association: Uniforms are often linked to discipline and equality. - Comparison: Uniforms vs. casual dress codes. - Analysis: Components include shirts, pants, colors, logos. - Application: How uniforms are used in schools to reduce distractions. - Argumentation: Argue that uniforms promote unity or suppress individuality.

err-advice-letter-paragraphs

Student believes an advice letter can be written as one continuous paragraph or does not understand the distinct function of each of the three paragraphs.

Explain: An advice letter must follow a clear three-paragraph structure. Paragraph 1 states your clear stance and core advice. Paragraph 2 provides supporting evidence, such as personal experience or logical comparison. Paragraph 3 gives actionable recommendations and an encouraging conclusion. Each paragraph has a specific job; mixing them together confuses the reader and weakens your message.

Example: Dear friend, I strongly advise you to join the school debate club. (Para 1: stance and core advice) I joined last year and improved my public speaking and critical thinking skills significantly. (Para 2: supporting evidence) I recommend you attend the next meeting on Thursday and sign up for the beginner workshop. (Para 3: actionable recommendations) Good luck!
