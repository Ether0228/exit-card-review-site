Q1. A person carries a 10 kg box horizontally across a room at constant speed. Which statement best describes the work done by the person on the box?

errorId: err-work-perpendicular-force | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of work, efficiency, power, gravitational potential

Official: D3 | Node: SPH3U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Thinking that any applied force does work regardless of direction.

Remediation → err-work-perpendicular-force | Retest → err-work-perpendicular-force

  1. ✓ A. Zero work because the lifting force is perpendicular to the horizontal displacement.
  2. B. 98 J of work because the box has weight.
    The weight is vertical, but displacement is horizontal, so no work is done by the person. Work requires force component in the direction of displacement.
  3. C. Work depends on how fast the person walks.
    Work is independent of speed when force is constant and displacement is horizontal; the perpendicular force does no work.
  4. D. Cannot determine without knowing the time taken.
    Work is independent of time; only force, displacement, and the angle between them matter.

Q5. A student says that a 1 kg ball on a 2 m high shelf has exactly 19.6 J of gravitational potential energy. Is this value always correct? Explain your reasoning in terms of gravitational potential energy and reference points.

errorId: err-gpe-reference-point | Bloom: evaluate | Scaffold: L3

Achievement: Thinking | Action: evaluate | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of work, efficiency, power, gravitational potential

Official: D3 | Node: SPH3U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Treating GPE as an absolute quantity independent of reference point.

Remediation → err-gpe-reference-point | Retest → err-gpe-reference-point

    Rubric: Full marks: Clearly states that the value depends on the reference point. A common reference is the floor, giving 19.6 J, but if the reference is the shelf itself, GPE = 0 J. So the statement is not always true. Partial: Mentions reference point but lacks clarity. Minimal: Restates the student's claim without critique.

    Sample: No, the value is not always true because gravitational potential energy is measured relative to a chosen reference point. If the reference point is the floor, then GPE = mgh = 1 kg * 9.8 N/kg * 2 m = 19.6 J. However, if the reference point is the shelf itself, the height is 0, so GPE = 0 J. The student's statement assumes a specific reference point that may not always be valid.

    补救模块

    err-work-perpendicular-force

    The student may think that any applied force does work regardless of its direction relative to displacement, not recognizing that only the component of force parallel to displacement contributes to work.

    Explain: Work is a scalar quantity calculated as W = F * d * cosθ, where θ is the angle between the force and displacement. When the force is perpendicular to the displacement (θ = 90°), cos90° = 0, so the work done is zero. Only the component of force parallel to displacement does work.

    Example: When you carry a backpack horizontally, you exert an upward force to support its weight. Since the displacement is horizontal and the force is vertical (perpendicular), the work done by that upward force is zero. The backpack moves horizontally because of other forces, but the vertical support force does no work.

    err-work-friction-sign

    The student believes work done by friction is positive, not understanding that friction opposes motion, so its work is negative.

    Explain: Work is a scalar, but its sign indicates the force's direction relative to displacement. Forces in the same direction as displacement do positive work; forces opposite do negative work. Friction always opposes motion, so its work is negative. Net work is the algebraic sum of all works. Example: applied force 41 N over 11 m gives W_app = 41 × 11 = 451 J. Friction 35 N opposite gives W_fric = -35 × 11 = -385 J. Net work = 451 + (-385) = 66 J.

    Example: From the lesson: A shopping cart is pushed with a 41 N horizontal force over 11 m, while friction of 35 N acts opposite. The net work is 66 J because friction does negative work.

    err-kinetic-energy-linear-velocity

    The student believes that kinetic energy is proportional to velocity (linear), rather than proportional to the square of velocity.

    Explain: Kinetic energy (KE) is the energy of motion. Its formula is KE = ½mv², where m is mass and v is speed. The velocity is squared, meaning if you double the speed, KE becomes four times larger (not two times). For example, if speed triples, KE becomes nine times larger. This is because v² grows faster than v.

    Example: Consider a 165 g (0.165 kg) hockey puck. When pushed from rest to 5.5 m/s, its KE = ½ * 0.165 * (5.5)² = 2.5 J. If the speed doubled to 11 m/s, KE = ½ * 0.165 * (11)² = 10 J, which is four times 2.5 J. So doubling speed quadruples KE.

    err-work-energy-theorem-application

    Student may think work-energy theorem applies to individual forces rather than net work.

    Explain: The work-energy theorem states that the total (net) work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy. It applies to the net work, which is the sum of work done by all forces. Do not apply it to individual forces separately; only the net work matters.

    Example: For a 165 g hockey puck pushed 0.50 m by a 5.0 N horizontal force (no friction), the net work is 2.5 J. Using W_net = ΔKE, and initial KE = 0, we get ½mv² = 2.5 J, so v = √(2×2.5/0.165) ≈ 5.5 m/s.

    err-gpe-reference-point

    The student may think that gravitational potential energy (GPE) is an absolute quantity, rather than depending on a chosen reference point.

    Explain: Gravitational potential energy (GPE) is the energy stored due to an object's position in a gravitational field. It is calculated as GPE = mgh, where h is the height relative to a chosen reference point. The value of GPE changes if you choose a different reference point. For example, a 1 kg ball on a 2 m high shelf has GPE = 19.6 J if the reference point is the floor, but GPE = 0 J if the reference point is the shelf itself. Always specify or agree on a reference point before calculating GPE.

    Example: A 1 kg book is 1.5 m above the floor. If we choose the floor as reference, GPE = 1 * 9.8 * 1.5 = 14.7 J. If we choose the table 1 m above the floor as reference, then the book's height is 0.5 m relative to the table, so GPE = 1 * 9.8 * 0.5 = 4.9 J.
