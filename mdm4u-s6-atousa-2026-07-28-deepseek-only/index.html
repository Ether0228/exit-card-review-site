Q1. Which of the following situations is most appropriate for modeling with a geometric distribution?

errorId: err-geo-confuse-binomial | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of discrete probability distributions, represent th

Official: B1 | Node: MDM4U-B-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student thinks binomial and geometric are the same, or chooses a scenario with fixed number of trials.

Remediation → err-geo-confuse-binomial | Retest → err-geo-confuse-binomial

  1. A. Counting how many successes you get when you roll a dice 10 times.
    This is binomial: fixed number of trials (10), counting successes.
  2. ✓ B. Rolling a dice until you get a 6, and counting how many rolls it took.
    Correct! Geometric distribution counts trials until the first success (getting a 6).
  3. C. Selecting 5 cards from a deck without replacement and counting how many are hearts.
    This is hypergeometric, not geometric; trials are dependent (no replacement) and you count successes in a fixed sample.
  4. D. Spinning a spinner 20 times and recording the outcomes.
    This is multinomial or categorical, not geometric; it doesn't focus on time until first success.

Q2. For a geometric distribution with success probability p = 0.25, what is P(Y = 4)?

errorId: err-geo-wrong-formula | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of discrete probability distributions, represent th

Official: B1 | Node: MDM4U-B-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student uses p^k, or (1-p)^k p (forgetting the -1 on the exponent), or uses binomial formula.

Remediation → err-geo-wrong-formula | Retest → err-geo-wrong-formula

  1. A. (0.75)^4 * 0.25 ≈ 0.0791
    Almost, but the exponent should be k-1 = 3, not 4. The correct exponent counts failures before the success.
  2. B. (0.25)^4 ≈ 0.0039
    You used p^k, but the formula includes failure probability (1-p) raised to k-1 times p.
  3. ✓ C. (0.75)^3 * 0.25 ≈ 0.1055
    Correct! P(Y=4) = (1-0.25)^(4-1) * 0.25 = (0.75)^3 * 0.25 ≈ 0.1055.
  4. D. 4 * 0.25 * (0.75)^3 ≈ 0.4219
    That looks like the binomial formula for exactly 1 success in 4 trials, not geometric. Geometric does not have a combinatorial coefficient.

Q3. In a geometric distribution with success probability p = 0.2, the expected value E(Y) = 1/p = 5. Interpret this value in the context of the distribution.

errorId: err-geo-expectation-misapplied | Bloom: evaluate | Scaffold: L3

Achievement: Thinking | Action: evaluate | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of discrete probability distributions, represent th

Official: B1 | Node: MDM4U-B-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Student thinks E(Y) guarantees the first success occurs exactly on the 5th trial, or that it's the most likely value.

Remediation → err-geo-expectation-misapplied | Retest → err-geo-expectation-misapplied

    Rubric: Full credit (2 points): Mentions that on average it takes 5 trials to achieve first success, and that this is a mean over many repetitions, not a guarantee. Partial credit (1 point): Only states that the expected number of trials is 5, but does not clarify it's an average. No credit: Says the first success always occurs on the 5th trial or misinterprets as mode.

    Sample: The expected value of 5 means that if you repeat this process many times, the average number of trials needed to get the first success will be about 5. It does not mean that the first success is guaranteed to happen on the 5th trial; some attempts may take fewer or more trials.

    补救模块

    err-geo-confuse-binomial

    The student thinks that any distribution involving repeated trials with two outcomes is binomial. They fail to recognize that geometric distribution counts trials until the first success, not the number of successes in a fixed number of trials.

    Explain: The geometric distribution models the number of independent trials needed to achieve the first success, where each trial has the same success probability p. In contrast, the binomial distribution counts the number of successes in a fixed number of trials. Key phrase: geometric asks 'how many trials until first success?', binomial asks 'how many successes in n trials?'.

    Example: Lawn mower pull start: p=0.2. Geometric: P(first success on 3rd pull) = (0.8)^2 * 0.2 = 0.128. Binomial would ask probability of getting exactly 1 success in 3 pulls, which is 3*0.2*(0.8)^2 = 0.384. They are different!

    err-geo-wrong-formula

    Student thinks the exponent for (1-p) should be k instead of k-1, or uses p^k directly, missing the required failures before success.

    Explain: The geometric distribution counts trials until first success. The probability that the first success occurs on trial k is P(Y=k) = (1-p)^(k-1) * p. The exponent (k-1) represents the number of failures before the success. A common mistake is using (1-p)^k or p^k. Remember: you must have k-1 failures (each with probability 1-p) and then one success (probability p).

    Example: For p = 0.2 (lawn mower pull start), P(Y=3) = (0.8)^2 * 0.2 = 0.128. This is correct because there are 2 failures (first two pulls) and then success on the third.

    err-geo-expectation-misapplied

    The student thinks the expected value of a geometric distribution equals the success probability p or the failure probability 1-p, instead of 1/p. They may also believe that the expected value guarantees the first success occurs on that trial.

    Explain: In a geometric distribution, the expected value E(Y) represents the average number of trials needed to get the first success. The correct formula is E(Y) = 1/p, where p is the success probability for each trial. For example, if p = 1/38, then on average it takes 38 trials. It does NOT mean that the first success will always happen on the 38th trial; it's just the long-run average. Common mistakes include thinking E(Y) = p or E(Y) = 1-p. Remember: the larger the success probability, the fewer trials expected.

    Example: Consider the roulette example where p = 1/38. The expected number of spins to win is 38. That means if you play many times, the average number of spins until your number appears is 38. Some attempts may succeed earlier (e.g., on the 5th spin) or later (e.g., on the 100th spin). The expected value does not guarantee a result on a specific trial.
