Q1. According to mercantilism, if Country X runs a trade surplus with Country Y, what is the most accurate description of Country Y's position?

errorId: err-mercantilism-zero-sum | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of key aspects of trade theories and explain how th

Official: E1 | Node: CIA4U-E-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student assumes trade can make both countries better off, importing a positive-sum idea into mercantilism.

Remediation → err-mercantilism-zero-sum | Retest → retest-err-mercantilism-zero-sum

  1. ✓ A. Country Y loses because mercantilism views trade as zero-sum.
    Correct. Mercantilism treats trade as a zero-sum game: one country's surplus is another's loss.
  2. B. Country Y also gains because both countries enjoy the same benefits from trade.
    That is the positive-sum view of later trade theories, not mercantilism. Mercantilism assumes one country's surplus is another's loss.
  3. C. Country Y is unaffected because mercantilism only focuses on exports.
    Mercantilism depends on trade balances; a surplus for X implies a deficit for Y, which mercantilists viewed as a loss.
  4. D. Country Y gains as long as the goods are cheaper than domestic production.
    That reasoning reflects comparative advantage logic, not mercantilism.

Q2. Which statement correctly distinguishes absolute advantage from comparative advantage?

errorId: err-absolute-comparative-mixup | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of key aspects of trade theories and explain how th

Official: E1 | Node: CIA4U-E-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student equates absolute advantage with lower opportunity cost, mixing the two concepts.

Remediation → err-absolute-comparative-mixup | Retest → retest-err-absolute-comparative-mixup

  1. ✓ A. Absolute advantage compares how much a country can produce with the same resources; comparative advantage compares opportunity cost.
    Correct. Absolute advantage is about productive efficiency; comparative advantage is about lower opportunity cost.
  2. B. Absolute advantage compares opportunity cost; comparative advantage compares output per worker.
    That is backwards. Absolute advantage is about efficiency measured by output per input; comparative advantage is about lower opportunity cost.
  3. C. Absolute advantage determines exports; comparative advantage determines imports.
    Both concepts can determine trade patterns; the distinction is in how advantage is measured, not which side of the border it applies to.
  4. D. The two concepts are interchangeable and state the same idea.
    They are related but distinct: a country can have absolute advantage in all goods yet still gain from specializing according to comparative advantage.

补救模块

err-mercantilism-zero-sum

The student treats mercantilism as a positive-sum theory, importing later trade theory logic into a 16th-17th century zero-sum framework.

Explain: Mercantilism (16th-17th century) is a trade theory that advocates maintaining a trade surplus by encouraging exports and restricting imports. It views trade as a zero-sum game: one country's surplus must be another country's loss. If Country X runs a surplus with Country Y, X's gain is Y's loss. This contrasts with Adam Smith's 1776 absolute advantage theory, which shows specialization and trade can be positive-sum and raise overall output and living standards.

Example: In the lesson's simplified two-country, two-product example, mercantilists would applaud a country that exports more than it imports, but they would see the trading partner's deficit as a loss. Under Adam Smith's 1776 view, both countries can gain by specializing in what they produce more efficiently, making trade positive-sum rather than zero-sum.

err-absolute-comparative-mixup

The student thinks absolute advantage means having the lower opportunity cost, or believes the two concepts are interchangeable. They may not realize that a country with absolute advantage in all goods can still benefit from specialization according to comparative advantage.

Explain: Absolute advantage, from Adam Smith's 1776 theory, means a country can produce a good more efficiently than another country—using the same resources to produce more output. Comparative advantage means a country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost, meaning it gives up less of another good to make it. These are different ideas: a country can have absolute advantage in every good, but it cannot have comparative advantage in every good. According to Heckscher-Ohlin theory, comparative advantage originates from differences in national factor endowments like land, labor, and capital. Specialization and trade improve overall output and raise living standards, as demonstrated by simple two-country, two-product models.

Example: In the simplified two-country, two-product example used in class, one country may be more efficient at producing both goods. Yet both countries still gain if each specializes in the good where it has the lower opportunity cost. The key is not who produces more, but who gives up less to produce it.

err-ho-factor-misconception

The student thinks Heckscher-Ohlin simply means a country with abundant factors becomes absolutely more efficient at producing everything using those factors, rather than recognizing that factor endowments create comparative advantage through lower opportunity cost.

Explain: Heckscher-Ohlin theory links comparative advantage to national factor endowments (land, labor, capital). More abundant factors lead to lower production costs and stronger export competitiveness. This is not about being the absolute best or most efficient producer in every use of that factor. It is about opportunity cost: a country with abundant labor can produce labor-intensive goods at lower opportunity cost, so it specializes in and exports those goods, even if another country is technically more efficient overall. Adam Smith's absolute advantage is about output per input; Heckscher-Ohlin is about factor-based comparative advantage.

Example: In a simplified two-country, two-product model, suppose one country has abundant labor and the other has abundant capital. The labor-abundant country will export the labor-intensive good, and the capital-abundant country will export the capital-intensive good, even if the labor-abundant country could also produce the capital-intensive good with slightly fewer resources than its partner. The reason is that the opportunity cost of shifting resources is lower for the country with the abundant factor.

err-later-single-cause

The student treats product life cycle theory and new trade theory as competing explanations and assumes modern trade has a single cause, when each theory explains different aspects of trade and they are complementary.

Explain: Vernon's product life cycle theory explains trade patterns by following products from newness to maturity: as a product matures, production tends to move to locations that can produce it most efficiently. New trade theory, developed in the 1970s, focuses on economies of scale, which can create first-mover advantages and trade even between countries with similar factor endowments. The two theories are not mutually exclusive. Product life cycle helps explain where production is located at different maturity stages; new trade theory helps explain why early movers in emerging industries dominate and why scale matters. In practice, businesses use both: they disperse production to efficient countries as products mature and invest in first-mover advantages in emerging industries. Therefore, modern trade patterns cannot be reduced to a single cause.

Example: A product may first be produced in an innovative country, then shift to a lower-cost country as it matures (product life cycle). Meanwhile, in a new industry, a firm that achieves large scale early gains a lasting cost advantage (new trade theory). Both processes can be observed in the same market, so the two theories complement each other.
