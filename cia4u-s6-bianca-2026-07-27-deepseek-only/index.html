Misconception: Thinking CBA is only about direct financial costs and revenues.

Remediation → err-cba-financial-only | Retest → err-cba-financial-only

  1. A. They correctly limited the analysis to financial impacts because parks must pay for themselves.

  2. ✓ B. They ignored non-market social and environmental benefits, such as improved community well-being and air quality.

Q3. Why do economists discount future costs and benefits to present value in Cost-Benefit Analysis?

errorId: err-cba-discounting-misconception | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Assess some responses, or possible responses, to current economic issues, using

Official: CC2.4 | Node: ? | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Thinking discounting is only about inflation or risk, or that it's arbitrary.

Remediation → err-cba-discounting-misconception | Retest → err-cba-discounting-misconception

  1. A. To account for inflation, which always reduces the real value of money over time.
    Inflation is one factor, but the main reason is that people prefer to receive benefits sooner rather than later (time preference). Discounting captures this even if there is no inflation.
  2. B. Because future benefits are inherently less certain and should be weighted less.
    Uncertainty is addressed through sensitivity analysis, not discounting. Discounting primarily reflects time preference.
  3. ✓ C. To reflect the social time preference: people generally value present consumption over future consumption.
    Exactly. Discounting ensures that benefits received sooner are valued more highly than those received later.
  4. D. To comply with government regulations that require a fixed discount rate of 5%.
    The discount rate is a chosen parameter reflecting time preferences and opportunity costs, not a fixed legal requirement.

    补救模块

    err-cba-financial-only

    CBA only considers financial costs and benefits; non-market impacts (social, environmental) are excluded.

    Explain: Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) is a public policy assessment method that quantifies all policy impacts in monetary terms, including non-market impacts such as social and environmental effects. Net social benefits equal total social benefits minus social costs. Limiting CBA to financial impacts ignores important non-market benefits like improved community well-being or environmental quality. A full CBA monetizes all significant impacts based on willingness to pay, ensuring a comprehensive comparison of alternatives.

    Example: In the urban traffic light installation case, economists compared net benefits of different schemes by including reduced accident costs (non-market) and travel time savings (non-market), not just installation and maintenance costs. Similarly, a city council evaluating a new park should include social and environmental benefits like improved air quality and recreation value, not just construction costs and ticket revenue.

    err-cba-exante-expost

    You confused Ex Ante CBA (conducted before implementation) with Ex Post CBA (conducted after completion). They serve different purposes: Ex Ante predicts outcomes to decide whether to proceed, while Ex Post evaluates actual outcomes to assess effectiveness.

    Explain: Cost-Benefit Analysis has four types based on timing. Ex Ante CBA is done before a policy is implemented to predict its net benefits and guide decision-making. Ex Post CBA is done after the policy is completed to evaluate actual outcomes against predictions. Using the wrong type can lead to inappropriate conclusions.

    Example: Imagine a city planning to install traffic lights. An Ex Ante CBA would estimate future benefits (e.g., reduced accidents) and costs (installation) before deciding. After installation, an Ex Post CBA would collect real data to see if the estimated benefits actually occurred.

    err-cba-discounting-misconception

    The student thinks discounting is arbitrary or only about inflation, not understanding that it reflects social time preference—people value present benefits more than future benefits.

    Explain: Discounting converts future costs and benefits into present value terms so that different options can be compared on a common basis. The fundamental reason is time preference: people generally prefer to receive benefits sooner rather than later, and to defer costs if possible. Even without inflation or risk, a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future because today's dollar can be invested or consumed now. In CBA, a social discount rate is chosen to reflect society's preference for present over future consumption.

    Example: Consider an urban traffic light installation. Without discounting, total future benefits over 10 years might appear large, but after applying a discount rate, the present value of those benefits is reduced, reflecting that benefits received sooner are more valuable. This ensures a fair comparison with upfront costs.

    err-cba-objectivity

    Students may think that because CBA uses numbers and formulas, it is completely objective. In reality, CBA involves many subjective choices such as how to monetize non-market impacts (e.g., value of human life), which discount rate to use, and whose perspective (Guardian vs Spender) to adopt. These choices can significantly affect the outcome.

    Explain: Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) is not purely objective. Subtle choices must be made in three areas: (1) Monetization: assigning a dollar value to non-market goods like clean air or human life involves subjective willingness-to-pay estimates. (2) Discount rate: the choice of social discount rate reflects value judgments about time preference; the Guardian perspective uses a higher rate to prioritize fiscal caution, while the Spender perspective uses a lower rate to emphasize long-term benefits. (3) Stakeholder bias: the perspective of the analyst (Guardian vs Spender) influences which costs and benefits are counted. These subjective elements mean CBA results can vary depending on the values chosen.

    Example: Consider a city deciding whether to install a traffic light. The analyst must place a dollar value on the human lives saved by reduced accidents. This valuation is inherently subjective—different people might assign different amounts. Also, if the Guardian perspective is used, a high discount rate might make future benefits seem smaller, possibly causing the project to be rejected; while a Spender perspective might favor it. The same CBA can yield different conclusions based on these subjective choices.
