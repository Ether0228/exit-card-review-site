      补救模块

      err-inference-only-text-clues

      Students believe that inference requires only explicit clues from the text and that personal background knowledge (schema) is unnecessary.

      Explain: Inference is not just finding clues in the text. It requires combining those clues with your own background knowledge (schema) to reach a logical conclusion. The formula is: Explicit Clues + Schema = Inference. Without schema, you cannot fully understand deeper meanings.

      Example: Consider a photo of a person with a scar holding a flower. The scar is a clue; the flower is a clue. But to infer that the person has experienced hardship yet retains gentleness, you use schema about scars (past injury) and flowers (delicate, beauty). Without schema, you only see a mark and a plant.

      err-inference-equals-guessing

      Students believe inference is any idea that pops into their head, without needing support from explicit clues in the text or visual.

      Explain: Inference is not guessing randomly. It is a logical conclusion based on explicit, verifiable clues from the text or image combined with your own background knowledge (schema). For example, from a photo of a person with a scar holding a flower, you can infer a contrast between hardship and gentleness (scar = past pain, flower = fragile beauty). You cannot infer he is a soldier because no military clues appear. Always ask: 'What specific clues support my idea?'

      Example: Lesson example: The scarred person holding a flower. A wild guess: 'He is sad.' But that is not supported. A logical inference: 'He has experienced something painful (scar) but still appreciates beauty (flower).' This uses the visible clues plus common schema about symbols.

      err-literal-reading-sufficient

      Students believe surface-level literal reading is enough for advanced courses.

      Explain: University-level reading requires going beyond the literal meaning of the text. You must read between the lines to infer the author's intent, bias, or deeper themes. The inference formula is: explicit clues from the text + your background knowledge (schema) = a logical conclusion.

      Example: In the Nicki Minaj profile, literal facts (e.g., her Trinidadian background, her lyrics) don't directly reveal her attitude. You must infer her confidence or criticism from her tone and word choices. For the childhood runaway narrative, the events themselves (leaving home) don't tell you the author's feelings; you infer fear, hope, or regret from descriptive details.

      err-inference-writing-tell-only

      Students do not apply inference in writing, relying on direct statements instead of 'showing' through descriptive details.

      Explain: Good writing uses inferential logic by 'showing' rather than 'telling'. Instead of directly stating an emotion or idea, use descriptive details (sensory images, actions, figurative language) that let the reader infer the meaning. For example, instead of 'She was sad,' you could write 'A single tear traced a path down her cheek.' The reader infers sadness without being told.

      Example: From the lesson: In the childhood runaway narrative, the author didn't say 'I was scared.' Instead, they described 'the pounding of my heart, the quick glances over my shoulder, the way the shadows seemed to reach for me.' These details allow readers to infer the fear.
