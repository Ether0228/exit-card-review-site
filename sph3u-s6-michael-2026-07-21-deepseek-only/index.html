Q1. A box of mass m sits on a frictionless incline of angle θ. What is the normal force acting on the box?

errorId: err-normal-force-incline | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student thinks normal force always equals weight regardless of incline.

Remediation → err-normal-force-incline | Retest → err-normal-force-incline

  1. A. mg
    That would be true only on a horizontal surface. On an incline, part of the weight is parallel to the surface, so the normal force is less than mg.
  2. ✓ B. mg cosθ
    Correct. The normal force is the component of weight perpendicular to the incline: N = mg cosθ.
  3. C. mg sinθ
    That is the component of weight parallel to the incline, which causes acceleration down the ramp, not the normal force.
  4. D. 0
    The normal force is zero only if the object is in free fall or not in contact with the surface. Here, the box is in contact, so N ≠ 0.

Q2. A crate is being pushed to the right across a rough floor. The kinetic friction force on the crate acts:

errorId: err-friction-direction | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Student may think friction acts in the direction of motion or perpendicular to it.

Remediation → err-friction-direction | Retest → err-friction-direction

  1. A. to the right
    Kinetic friction always opposes the motion, so it cannot be in the same direction as the push.
  2. ✓ B. to the left
    Correct. Kinetic friction opposes the motion, so it acts to the left, opposite the push.
  3. C. upward
    Upward would be normal force, not friction. Friction is parallel to the surface.
  4. D. downward
    Downward is not a horizontal direction. Friction acts parallel to the contact surface.

Q3. A heavy truck and a light bicycle are both moving at the same speed. Which has more inertia?

Official: C3 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-4

Misconception: Student thinks faster objects have more inertia.

Remediation → err-inertia-speed | Retest → err-inertia-speed

  1. ✓ A. the truck
    Correct. Inertia depends only on mass, so the heavier truck has more inertia.
  2. B. the bicycle
    The bicycle has less mass, so less inertia. Speed does not affect inertia.
  3. C. same because same speed
    Inertia is not related to speed; it depends solely on mass.
  4. D. cannot tell without more information
    Enough information is given: mass determines inertia. The truck has more mass, so more inertia.

Q4. A net force of 10 N is applied to a 2 kg object in the positive x-direction. Which statement about the acceleration is correct?

errorId: err-second-law-scalar | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-4

Misconception: Student ignores direction or uses magnitude only.

Remediation → err-second-law-scalar | Retest → err-second-law-scalar

  1. ✓ A. 5 m/s² in the positive x-direction
    Correct. Using F_net = m*a gives a = F_net / m = 10 N / 2 kg = 5 m/s², and direction same as force.
  2. B. 5 m/s² in the negative x-direction
    The acceleration is in the same direction as the net force (positive x), not opposite.
  3. C. 20 m/s² in any direction
    The magnitude is 5 m/s², not 20. Also direction is determined by the force.
  4. D. acceleration depends on speed
    Newton's second law gives a = F_net / m, independent of current speed.

Q5. A book rests on a table. Which pair of forces is an action-reaction pair according to Newton's third law?

errorId: err-third-law-same-object | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of the relationship between changes in velocity and

Official: C3 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-4

Misconception: Student thinks weight and normal force are action-reaction since they are equal and opposite but on same object.

Remediation → err-third-law-same-object | Retest → err-third-law-same-object

  1. A. Earth's pull on the book (weight) and the table's upward force on the book (normal force)
    These forces act on the same object (the book) and therefore are not an action-reaction pair. They are equal and opposite but not third-law partners.
  2. B. The book's weight and the table's normal force on the book
    Same as A; both act on the book, so they cannot be action-reaction.
  3. C. Earth's pull on the book and the book's pull on Earth
    This is close but not the pair directly asked; Earth pulls on book (gravity) and book pulls on Earth (gravity), but the question is about contact and normal force. However, the most common correct pair in this scenario is the force of the book on the table and the force of the table on the book. Option C is a valid action-reaction pair but not the one involving the table. The question targets contact forces.
  4. ✓ D. The book's downward force on the table (action) and the table's upward force on the book (reaction)
    Correct. The book pushes down on the table, and the table pushes up on the book. These forces act on different objects and are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

补救模块

err-normal-force-incline

The student believes that the normal force on an inclined surface is always equal to the full weight (mg) of the object, ignoring that only the perpendicular component contributes.

Explain: Normal force is the force exerted by a surface perpendicular to the surface. On a flat horizontal surface, it equals mg. On an incline, only the component of weight perpendicular to the surface contributes: N = mg cosθ. The parallel component mg sinθ causes acceleration down the incline.

Example: For a 10 kg box on a 30° incline: N = 10*9.8*cos30° ≈ 84.9 N, not 98 N. The weight is 98 N, but the normal force is less because part of the weight pulls parallel to the incline.

err-friction-direction

Student may think kinetic friction can act in the same direction as motion or perpendicular to the surface, rather than always opposing the direction of motion (or impending motion) relative to the surface.

Explain: Kinetic friction always acts opposite to the direction in which an object slides relative to the surface. For example, if a crate is pushed to the right, the kinetic friction force acts to the left, opposing the motion. This is true regardless of the object's speed or other forces. The direction of kinetic friction is independent of the applied force; it always opposes the relative motion.

Example: Consider a curling stone sliding on ice. As it moves forward, kinetic friction from the ice acts backward, slowing it down. If you push a box across a rough floor, the friction force points opposite to the push, trying to stop the box.

err-inertia-speed

Students may believe inertia depends on speed rather than mass, e.g., thinking a fast bicycle has more inertia than a slow truck.

Explain: Newton's First Law states that an object at rest or moving at constant velocity remains so unless acted upon by a nonzero net external force. This property is called inertia. Inertia is the resistance to change in motion, and it depends only on the object's mass. A heavier object has more inertia regardless of its speed. For example, a slow-moving truck has more inertia than a fast-moving bicycle because it has more mass.

Example: During car braking, passengers lurch forward because their bodies resist the change in motion (inertia). A heavier passenger lurches with more force than a lighter one, even if they were traveling at the same speed. This shows inertia depends on mass, not speed.

err-second-law-scalar

Student treats Newton's Second Law as scalar, ignoring the vector nature of force and acceleration.

Explain: Newton's Second Law is a vector equation: F_net = m*a, meaning acceleration is directly proportional to net force and in the exact same direction. Both force and acceleration have magnitude and direction; the direction of acceleration always matches the direction of the net force.

Example: A 3.2×10³ kg barge pulled by two tugboats experiences a net force of 2848 N at 6.7° north of east. Its acceleration is 0.89 m/s² also at 6.7° north of east (F_net = m*a).

err-third-law-same-object

Students often think that action and reaction forces act on the same object and cancel each other. In reality, they act on two different objects and cannot cancel because they are applied to different bodies.

Explain: Newton's Third Law states: For every action force, there is an equal and opposite reaction force acting on the two interacting objects. These forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but they act on different objects. Therefore, they do not cancel each other out when considering the motion of a single object. A common mistake is to think that the weight of an object and the normal force from a table are an action-reaction pair; they are not, because both act on the same object. The correct action-reaction pair is the book pushing down on the table (action) and the table pushing up on the book (reaction).

Example: When you walk, your foot pushes backward against the ground (action). The ground pushes forward on your foot (reaction). These forces act on different objects: the ground and your foot. The forward force on your foot propels you forward. If they acted on the same object, you would not move.
