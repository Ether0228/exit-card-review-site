Q1. Which of the following is an example of a positive externality?

Official: B3 | Node: CIA4U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students may think any personal benefit is a positive externality, or they may confuse positive with negative.

Remediation → err-externality-direction | Retest → err-externality-direction

  1. A. A factory emitting pollution affecting nearby residents
    That is a negative externality because it imposes a cost on third parties.
  2. ✓ B. A person getting a flu vaccination, which reduces spread of disease to others
    Correct! The vaccination benefits not only the individual but also others who are less likely to catch the flu.
  3. C. A company laying off workers due to decreased demand
    This is a private cost to workers, not an externality, as it directly involves the parties in the transaction.
  4. D. A smoker's second-hand smoke affecting bystanders
    That is a negative externality because it harms third parties.

补救模块

err-externality-direction

Student may think that any personal cost is a negative externality or that any personal benefit is a positive externality, without considering third-party effects.

Explain: Externalities are costs or benefits of economic transactions that affect third parties not directly involved. Positive externalities provide benefits to others (e.g., vaccinations reduce disease spread). Negative externalities impose costs on others (e.g., pollution from factories harms nearby residents).

Example: Vaccination: The person getting vaccinated is protected, but others also benefit from reduced transmission – a positive externality. Cigarette smoking: The smoker chooses to smoke, but second-hand smoke harms bystanders – a negative externality.

err-unemployment-type-confusion

Student cannot distinguish between frictional, structural, cyclical, and seasonal unemployment.

Explain: Unemployment is classified into four types. Cyclical occurs due to a recession (drop in overall demand). Seasonal occurs when demand shifts predictably with seasons (e.g., tourism). Structural happens when workers' skills no longer match available jobs (e.g., industry decline). Frictional is temporary while workers search for new jobs (e.g., recent graduates).

Example: A ski instructor laid off in summer is seasonal unemployment. A coal miner losing work permanently due to renewable energy is structural. A recession causing factory closures is cyclical. A person quitting to find a better job is frictional.

err-capitalism-socialism-mix

Student thinks capitalism and socialism are mutually exclusive or that mixed economy has no defining features.

Explain: Capitalism and socialism are not mutually exclusive; they are ideal types at opposite ends of a spectrum. Most real-world economies are mixed, combining private ownership and profit motive (capitalism) with public ownership and government intervention (socialism) to varying degrees. A mixed economy has defining features: government regulates businesses, provides welfare, and invests in infrastructure while allowing market competition.

Example: Capitalism: private ownership, profit motive, minimal government (e.g., US in some sectors). Socialism: public ownership, central planning (e.g., Cuba). Mixed: government regulates industry and provides public services, but private firms operate for profit (e.g., Canada, UK).

err-government-measures-incomplete

Student may conflate monetary policy (e.g., lowering interest rates) with the six fiscal/regulatory measures, or may only recall 3-4 measures.

Explain: Governments promote economic growth through six specific measures: (1) achieving full employment, (2) supervising financial markets, (3) regulating industry, (4) protecting copyrights and contracts, (5) investing in public infrastructure, and (6) building welfare systems. These are distinct from monetary policy tools like changing interest rates. Note that 'achieving full employment' is a goal, while the other five are policy actions. But all six are listed as measures.

Example: For instance, the Canadian government's investment in public transit (infrastructure) and its oversight of banks (supervising financial markets) are examples of these measures. In contrast, the Bank of Canada's decision to lower interest rates is a monetary policy tool, not one of these six.
