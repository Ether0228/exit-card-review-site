补救模块

err-ext-positive-negative-confusion

The student incorrectly categorizes externalities, e.g., believing pollution is a positive externality or vaccination is a negative externality, failing to recognize that negative externalities impose costs on third parties while positive externalities provide benefits.

Explain: Externalities are unintended spillover effects on unrelated third parties. A negative externality harms third parties without compensation (e.g., factory air pollution causing respiratory illness). A positive externality benefits third parties without payment (e.g., herd immunity from vaccination reduces disease spread for everyone). The key is whether the effect imposes a cost (negative) or provides a benefit (positive) to others.

Example: Factory smoke that causes health problems is a negative externality because it imposes costs on nearby residents. In contrast, a person getting a flu shot creates a positive externality by reducing transmission to others (herd immunity).

err-ext-intentionality

Student may believe externalities are deliberate consequences rather than unintended side effects of production or consumption.

Explain: Externalities are unplanned cost or benefit spillovers from production or consumption to unrelated third parties; they are unintended, uncompensated side effects. They are not planned outcomes—they happen as a byproduct of economic activity. For example, a factory producing goods may unintentionally pollute the air, affecting nearby residents without compensation.

Example: Factory air pollution is a negative externality because the factory does not intend to harm residents; the pollution is an unintended side effect of production.

err-ext-market-failure-misconception

Market failure only happens when the government makes bad decisions; it has nothing to do with the market not accounting for social costs or benefits of externalities.

Explain: Market failure occurs when a free, unregulated market does not allocate resources efficiently. For externalities, this happens because market prices only reflect private costs and private benefits, ignoring the costs or benefits that spill over to third parties. When a negative externality exists (e.g., pollution), the social cost is higher than the private cost, so the market overproduces the good. When a positive externality exists (e.g., vaccination), the social benefit is higher than the private benefit, so the market underproduces the good. This is not due to government failure but due to the market's inability to price externalities.

Example: Consider a factory that emits smoke, causing respiratory illness in nearby residents. The factory only pays for its private costs (materials, labour, energy). It does not pay for the health damage imposed on residents. As a result, the market price of the factory's product is too low, encouraging overproduction. The result is more pollution than is socially optimal. This is a market failure caused by a negative externality, not by a government mistake.

err-ext-tradable-permits-vs-tax

Student may confuse tradable emission permits with a carbon tax, not understanding cap-and-trade mechanism.

Explain: Tradable emission permits (cap-and-trade) and carbon tax are both government tools to reduce negative externalities like pollution, but they work differently. A carbon tax sets a fixed price per unit of pollution, so firms decide how much to reduce and total emissions are uncertain. Tradable permits set a fixed total cap on emissions, and firms trade permits; the price is market-determined and the total reduction is certain.

Example: Example: For carbon tax, the government charges firms $30 per ton of CO2 emitted. For tradable permits, the government sets a total cap of 1000 tons, issues permits, and firms trade them.
