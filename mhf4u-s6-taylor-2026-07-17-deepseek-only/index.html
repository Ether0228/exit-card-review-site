补救模块

err-polynomial-degree-leading-coefficient

Confuses degree (highest exponent) with leading coefficient (coefficient of that term) or misinterprets the sign of the leading coefficient.

Explain: The degree of a polynomial is the highest exponent of the variable. The leading coefficient is the number in front of that term, including its sign. For example, in f(x) = -2x^3 + 5x^2 - x + 4, the term with the highest exponent is -2x^3, so the degree is 3 and the leading coefficient is -2 (negative).

Example: Find the degree and leading coefficient of f(x) = 4x^5 - 3x^2 + 7. The highest exponent is 5, so degree = 5. The coefficient of x^5 is 4, so leading coefficient = 4 (positive).

err-root-order-behavior

Student thinks that every root causes the graph to cross the x-axis, or that multiplicity 2 still results in crossing; they do not recognize that even multiplicities cause the graph to touch and turn.

Explain: The order (multiplicity) of a root determines how the graph behaves near the x-axis at that point. For a root of order 1 (odd), the graph crosses the x-axis. For a root of order 2 (even), the graph touches the x-axis and turns back (does not cross). For a root of order 3 (odd >1), the graph crosses with a point of inflection (flattening at the crossing).

Example: Consider f(x) = (x-1)^1, g(x) = (x-1)^2, h(x) = (x-1)^3. At x=1, f crosses straight through, g touches and turns, h crosses with a flattening (inflection).

err-end-behavior-direction

The student confuses the effect of degree parity (even vs odd) and leading coefficient sign on the end behavior, often mixing up which ends go up or down.

Explain: The end behavior of a polynomial is determined solely by its leading term (the term with the highest exponent). For a polynomial with degree n and leading coefficient a: If n is even, both ends go the same direction: both up if a > 0, both down if a < 0. If n is odd, ends go opposite directions: left down & right up if a > 0, left up & right down if a < 0.

Example: For f(x) = -2x^3 + 5x^2 - x + 4: degree n=3 (odd), leading coefficient a=-2 (negative). So as x→-∞, f(x)→+∞ (left up); as x→+∞, f(x)→-∞ (right down).

err-finite-differences-degree

Students think that if the first differences are constant, the degree is 1; if second differences are constant, the degree is 2, etc. They may also confuse degree with the number of differences computed.

Explain: Finite differences help find the degree of a polynomial from a table of equally spaced x-values. Compute successive differences (Δy, Δ²y, Δ³y, ...). The smallest order where differences are constant equals the degree. For example, constant first differences → degree 1; constant second differences → degree 2; constant third differences → degree 3.

Example: Given x: 0,1,2,3,4 and y: 1,2,5,10,17. First differences: 1,3,5,7 (not constant). Second differences: 2,2,2 (constant). So polynomial degree is 2.

err-polynomial-equation-from-graph

The student knows that zeros correspond to factors, but does not know how to determine the leading coefficient from the y-intercept, or forgets to account for multiplicity.

Explain: To write the equation of a polynomial from its graph, identify the x-intercepts (zeros) and their multiplicities. Write factors in the form (x - r)^m where r is the zero and m is the multiplicity. Then use the y-intercept (0, y) to solve for the leading coefficient a. The equation is f(x) = a(x - r1)^m1 (x - r2)^m2 ... .

Example: Graph shows zeros at x = -1 (touching, multiplicity 2) and x = 2 (crossing, multiplicity 1), and y-intercept at (0, 4). Factors: (x+1)^2 and (x-2). So f(x) = a(x+1)^2(x-2). Plug y-intercept: 4 = a(1)^2(-2) => -2a = 4 => a = -2. Equation: f(x) = -2(x+1)^2(x-2).
