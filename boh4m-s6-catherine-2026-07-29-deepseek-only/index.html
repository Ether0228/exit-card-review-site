Q1. Two people look at the same duck-rabbit illusion image. One sees a duck, the other sees a rabbit. Which statement best explains this difference?

errorId: err-perception-objectivity | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Apply an understanding of human behaviour to explain how individuals and groups

Official: B1 | Node: BOH4M-B-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/business1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students may think perception is objective and identical for everyone, not recognizing that different people interpret same stimuli differently.

Remediation → err-perception-objectivity | Retest → err-perception-objectivity

  1. A. One person has better eyesight.
    The duck-rabbit illusion is about interpretation, not eyesight.
  2. ✓ B. Perception is subjective and can vary.
    Exactly. The same image can be perceived differently because perception involves interpretation.
  3. C. The image is ambiguous.
    While it is ambiguous, the key point is that different individuals perceive it differently, which is about perception.
  4. D. One person is lying.
    It is unlikely that either is lying; both are describing what they see.

Remediation → err-belbin-role-fixed | Retest → err-belbin-role-fixed

  1. A. Three shapers
    A team with three shapers might be too competitive; balance across categories is better.

    A team needs action and people roles too; all three categories are important.

    补救模块

    err-perception-objectivity

    Students may think perception is objective and identical for everyone, not recognizing that different people interpret same stimuli differently.

    Explain: Perception is not a passive recording; it's an active process where your brain organizes and interprets sensory information based on your experiences, expectations, and culture. The duck-rabbit illusion shows that two people can look at the same image and genuinely see different things. Both interpretations are valid. This subjectivity is crucial in business: a manager's perception of an employee's behaviour may differ from the employee's intention, leading to misunderstandings. Effective communication and perspective-taking can reduce these gaps.

    Example: Consider the duck-rabbit illusion: one person sees a duck, another sees a rabbit. Neither is wrong. Similarly, in a business meeting, one manager may perceive a colleague's question as critical, while another sees it as curious. This demonstrates that perception is subjective.

    err-media-bias-all-news-unbiased

    Many students assume that news outlets report facts objectively without any bias, not recognizing that media often carry implicit political or commercial biases that influence language, framing, and selection of facts.

    Explain: Bias is an inappropriate or incorrect pre-held perspective. Media can spread bias through emotional language and selective framing. For example, in the 1983 Soviet-Korean jet incident, the New York Times used the phrase 'murder in the air' to frame the Soviet Union negatively. In contrast, when the US shot down an Iranian jet in 1988, the same publication described it as an 'accidental event.' This shows political bias: the language used can shape your perception of events. Most public media (official outlets, social platforms) carry implicit biases; therefore, you should critically evaluate information before accepting it.

    Example: The New York Times' different framing of two similar incidents: 1983 Soviet-Korean jet = 'murder in the air' (negative language for USSR), 1988 US-Iranian jet = 'accidental event' (neutral language for US). This demonstrates how media can influence public opinion through word choice.

    err-belbin-role-fixed

    Students may think each person has only one Belbin role and cannot adapt, or that blocking roles are acceptable.

    Explain: Belbin team roles are not fixed labels; individuals can exhibit multiple roles and adapt their behavior to team needs. The model categorizes roles into three areas (action, thinking, people) for balanced teams. Blocking roles like free riders or distractors are harmful and should be avoided.

    Example: In a 3-person team, you might assign one action-oriented role (e.g., shaper), one thinking-oriented role (e.g., plant), and one people-oriented role (e.g., coordinator). However, team members can flex into other roles as needed. Blocking roles such as free riders undermine team efficiency.

    err-communication-face-to-face-always

    Students may think face-to-face communication is always the best method, ignoring practical barriers like distance, time differences, or large groups, which make written communication necessary. They may not realize that written communication, when concise and jargon-free, can be effective when face-to-face is impossible.

    Explain: Face-to-face communication is the most preferred workplace method because it minimizes misunderstandings through real-time feedback, gestures, and facial expressions. However, it is not always possible due to distance, time differences, or large groups. In those cases, written communication should be used. Written communication must be concise and jargon-free to be effective. For example, if team members are in different time zones, an email or instant message is more practical than trying to schedule a face-to-face meeting. Knowing when to choose each method is a key skill.

    Example: Your team has members in Toronto, London, and Tokyo. Scheduling a face-to-face video call at a time that works for everyone is difficult because of time zone differences. Instead, you write a clear, concise email summarizing the project updates and action items. This ensures everyone gets the information without the need for all to be awake at the same time.

    err-listening-passive

    Students may equate hearing with listening, not understanding that effective listening requires active focus, targeted questions, and an open mind.

    Explain: Effective listening is not just hearing words; it requires active focus, asking targeted questions, and keeping an open mind. Passive listening—being physically present but mentally distracted—leads to misunderstanding and missed information. To listen effectively, maintain eye contact, nod appropriately, ask clarifying questions, and avoid jumping to conclusions or letting biases interfere.

    Example: A manager listens to a team member's report. Passive: the manager nods but is checking emails, then misinterprets the deadline. Active: the manager asks clarifying questions like 'Can you explain the timeline again?' and summarizes key points to confirm understanding.
