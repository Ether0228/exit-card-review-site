Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Sign errors in factors (e.g., using +2 and +3 instead of -2 and -3).

Remediation → err-quadratic-form-conversion | Retest → err-quadratic-form-conversion

  1. ✓ A. f(x)=(x-2)(x-3)
  2. B. f(x)=(x+2)(x+3)
    Check the signs: the constant term is +6, so the factors should multiply to +6 and sum to -5, meaning both factors are negative.
  3. C. f(x)=(x-1)(x+6)
    This expands to x²+5x-6, not x²-5x+6.
  4. D. f(x)=(x-6)(x+1)
    This expands to x²-5x-6, not x²-5x+6.

补救模块

err-quadratic-form-conversion

Confusing the conversion between standard and factored forms of a quadratic function.

Explain: To convert a quadratic from standard form (ax^2+bx+c) to factored form (a(x-r1)(x-r2)), find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b when a=1. Write as (x + p)(x + q) with correct signs. For a≠1, find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b, then factor by grouping.

Example: Convert f(x)=x²-5x+6 to factored form. Find numbers that multiply to 6 and add to -5: -2 and -3. Thus f(x)=(x-2)(x-3).

err-reciprocal-zeros-to-asymptotes

The student believes that the zeros of the quadratic become zeros of its reciprocal, rather than vertical asymptotes.

Explain: When you take the reciprocal of a quadratic function, the zeros (x-intercepts) of the quadratic cause the reciprocal to become undefined because division by zero is not allowed. Instead of having a value at those points, the reciprocal function has vertical asymptotes, which are lines that the graph approaches but never touches. The reciprocal function never has x-intercepts because a fraction can only be zero if its numerator is zero, and here the numerator is 1.

Example: Consider f(x)=x^2-4. Its zeros are x=2 and x=-2. The reciprocal g(x)=1/(x^2-4) has vertical asymptotes at x=2 and x=-2. Graphically, as x approaches 2 from the left, g(x) goes to negative infinity; from the right, to positive infinity. The reciprocal has no x-intercepts because 1 over something cannot be zero.

err-reciprocal-x-intercept

Students think that the reciprocal function g(x)=1/(ax^2+bx+c) has x-intercepts, similar to the original quadratic. They may incorrectly set the reciprocal equal to zero and solve, not realizing that a fraction equals zero only when the numerator is zero.

Explain: The reciprocal of a quadratic function, g(x)=1/f(x), has no x-intercepts because the numerator is constant (1). For a rational function to have an x-intercept, the numerator must be zero. Since 1 is never zero, the graph never crosses the x-axis. Vertical asymptotes occur where the denominator is zero, which are the x-intercepts of the original quadratic.

Example: Consider f(x)=x^2-4. Its reciprocal is g(x)=1/(x^2-4). The x-intercepts of f are at x=2 and x=-2. For g, setting numerator=1 to zero gives no solution, so no x-intercepts. At x=2 and x=-2, g has vertical asymptotes.

err-horizontal-asymptote

Believing the horizontal asymptote of a reciprocal quadratic is something other than y=0.

Explain: For a reciprocal quadratic function f(x)=1/(ax²+bx+c), the degree of the numerator (0) is less than the degree of the denominator (2). When the degree of the denominator is greater than the numerator, the horizontal asymptote is always y=0. As x → ±∞, the denominator grows without bound, so the fraction approaches 0.

Example: Consider g(x)=1/(x²-4). As x → ±∞, x²-4 → ∞, so g(x) → 0. The graph approaches the line y=0 but never crosses it.

err-sign-consistency

Thinking the original quadratic and its reciprocal can have different signs (positive/negative) in the same interval.

Explain: The original quadratic function and its reciprocal function always have the same sign in any interval where the quadratic is not zero. This is because the reciprocal of a positive number is positive, and the reciprocal of a negative number is negative.

Example: Consider a quadratic function that is positive on an interval. Its reciprocal, 1 divided by a positive number, is also positive. Similarly, if the quadratic is negative, the reciprocal is negative.

err-extrema-correspondence

Confusing the correspondence between local maxima/minima of the quadratic and the reciprocal (e.g., thinking both are maxima).

Explain: The local maximum or minimum (vertex) of a quadratic function corresponds to the opposite type of extremum in its reciprocal function. If the quadratic has a local minimum at a point, the reciprocal has a local maximum at that same x-coordinate, and vice versa. This happens because the reciprocal function 'flips' the shape: a valley becomes a peak and a peak becomes a valley.

Example: Consider f(x)=x²-4x+3. This quadratic opens upward, so its vertex at x=2 is a minimum. The reciprocal g(x)=1/(x²-4x+3) will have a maximum at x=2. Indeed, g(2)=1/(-1)=-1, and nearby values are less than -1 (more negative) so it is a local maximum.
