补救模块

err-csr-confuse-accommodating-proactive

Students often think that any positive social action is Proactive, but they do not recognize whether the action was triggered by external pressure (Accommodating) or initiated voluntarily (Proactive). They may also overlook the reactive nature of Accommodating strategies.

Explain: The key difference between Accommodating and Proactive CSR strategies lies in motivation and timing. Accommodating companies respond to stakeholder pressure or criticism; they meet ethical obligations but do not go beyond what is demanded. Proactive companies actively seek opportunities to contribute to public good without waiting for pressure; they integrate social responsibility into their core strategy. Remember: Accommodating = reactive, responds to pressure; Proactive = proactive, initiates action.

Example: McDonald's building Ronald McDonald Houses for families of sick children is Proactive because it was initiated voluntarily to support communities. In contrast, a company that matches employee donations only after employees repeatedly request it is Accommodating because it responds to pressure.

err-email-gendered-salutation

Students believe that terms like 'Sir' or 'Madam' are universally appropriate for addressing unknown recipients in professional emails, not realizing that these terms assume a specific gender and may misgender the recipient, which is unprofessional and can cause offense.

Explain: In professional emails, avoid gendered salutations like 'Dear Sir' or 'Dear Madam' when the recipient's name or gender is unknown. These terms assume the recipient's gender, which may be incorrect or disrespectful. Instead, use gender-neutral salutations such as 'To Whom It May Concern,' 'Dear Hiring Manager,' or 'Dear [Department Name] Team.' This ensures your communication is inclusive and professional.

Example: Instead of writing 'Dear Sir,' write 'To Whom It May Concern' or 'Dear Customer Service Team.'

err-ethical-dilemma-disclose-layoffs

Student believes directly disclosing layoff information is ethical, failing to recognize confidentiality obligations.

Explain: This is an ethical dilemma that requires balancing corporate confidentiality with personal responsibility to a colleague. Directly disclosing confidential information violates company policy and trust. The ethical solution is to protect confidentiality while still advising caution, e.g., suggesting postponing large financial decisions without giving specific reasons.

Example: Your organization plans layoffs next month. A colleague is about to sign a mortgage and asks if the company is financially stable. The ethical response is to advise them to postpone large financial decisions without mentioning the layoffs. This protects confidentiality and fulfills your responsibility to warn them.
