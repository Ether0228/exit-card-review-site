Q1. Which of the following is an example of a discrete random variable?

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Solve problems involving the probability of an event or a combination of events

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Thinking discrete means 'any integer' without the requirement of countability or that continuous variables cannot take integer values.

Remediation → err-continuous-vs-discrete | Retest → err-continuous-vs-discrete

  1. A. The height of a randomly selected student
    Height is measured on a continuous scale; it is a continuous random variable.
  2. ✓ B. The number of heads when flipping a coin 5 times
    Correct! The number of heads is a countable integer from 0 to 5, making it a discrete random variable.
  3. C. The time taken to run a 100m race
    Time is measured on a continuous scale; it is a continuous random variable.
  4. D. The temperature in Toronto on a given day
    Temperature is measured on a continuous scale; it is a continuous random variable.

Q2. In a discrete probability distribution, the sum of the probabilities of all possible outcomes must be:

errorId: err-prob-dist-sum-not-1 | Bloom: remember | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: remember | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of discrete probability distributions, represent th

Official: B1 | Node: MDM4U-B-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Thinking probabilities can add to less than 1 or more than 1, or confusing with other properties.

Remediation → err-prob-dist-sum-not-1 | Retest → err-prob-dist-sum-not-1

  1. A. Between 0 and 1 (but not necessarily 1)
    While each probability is between 0 and 1, their total must sum to exactly 1.
  2. ✓ B. Exactly 1
    Correct! The sum of all probabilities in a probability distribution must equal 1.
  3. C. Equal to the number of outcomes
    The sum of probabilities is always 1, regardless of the number of outcomes.
  4. D. Less than 1 if the distribution is discrete
    The total probability is always 1, whether the distribution is discrete or continuous.

Q3. When rolling two fair six-sided dice and recording the sum, how many distinct outcomes (ordered pairs) are in the sample space?

errorId: err-two-dice-sample-space | Bloom: remember | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: remember | OSSD: Solve problems involving the probability of an event or a combination of events

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Thinking the sample space has 21 outcomes because order doesn't matter, or thinking sums are equally likely.

Remediation → err-two-dice-sample-space | Retest → err-two-dice-sample-space

  1. A. 11
    11 is the number of possible sums (2 to 12), not the number of ordered outcomes.
  2. B. 21
    21 would be the number of unordered outcomes if dice were identical; but dice are distinct, giving 36 ordered pairs.
  3. ✓ C. 36
    Correct! 6 faces on first die x 6 faces on second die = 36 equally likely ordered pairs.
  4. D. 72
    Double counting; there are only 36 distinct pairs.

Learning Goal: lg-4

Misconception: Thinking that after a small number of trials the experimental probability will already be very close to theoretical, or that it never converges.

Remediation → err-law-of-large-numbers-speed | Retest → err-law-of-large-numbers-speed

  1. A. The experimental probability will stay at 0.4 because the initial pattern is set.
    Experimental probability fluctuates initially but stabilizes close to theoretical value with many trials.
  2. B. The experimental probability will become exactly 0.167 after exactly 1000 rolls.
    Experimental probability approaches theoretical but rarely matches exactly, even with many trials.
  3. ✓ C. The experimental probability will likely be closer to 0.167 than it was at 10 rolls.
    Correct! According to the law of large numbers, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability as the number of trials increases.
  4. D. The experimental probability will be exactly half of the theoretical probability.
    No guarantee; the law of large numbers says it approaches, not a specific fraction.

补救模块

err-prob-dist-sum-not-1

Students may think that probabilities can sum to a value other than 1, such as treating them as percentages that don't need to add to 100%, or confusing with other properties.

Explain: In any valid discrete probability distribution, the total probability of all possible outcomes must sum to 1 (or 100%). This is a fundamental rule because one of the outcomes must occur. For example, when rolling two dice, the probabilities for sums 2 through 12 are: P(2)=1/36, P(3)=2/36, ..., P(7)=6/36, ..., P(12)=1/36. If you add all these fractions, you get exactly 1. This verifies the distribution is complete and consistent.

Example: Consider the probability distribution for the sum of two dice. The possible sums are 2 to 12 with probabilities: 1/36, 2/36, 3/36, 4/36, 5/36, 6/36, 5/36, 4/36, 3/36, 2/36, 1/36. Sum = (1+2+3+4+5+6+5+4+3+2+1)/36 = 36/36 = 1. If the sum were less than 1, it would mean some outcomes are missing; if greater, the probabilities would be too large.

err-expected-value-guarantee

Student believes the expected value must be a possible outcome of an experiment or guarantees a result in any single trial.

Explain: Expected value is the long-run average of a random variable. It is calculated by summing each outcome multiplied by its probability. It does not have to be a possible outcome (e.g., 3.5 for a die) and does not guarantee what will happen in one trial. Over many trials, the average tends to be close to the expected value.

Example: Rolling a single die: expected value = 3.5, but you never actually roll 3.5. Rolling two dice: expected value = 7, but many rolls will not be exactly 7. After 1000 rolls, the average sum might be 6.98 or 7.05, not exactly 7.

err-continuous-vs-discrete

Student confuses discrete random variables (countable outcomes) with continuous (measurable range) – e.g., thinks a die roll can yield 3.5.

Explain: Discrete random variables have countable outcomes, typically integers. For example, the number of heads when flipping a coin 5 times can only be 0,1,2,3,4,5. Continuous random variables can take any value in a range, including decimals and fractions. Height, time, and temperature are continuous because they are measured, not counted.

Example: Rolling a die: the outcome is 1,2,3,4,5,6 – discrete. The height of a student can be 150.3 cm – continuous.

err-two-dice-sample-space

Student thinks the sample space for two dice has 21 outcomes (because they treat the dice as indistinguishable or ignore order) or believes sums like 2 and 7 are equally likely. They fail to recognize that each die is distinct, yielding 36 ordered pairs, and that sums have different probabilities based on the number of combinations.

Explain: When rolling two six-sided dice, each die is separate. The first die can show 1–6, and the second can also show 1–6. That gives 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely ordered pairs (e.g., (1,1), (1,2), ..., (6,6)). The sum of the dice is not equally likely: for example, sum 2 occurs only in (1,1), while sum 7 occurs in six pairs: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1). Always use ordered pairs to count correctly.

Example: List all pairs for sum 3: (1,2) and (2,1) → 2 out of 36, probability = 2/36 = 1/18. Never just count sums without considering the two dice separately.

err-law-of-large-numbers-speed

The student believes that experimental probability converges quickly to theoretical probability, even with a small number of trials. They may expect exact match after only 10 or 20 rolls.

Explain: The law of large numbers states that as the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability. However, with very few trials, variability is high and the experimental probability can be far off. It takes many trials (often in the hundreds or thousands) for the experimental probability to stabilize near the theoretical value.

Example: In the two-dice sum simulation, after 10 rolls you might get a sum of 7 five times (experimental probability 0.5), while theoretical is about 0.167. After 1000 rolls, the experimental probability typically falls within 0.2 of the theoretical value. For sum 7, that would be between about 0.147 and 0.187.

err-simulation-code-misunderstanding

The student believes they must write simulation code entirely from scratch, or they distrust AI-generated code and think they must avoid it, failing to recognize that the focus is on interpreting results.

Explain: In this course, simulations are used to explore probability distributions. The simulation code is often provided (e.g., AI-generated or in the lesson). Your main task is to run the code, interpret the output (experimental probabilities, histograms), and compare with theoretical values. You do not need to write the code from scratch; understanding the logic helps you interpret results.

Example: For the two-dice sum simulation, you are given Python code that randomly generates 1000 rolls, calculates sums, and plots a histogram. You run it and see that with many trials, experimental probabilities approach theoretical (e.g., sum 7 frequency ~ 1/6). The code is provided; you focus on analyzing the output.
