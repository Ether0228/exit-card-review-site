Q1. The Bank of Canada raises its policy interest rate to reduce inflation. According to the lesson, which short-run consequence is most likely?

errorId: err-monetary-goals-tradeoff | Bloom: analyze | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Thinking | Action: analyze | OSSD: Analyse various aspects of monetary policy in Canada and their impact on the eco

Official: D3 | Node: CIA4U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: All six monetary policy goals can be achieved simultaneously without any trade-offs, or inflation control has no employment cost.

Remediation → err-monetary-goals-tradeoff | Retest → err-monetary-goals-tradeoff

  1. A. Unemployment immediately falls because lower inflation boosts consumer spending.
    Disinflation usually does not immediately boost employment; the lesson explicitly notes that raising rates to curb inflation may temporarily increase unemployment.
  2. ✓ B. Unemployment may temporarily rise, reflecting a trade-off between lower inflation and employment.
    Correct. The lesson states that policy targets can conflict in the short run; disinflation can temporarily raise unemployment.
  3. C. All six monetary policy goals are achieved simultaneously without any costs.
    The lesson highlights short-run trade-offs. Simultaneous success is not what happens when the Bank raises rates to fight inflation.
  4. D. The money supply automatically expands, so no further policy action is needed.
    Raising the policy interest rate is a contractionary action; it does not automatically expand the money supply.

Q2. Based on the diagram, which statement correctly describes the relationship among Canada's money supply definitions?

errorId: err-money-supply-classification | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Analyse various aspects of monetary policy in Canada and their impact on the eco

Official: D3 | Node: CIA4U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students think M1 includes M2, or that M0/M1/M2 are unrelated categories.

Remediation → err-money-supply-classification | Retest → err-money-supply-classification

  1. ✓ A. M2 includes M1, which includes M0.
    Correct. M0 is physical cash, M1 adds demand deposits, and M2 adds money market shares and savings deposits.
  2. B. M1 includes M2, which includes M0.
    The order is wrong. M1 is narrower than M2; M2 contains M1, not the other way around.
  3. C. M0 includes M1, which includes M2.
    M0 is the narrowest measure, so it cannot include the broader M1 or M2 definitions.
  4. D. M0, M1, and M2 are mutually exclusive categories.
    Each broader definition builds on the previous one. M1 contains M0, and M2 contains M1.

Misconception: Students believe a central bank can independently control both an interest rate and a monetary aggregate at the same time.

Remediation → err-target-incompatibility | Retest → err-target-incompatibility

  1. ✓ A. Impossible, because a central bank cannot precisely target both at the same time.
    Correct. The lesson states a central bank can target either an interest rate or a monetary aggregate, but not both simultaneously.
  2. B. Normal, because interest rates and monetary aggregates are independent tools.
    They are not independent. Choosing one determines money-market conditions and makes the other endogenous.
  3. C. Possible, if the aggregate target is set first and the interest rate target is then abandoned.
    That would mean targeting only one of them. The strategy described in the stem tries to target both at once.
  4. D. Required by the inflation-targeting framework since 1991.
    Inflation targeting sets an inflation band, not a simultaneous interest-rate and monetary-aggregate target.

    补救模块

    err-monetary-goals-tradeoff

    The student believes the Bank of Canada can simultaneously achieve high employment, steady growth, price stability, interest rate stability, financial market stability, and foreign exchange market stability with no costs. The student does not see that raising interest rates to curb inflation may temporarily increase unemployment.

    Explain: Canada’s monetary policy has six core objectives, but they can conflict in the short run. The lesson states that raising interest rates to curb inflation may temporarily increase unemployment. Price stability is the top priority because unanchored inflation disrupts long-term planning and creates social conflict. When the Bank of Canada raises rates by 300 basis points to cool demand and ease inflation, it accepts a possible short-term softening in employment as part of the trade-off.

    Example: Inflation is above the 1–3% target range. The Bank raises its policy interest rate. The higher rate cools demand and helps bring inflation back toward the 2% midpoint, but it also slows hiring, so unemployment may temporarily rise. This is a short-run trade-off between lower inflation and employment.

    err-money-supply-classification

    The student treats M0, M1, and M2 as separate, unrelated categories instead of nested aggregates, so they may place demand deposits in M2 or omit cash from M1.

    Explain: Canada's money supply is classified into three nested definitions. M0 is physical cash only. M1 is M0 plus demand deposits. M2 is M1 plus money market shares and savings deposits. Therefore every component of M0 is also in M1, and every component of M1 is also in M2. When classifying, start with cash, then add demand deposits to get M1, then add money market shares and savings deposits to get M2.

    Example: Cash in circulation is M0. Once demand deposits are added to cash, that total becomes M1. Adding money market shares and savings deposits to M1 produces M2.

    err-target-incompatibility

    The student thinks interest-rate targeting and monetary-aggregate targeting are independent tools that can be set at the same time, rather than mutually exclusive alternatives. This often comes from treating central bank actions as if they can directly control both the price and quantity of money without conflict.

    Explain: The central bank operates in the money market. When it sets a target for the interest rate, it must supply or absorb reserves to hold that rate; the quantity of money is then whatever the public and banks want at that rate. Conversely, if it commits to a specific growth rate for an aggregate like M1, it cannot also hold interest rates fixed—rates will rise or fall to keep money growth in line. Therefore, trying to target both would force contradictory actions. The Bank of Canada therefore chooses one operating target at a time, based on measurability, controllability, and predictable correlation with its final goals.

    Example: In the late 1970s and early 1980s (1975–1981), the Bank of Canada chose monetary aggregate targeting, gradually reducing M1 growth. It did not simultaneously fix a precise interest-rate target. Later, after 1991, it adopted explicit inflation targeting; within that framework, it uses the interest rate as its operating instrument. The shift between phases shows that the Bank could not pursue both at once.

    err-inflation-targeting-lag

    Inflation targeting is often misunderstood as an immediate, precise tool. In reality, monetary policy operates with long and variable lags, so a change in interest rates today affects output before it affects inflation, potentially creating short-run output fluctuations.

    Explain: The Bank of Canada's inflation-targeting framework—targeting 1–3% inflation with a 2% midpoint—has been in place since 1991. It is designed to keep long-run inflation expectations anchored near 2%, and it has generally succeeded over 30 years. However, monetary policy works with long and variable lags: when the Bank raises interest rates, it takes time for borrowing, spending, investment, and ultimately prices to respond. Because the lag is long and unpredictable, by the time the policy takes full effect, economic conditions may have changed, which can cause output to overshoot or undershoot. In the short run, this trade-off appears as fluctuations in output and employment.

    Example: The Bank of Canada raised rates by 300 basis points since March to cool domestic demand and ease inflation. These rate hikes did not instantly lower inflation; rather, they work gradually through the economy. The Bank also abandoned its 'transitory inflation' forecast after the Russia–Ukraine war pushed up commodity and supply chain costs, acknowledging that inflation would persist longer than initially expected. If inflation targeting worked instantly, there would be no need for a 300-basis-point campaign, and no risk of temporary output disruption.

    err-plaza-accord-members

    The student confuses the G5 Plaza Accord with the later G7 Louvre Accord, incorrectly believing Canada and Italy were part of the 1985 agreement.

    Explain: The 1985 Plaza Accord was signed by the G5 countries: the United States, United Kingdom, France, West Germany, and Japan. Canada and Italy were NOT part of this agreement. They later joined the 1987 Louvre Accord, which expanded the group to the G7. The Plaza Accord aimed to depreciate the US dollar after it had appreciated 50% from 1980 to 1985.

    Example: Imagine a five-member club (G5) meets in 1985 to fix a currency problem: the US dollar is too strong and hurting exports. They agree to coordinate intervention. Canada and Italy are not club members yet, so they do not attend. That club later expands to seven members (G7) in 1987 for the Louvre Accord.

    err-louvre-accord-outcome

    The student confuses the Louvre Accord with the Plaza Accord's success. The Louvre Accord was a G7 attempt to halt the dollar's unplanned depreciation, but it failed to stabilise exchange rates and was followed by the 1987 global stock market crash.

    Explain: The 1985 Plaza Accord succeeded because the G5 countries coordinated a depreciation of the US dollar, which fell roughly 35% by 1987. By 1987, the dollar's continued decline was unplanned, so the G7 (G5 plus Canada and Italy) signed the Louvre Accord to try to halt it. Unlike the Plaza Accord, the Louvre Accord failed to stabilise exchange rates, and the 1987 global stock market crash followed. Therefore, the Louvre Accord is an example of coordinated intervention that did not achieve its goal.

    Example: After the dollar appreciated 50% from 1980 to 1985, the Plaza Accord produced a coordinated depreciation of about 35% by 1987. The 1987 Louvre Accord tried to stop further dollar declines but did not stabilise exchange rates; it preceded the 1987 global stock market crash.
