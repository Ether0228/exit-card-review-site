Q1. Two skaters push on each other. Skater A exerts a force of 70 N east on Skater B. According to Newton's Third Law, what is the force exerted by Skater B on Skater A?

errorId: err-action-reaction-cancel | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Analyse and propose improvements to technologies that apply concepts related to

Official: C1 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students think the forces cancel each other because they are equal and opposite, ignoring they act on different bodies.

Remediation → err-action-reaction-cancel | Retest → err-action-reaction-cancel

  1. A. 0 N, because the forces cancel
    Action and reaction forces act on different objects, so they don't cancel. Skater B exerts a force back on Skater A.
  2. ✓ B. 70 N west
    Correct. Newton's Third Law states the reaction force is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, acting on the other object.
  3. C. 70 N east
    The reaction force is opposite in direction to the action force. Skater B pushes west, not east.
  4. D. More than 70 N east because Skater B is larger
    The forces are always equal in magnitude regardless of mass or size. Newton's Third Law is universal.

Q2. A cart (mass 0.50 kg) is connected by a rope over a pulley to a hanging mass (mass 0.20 kg). The system accelerates. Which statement best describes the tension in the rope?

errorId: err-tension-different | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students assume tension is greater on the heavier side or varies along the rope.

Remediation → err-tension-different | Retest → err-tension-different

  1. A. Tension is greater on the cart side because it has more mass.
    In a massless rope over a frictionless pulley, tension is the same throughout. The system's acceleration arises from the net force on the total mass.
  2. B. Tension is greater on the hanging mass side because it is accelerating downward.
    Tension is a contact force that is transmitted equally along the rope. Both ends experience the same tension magnitude.
  3. ✓ C. Tension is the same on both sides of the rope.
    Correct. For an ideal rope (massless and inextensible) over a frictionless pulley, the tension is uniform.
  4. D. Tension cannot be determined without knowing the acceleration.
    Although we need the acceleration to calculate the tension value, the property of uniformity always holds for an ideal rope.

Q3. In the cart-hanging mass system from the lesson, the acceleration of the cart is 3.26 m/s². What is the acceleration of the hanging mass?

errorId: err-connected-acceleration-mismatch | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students think the hanging mass accelerates differently (faster or slower) than the cart.

Remediation → err-connected-acceleration-mismatch | Retest → err-connected-acceleration-mismatch

  1. A. 0 m/s² because it is hanging
    The hanging mass is not in equilibrium; it accelerates downward. Since the rope is taut, it must accelerate at the same rate as the cart.
  2. ✓ B. 3.26 m/s² downward
    Correct. Both objects are connected by a rigid rope, so they have the same magnitude and direction of acceleration (hanging mass downward, cart horizontal).
  3. C. Greater than 3.26 m/s² because it is falling
    The rope constrains the motion; the acceleration is determined by the net force on the total system, and both parts accelerate together.
  4. D. Less than 3.26 m/s² because it is heavier
    Mass does not affect the acceleration ratio in a connected system; both objects have the same acceleration magnitude.

Q4. A 0.20 kg mass hangs from a rope and accelerates downward at 3.26 m/s². What is the direction of the net force acting on the hanging mass?

errorId: err-direction-net-force-hanging | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Students think net force is upward because tension pulls up, or that net force is zero because the object is moving downward.

Remediation → err-direction-net-force-hanging | Retest → err-direction-net-force-hanging

  1. A. Upward, because tension pulls up
    Although tension acts upward, the net force is the vector sum of weight (down) and tension (up). Since the mass accelerates downward, the net force must be downward.
  2. ✓ B. Downward, because it is accelerating downward
    Correct. According to Newton's second law, the net force is in the direction of acceleration. The hanging mass accelerates downward, so net force is downward.
  3. C. Zero, because the object is moving at constant speed
    The problem states acceleration is 3.26 m/s², not constant speed. A net force is required to accelerate.
  4. D. Depends on the tension value
    The direction of net force is determined by the direction of acceleration. Once we know acceleration is downward, net force must be downward regardless of the tension magnitude.

补救模块

err-action-reaction-cancel

Students think action and reaction forces cancel because they are equal and opposite, ignoring that they act on different objects.

Explain: According to Newton's Third Law, for every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force. However, these forces act on different objects, so they do not cancel each other. Cancellation requires forces to act on the same object. For example, when two skaters push on each other, Skater A exerts 70 N east on Skater B, and Skater B exerts 70 N west on Skater A. These forces are on different skaters, so they don't cancel. Each skater experiences a net force from the other, causing them to move apart.

Example: In the lesson, a cart and hanging mass system with acceleration 3.26 m/s² and tension 1.57 N demonstrates that forces are not canceled; the hanging mass accelerates downward because the net force is downward.

err-tension-different

Students assume tension is different on each end of a rope in a connected system.

Explain: In a connected system with a massless, inextensible rope over a frictionless pulley, the tension is the same throughout the rope. Both objects experience the same magnitude of tension because the rope transmits force equally. This is a key property for solving problems with connected masses.

Example: In the lesson's cart (0.50 kg) and hanging mass (0.20 kg) system, the calculated tension is 1.57 N. This tension acts on the cart to the right and on the hanging mass upward, but both are 1.57 N.

err-connected-acceleration-mismatch

Students think objects in a connected system may have different accelerations, forgetting that a rigid connection forces them to accelerate together.

Explain: In a connected system, objects are linked by a taut, inextensible rope. This forces them to move as one unit: the magnitude of acceleration is the same for all parts. For example, in the cart-hanging mass system from the lesson, both the cart and the hanging mass share the same acceleration magnitude (3.26 m/s²). The direction may differ (the cart moves horizontally, the hanging mass moves vertically), but the rate of change of speed is identical.

Example: Consider a cart (0.50 kg) connected by a rope over a pulley to a hanging mass (0.20 kg). The system accelerates; the lesson calculated the acceleration as 3.26 m/s². This single value applies to both objects: the cart accelerates horizontally at 3.26 m/s², and the hanging mass accelerates downward at 3.26 m/s².

err-direction-net-force-hanging

Students think the net force on the hanging object is upward because tension acts upward, or that net force is zero because the object is moving downward. They forget that net force is determined by the direction of acceleration, not by individual forces.

Explain: Newton's Second Law states that the net force on an object is equal to its mass times its acceleration, and the net force always points in the same direction as the acceleration. For a hanging mass accelerating downward, the net force must be downward, even though tension pulls upward. The net force is the vector sum of all forces. Since acceleration is downward, the downward gravitational force (weight) must be greater than the upward tension. The net force is the difference (weight minus tension) and points downward.

Example: In the cart and hanging mass system from the lesson, the hanging mass accelerates downward at 3.26 m/s². The net force on the hanging mass is the product of its mass and acceleration, and must be downward. The downward weight is larger than the upward tension, causing the net downward force.
