Q1. In a study, weekly study time (hours) is used to explain test score (%). Which variable is the dependent variable (y)?

errorId: err-LR-confuse-variables | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Analyse, interpret, and draw conclusions from two-variable data using numerical,

Official: D2 | Node: MDM4U-D-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student chooses the explanatory variable as y because they think it 'comes first' or is more important.

Remediation → err-LR-confuse-variables | Retest → retest-err-LR-confuse-variables

  1. A. Weekly study time, because it is measured in hours.
    Measurement unit does not determine which variable is dependent. Study time is the explanatory variable (x).
  2. B. Weekly study time, because it is used to explain the test score.
    The variable used to explain is the independent variable (x), not the dependent variable.
  3. ✓ C. Test score, because it is the outcome being predicted.
    Correct. The test score is the outcome that depends on study time, so it is the dependent variable (y).
  4. D. Test score, because it is measured in percent.
    Measurement unit does not determine the dependent variable. The outcome being predicted determines y.

Q2. For the grade dataset, the regression equation is Data Management mark = 0.62 × Grade 12 average + 29.58. The data include Grade 12 averages from 65% to 95%. What is the best interpretation of the intercept 29.58?

errorId: err-LR-intercept-meaningless | Bloom: analyze | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: interpret | OSSD: Analyse, interpret, and draw conclusions from two-variable data using numerical,

Official: D2 | Node: MDM4U-D-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student assumes the intercept is a real baseline value even when x=0 is outside the range of observed data.

Remediation → err-LR-intercept-meaningless | Retest → retest-err-LR-intercept-meaningless

  1. A. Every student will score at least 29.58 in Data Management.
    The intercept is not a minimum score; it is the predicted y-value when x=0.
  2. B. A student with a Grade 12 average of 0% would be predicted to score 29.58, so this is the baseline mark.
    No student in the data has an average near 0%, so extrapolating to x=0 is not meaningful.
  3. C. A one-point increase in Grade 12 average raises the predicted Data Management mark by 29.58.
    29.58 is the intercept, not the slope. The slope is 0.62.
  4. ✓ D. Since x=0 is outside the observed data range, the intercept is not a meaningful prediction by itself.
    Correct. The intercept is a mathematical value, but it should not be interpreted as a real prediction when x=0 is outside the observed data range.

补救模块

err-LR-confuse-variables

They think the variable that is mentioned first, measured in a particular unit, or used to explain something is automatically y. Actually, y is always the outcome being predicted or explained, and x is the predictor or cause.

Explain: In linear regression, the independent variable (x) is the input or cause, and the dependent variable (y) is the outcome or effect. The regression equation is always written as y = slope × x + intercept, so you substitute x to predict y. To tell them apart, ask: 'Which variable changes because of the other?' The one that changes as a result is y.

Example: In the grade dataset, the goal is to use Grade 12 overall average to explain Data Management marks. So x = Grade 12 average and y = Data Management mark. The correct equation is: Data Management mark = 0.62 × Grade 12 average + 29.58. If you swap them, you would be predicting Grade 12 average from Data Management mark, which is not the intended question.

err-LR-intercept-meaningless

The student thinks the intercept is a meaningful baseline or minimum value in the context, without checking whether x=0 is within or near the data range.

Explain: In a regression equation y = slope·x + intercept, the intercept is the predicted y-value when x = 0. However, this interpretation is only valid if x = 0 is within (or close to) the range of x-values in the data. When x = 0 is outside the observed range, the intercept is a mathematical constant that positions the line, but it does not represent a real-world prediction.

Example: For the grade dataset, the regression equation is Data Management mark = 0.62 × Grade 12 average + 29.58. The slope 0.62 means each additional percentage point in Grade 12 average is associated with a predicted increase of 0.62 points in Data Management mark. The intercept 29.58 would be the predicted Data Management mark if a student had a Grade 12 average of 0%, but all observed Grade 12 averages are around typical high-school values, far above 0%. Therefore, the intercept has no meaningful real-world interpretation for these students.

err-LR-residual-pattern

The student thinks a regression model is good only if residuals are very small, and that any residual pattern is acceptable as long as residuals are not large. They miss the key rule: residuals must be small and randomly scattered around zero to confirm a good fit.

Explain: A residual is the difference between an observed y-value and the predicted y-value from the regression line. A well-fitting model should have residuals that are small and randomly scattered around zero. If the residual plot shows a clear pattern — such as a curve or a fan shape — then the linear model is missing something, even if the residuals themselves are not large. So when you assess fit, always check for randomness in the residual plot first; the size of individual residuals alone is not enough to declare a model poor or good.

Example: For the grade dataset, the regression equation is Data Management mark = 0.62 × Grade 12 average + 29.58, with R² = 0.86. Imagine you plot the residuals and see a clear U-shaped curve. That pattern tells you the linear model does not capture the true relationship, even though R² is high. In contrast, if the residual plot looks like a random cloud of points scattered around zero, then the linear model is a good fit, regardless of a few moderately sized residuals.

err-LR-r-square-misinterpret

The student confuses the coefficient of determination R² with the correlation coefficient r, thinking R² measures strength/direction of association rather than the proportion of variation in y explained by x.

Explain: In linear regression, r and R² tell different things. r, the correlation coefficient, measures the strength and direction of the linear relationship and ranges from -1 to +1. R², the coefficient of determination, is the proportion of variation in the dependent variable (y) explained by the independent variable (x); it ranges from 0 to 1. A high R² means the regression line explains most of the variability in y, not that the correlation coefficient is equal to R².

Example: In the student grade dataset, R² = 0.86. This means 86% of the variation in Data Management marks is explained by Grade 12 average. It does not mean the correlation is 0.86. In the NBA dataset, r = 0.632 (moderate positive correlation) and R² = 0.39, meaning 39% of variation in home wins is explained by average home attendance.
