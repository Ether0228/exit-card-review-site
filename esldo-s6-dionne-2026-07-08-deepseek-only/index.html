Q7. Write one body paragraph about how learning English helps with career opportunities. Your paragraph should follow the TEE C format (Topic sentence, Evidence, Explanation, Concluding sentence).

errorId: err-tee-c-format | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L3

Achievement: Application | Action: construct | OSSD: Write in a variety of forms for different purposes and audiences.

Official: C1 | Node: ESLDO-C-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/esl912currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Student may omit explanation or write a weak topic sentence.

Remediation → err-tee-c-format | Retest → err-tee-c-format

    Rubric: Topic sentence clearly states that learning English improves career opportunities (e.g., 'Learning English can improve your career opportunities in many ways.'). Evidence provides a specific example (e.g., 'For example, many companies require employees to speak English.'). Explanation connects the evidence to the topic (e.g., 'This shows that English skills make you more eligible for international jobs.'). Concluding sentence wraps up (e.g., 'Therefore, learning English is a valuable investment for your future.'). Full paragraph is coherent and on topic.

    Sample: Learning English can greatly improve your career opportunities. For instance, many multinational companies require employees to speak English fluently. This means that by learning English, you become eligible for a wider range of jobs and can work in different countries. In conclusion, English skills open doors to better employment options.

    补救模块

    err-paragraph-unity

    Student thinks it is acceptable to include multiple unrelated topics in a single paragraph, not realizing that a paragraph must focus on one main idea for coherence.

    Explain: A paragraph must focus on one main idea. Use the hamburger analogy: the topic sentence is the top bun (states the main idea), the supporting details are the meat and ingredients (explain the main idea), and the concluding sentence is the bottom bun (wraps up). If you jump between different topics, your reader gets confused. For example, paragraph A: 'Yesterday I went to the store. Then I studied math. My sister likes dogs. I also play soccer.' — this jumps topics and lacks unity. Paragraph B: 'Learning English is important. It helps you communicate with more people and get a better job. Many resources are available online to practice English.' — this stays on one topic: the importance of learning English.

    Example: Bad (jumping topics): 'Yesterday I went to the store. Then I studied math. My sister likes dogs. I also play soccer.' Good (one main idea): 'Learning English is important. It helps you communicate with more people and get a better job. Many resources are available online to practice English.'

    err-topic-sentence-missing

    Student may omit the topic sentence or write one that does not clearly state the paragraph's main idea, often confusing a supporting detail or a general fact as the topic sentence.

    Explain: A paragraph is like a hamburger: the topic sentence is the top bun. It tells the reader the main idea of the whole paragraph. Every detail in the paragraph should support that main idea. Without a clear topic sentence, the reader will not know what the paragraph is about.

    Example: Consider this paragraph: 'First, it helps you make friends from different countries. Second, you can understand movies and songs in English. Finally, it is useful for traveling.' The best topic sentence is: 'There are many advantages to learning English.' This sentence tells the reader the paragraph will list benefits.

    err-thesis-not-previewing

    The student thinks a simple statement of the topic is sufficient for a thesis, not realizing that a thesis should also preview the main points that will be explained in the body paragraphs.

    Explain: A thesis statement is the last sentence of your introduction. It tells the reader your main idea AND previews the key points you will discuss in the body paragraphs. Without a preview, the reader doesn’t know what to expect. For example, a thesis that says 'Learning English has many benefits' is too vague. A better thesis would be 'Learning English is important because it improves communication, career opportunities, and personal growth.' This tells the reader exactly what the essay will cover.

    Example: From our lesson: 'Learning English is important because it helps with communication, career opportunities, and personal growth.' This thesis states the topic (learning English) and previews three reasons that become body paragraphs.

    err-tee-c-format

    The student may think that only a topic sentence and evidence are needed in a body paragraph, not realizing the importance of an explanation that connects evidence back to the topic.

    Explain: A body paragraph in an essay follows the TEE C format: Topic sentence, Evidence, Explanation, Concluding sentence. The topic sentence states the main idea of the paragraph. Evidence supports that idea with a fact, example, or quote. Explanation explains how the evidence proves the topic sentence. The concluding sentence wraps up the paragraph and transitions to the next idea. Without explanation, the reader does not understand why the evidence matters.

    Example: Example body paragraph: 'Learning English helps you communicate with more people. (Topic sentence) For example, you can talk to international friends and colleagues. (Evidence) This shows that English breaks language barriers and allows you to connect with a wider community. (Explanation) In conclusion, improving your communication skills through English is valuable. (Concluding sentence)'

    err-transition-missing

    You may not know how to use transition words to connect ideas smoothly. This can make your writing feel choppy and hard to follow.

    Explain: Transition words are like bridges that connect your ideas. They help the reader understand how your sentences relate to each other. For example, use 'first', 'second', 'third' to list steps, 'because' or 'therefore' to show cause and effect, and 'in conclusion' to end your paragraph. Without transitions, your writing can feel like separate sentences instead of a single, flowing paragraph.

    Example: Without transition: 'Learning English is useful for school. It can help you get a job.' With transition: 'Learning English is useful for school. Additionally, it can help you get a job.' The word 'additionally' connects the two ideas and shows you are adding another point.

    err-conclusion-repeat

    Student thinks that the conclusion should simply restate the thesis or introduction word-for-word, rather than summarizing main points in a new way and providing a final thought.

    Explain: The conclusion is the bottom bun of your essay. It should remind the reader of your main points—restating the thesis in fresh words and summarizing the key ideas—plus add a final thought to leave a lasting impression. Do not copy the introduction exactly.

    Example: For an essay on learning English with main points communication, career, and personal growth, a good conclusion: 'In conclusion, learning English helps you communicate with more people, improves your career opportunities, and allows you to grow personally. Embracing this skill can open many doors.' This restates main points and adds a final thought.
