Q1. In projectile motion without air resistance, the vertical component of velocity:

errorId: err-projectile-vertical-constant | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Official: B2 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: Analyze projectile motion as independent horizontal and vertical motions under gravity.

Misconception: Students think vertical velocity remains constant because horizontal velocity is constant.

Remediation → err-projectile-vertical-constant | Retest → retest-vertical-constant

  1. A. is constant because gravity is not acting vertically
    Gravity acts vertically, causing acceleration at 9.8 m/s² downward.
  2. ✓ B. accelerates downward at 9.8 m/s²
    Correct. Gravity causes constant downward acceleration, so vertical velocity changes.
  3. C. is always zero
    Vertical velocity is zero only at the peak of the trajectory; it changes throughout the flight.

Learning Goal: Analyze projectile motion as independent horizontal and vertical motions under gravity.

Misconception: Students think horizontal velocity changes due to gravity or other forces.

Remediation → err-projectile-horizontal-accelerates | Retest → retest-horizontal-accelerates

  1. A. increases due to gravity
    Gravity acts only vertically; horizontal acceleration is zero.
  2. ✓ B. remains constant
    Correct. No horizontal force means constant horizontal velocity.
  3. C. decreases over time
    Without air resistance, horizontal speed does not decrease.

Q3. Which of the following is the correct SI unit for force?

errorId: err-force-unit-confusion | Bloom: remember | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Investigate net force, acceleration, and mass in qualitative and quantitative te

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: Define force as a vector with SI unit Newton and describe common force types.

Misconception: Students confuse Newton with other units like kg·m or N·m.

Remediation → err-force-unit-confusion | Retest → retest-force-unit

  1. A. kg·m/s
    kg·m/s is the unit of momentum, not force. Force is kg·m/s² (Newton).
  2. ✓ B. kg·m/s²
    Correct. One Newton is equal to kg·m/s².
  3. C. kg·m
    kg·m is not a valid derived SI unit for force.
  4. D. N·m
    N·m is the unit of torque or work, not force.

Q4. If the net force on an object is zero, the object:

Official: C2 | Node: SPH3U-C-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: Distinguish between balanced and unbalanced forces and their effect on motion.

Misconception: Students think zero net force always means the object is stationary.

Remediation → err-net-force-zero-no-motion | Retest → retest-net-force-zero

  1. A. must be accelerating
    Acceleration requires a non-zero net force. Zero net force means no acceleration.
  2. ✓ B. may be at rest or moving at constant velocity
    Correct. According to Newton's first law, zero net force means constant velocity (including zero velocity).
  3. C. must be at rest
    Zero net force can also result in constant velocity motion, not just rest.

Learning Goal: Construct free body diagrams to represent forces on an object.

Misconception: Students omit the normal force or include fictional forces like 'force of motion'.

Remediation → err-free-body-omit-forces | Retest → retest-free-body

  1. A. Only the force of gravity
    The normal force from the table must also be included; it balances gravity.
  2. ✓ B. Gravity and the normal force
    Correct. The gravitational force (down) and normal force (up) are the only vertical forces acting on a book at rest on a horizontal surface.
  3. C. Gravity, normal force, and friction
    Friction only appears if there is a horizontal force trying to move the book. In this static case with no other forces, friction is zero and not typically drawn.
  4. D. Gravity, normal force, and an applied force
    No applied force is mentioned for a book simply resting on a table.

补救模块

err-projectile-vertical-constant

Students incorrectly assume that vertical velocity remains constant because horizontal velocity is constant, forgetting that gravity acts vertically.

Explain: In projectile motion without air resistance, the horizontal velocity is constant, but the vertical velocity changes because gravity pulls the object downward with constant acceleration g = 9.8 m/s². This means the vertical velocity decreases on the way up, is zero at the peak, and increases downward on the way down. The motion is independent: horizontal motion is uniform, vertical motion is uniformly accelerated.

Example: Consider a golfer hitting a ball from a 30 m cliff with an initial speed of 25 m/s at 50° above horizontal. The initial vertical velocity component is 25 sin 50° ≈ 19.2 m/s upward. The ball reaches a maximum height, then falls. The flight time is 5.11 s, and the final vertical velocity is 30.9 m/s downward, showing that vertical velocity changed from 19.2 m/s upward to 30.9 m/s downward—not constant.

err-projectile-horizontal-accelerates

Students may think the horizontal component of velocity changes due to gravity or other forces, not understanding that in the absence of air resistance, the horizontal velocity remains constant because no horizontal force acts on the projectile.

Explain: In projectile motion without air resistance, the only force acting on the projectile is gravity, which acts vertically downward. There is no horizontal force, so the horizontal component of velocity remains constant throughout the flight. This is a key concept: horizontal motion is uniform (constant velocity) while vertical motion is uniformly accelerated (due to gravity).

Example: Consider a golfer hitting a ball from a 30 m cliff with an initial speed of 25 m/s at 50° above the horizontal. The horizontal component of velocity is 25 m/s * cos(50°) ≈ 16.1 m/s. During the entire 5.11 s flight, the horizontal velocity remains 16.1 m/s, resulting in a horizontal range of 82.2 m (calculated as 16.1 m/s * 5.11 s). The ball does not speed up or slow down horizontally.

err-force-unit-confusion

They may think the Newton is equivalent to kg·m/s or other units, or forget it is kg·m/s².

Explain: Force is measured in Newtons (N), where 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². This comes from Newton's second law: F=ma. So force is mass times acceleration, hence kg times m/s².

Example: A 2 kg object accelerating at 5 m/s² experiences a net force of 10 N (=2 kg * 5 m/s²).

err-net-force-zero-no-motion

Students think that net force equals zero always means the object is stationary, but Newton's first law states that zero net force results in constant velocity (which can include rest).

Explain: When the net force on an object is zero, there is no acceleration. According to Newton's first law of motion, an object will maintain its state of motion: if it is at rest, it stays at rest; if it is moving, it continues moving with constant velocity. This is because one or more forces cancel out, resulting in no change in velocity.

Example: Consider a car cruising on a straight highway at constant speed. The forward force from the engine balances the backward friction and air resistance, so net force is zero. The car moves at constant velocity. Similarly, a book at rest on a table has gravity pulling down balanced by the normal force pushing up; net force is zero, so it remains at rest.

err-free-body-omit-forces

Students often think only gravity acts on a stationary object, forgetting that the surface exerts a normal force upward.

Explain: A free body diagram must include all forces acting on the object. For an object on a surface, there are at least two forces: gravity pulling down and the normal force pushing up from the surface. If there is motion or other interactions, include friction, tension, etc. Always ask: what is touching the object? and what non-contact forces (like gravity) are acting?

Example: Book on a table: gravity (down) and normal force (up). If the book is sliding, add friction opposing motion.
