补救模块

err-independent-events-confusion

Students often think that any two random events are independent, or that drawing without replacement is independent because it seems random.

Explain: Two events are independent if the occurrence or outcome of one has no effect on the probability of the other. For example, rolling a 6-sided die and flipping a fair coin are independent because the die outcome does not affect the coin flip. In contrast, drawing two marbles from a bag without replacement is dependent because the first draw changes the composition of the bag, affecting the probability of the second draw.

Example: Rolling a 6-sided die and flipping a coin: P(die shows 3) = 1/6, P(coin heads) = 1/2, and P(die shows 3 and coin heads) = (1/6)×(1/2)=1/12. The coin flip is unaffected by the die roll. But drawing two cards without replacement from a deck: P(first card Ace) = 4/52, but P(second card Ace given first Ace) = 3/51, which is not equal to 4/52.

err-multiplicative-counting-addition

Students may add numbers of outcomes instead of multiplying when counting total outcomes for a sequence.

Explain: When counting the total number of outcomes for a sequence of independent events, you multiply the number of outcomes for each event, not add them. Adding would only count the total number of choices if you were picking only one item from a single set. For example, if you have 4 pants and 6 shirts, the total outfits is 4 × 6 = 24, not 4 + 6 = 10.

Example: You have 4 pants, 6 shirts, and 2 pairs of shoes. The total number of different outfits is 4 × 6 × 2 = 48. If you added, you would get 4 + 6 + 2 = 12, which is incorrect because each combination of pants, shirt, and shoes is a distinct outfit.

err-conditional-probability-misapplication

You may be confusing conditional probability P(B|A) with joint probability P(A and B). For independent events, the occurrence of A does not affect the probability of B, so P(B|A) = P(B), not P(A and B).

Explain: For independent events A and B, the conditional probability of B given A equals the probability of B alone. This is because the outcome of A has no effect on B. The joint probability P(A and B) is found by multiplying P(A) × P(B), which is a different concept.

Example: Rolling a 6-sided die and flipping a coin are independent. The probability the coin shows heads given the die shows 4 is P(heads | die 4) = P(heads) = 1/2. Compare: P(heads and die 4) = (1/2) × (1/6) = 1/12, which is not the same as 1/2.

err-complement-forgetting

The student does not recognize that computing the probability of the complement (no successes) and subtracting from 1 is often simpler and less error-prone than calculating the probabilities of 1, 2, ... successes and summing them.

Explain: For repeated independent events (like multiple coin flips, free throws, or guesses on a test), the probability of at least one success can be found using: P(at least one success) = 1 - P(no successes). This is because 'no successes' is the opposite (complement) of 'at least one success'. Instead of adding many separate probabilities, we just calculate the single probability that no success occurs, which is often just the product of the probabilities of failure for each trial.

Example: A basketball player has an 85% chance of making a free throw. If she takes three free throws, what is the probability she makes at least one? P(at least one make) = 1 - P(all misses) = 1 - (0.15 × 0.15 × 0.15) = 1 - 0.003375 = 0.996625.

err-joint-probability-without-independence

Students may apply P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) without verifying that the events are independent.

Explain: The formula P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B) only works when events A and B are independent. Independence means the outcome of one event does not affect the probability of the other. Always check if events are independent before multiplying probabilities. For example, if you draw two cards without replacement, the events are dependent, so you cannot use multiplication directly. If events are independent, like rolling a die and flipping a coin, you multiply their individual probabilities.

Example: Find P(rolling a 3 on a six-sided die and flipping heads on a coin). Die and coin are independent. P(3) = 1/6, P(heads) = 1/2, so joint probability = 1/6 × 1/2 = 1/12.
