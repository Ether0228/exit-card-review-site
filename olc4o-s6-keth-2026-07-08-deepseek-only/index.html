Q1. Which of the following is a correctly written news headline?

Official: B2 | Node: OLC4O-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/english12curr.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students think headlines must be complete sentences with articles and past tense.

Remediation → err-headline-full-sentence | Retest → err-headline-full-sentence

  1. A. A student won a scholarship at the local school.
    This headline uses past tense ('won') and articles ('a', 'the'). Headlines use historical present tense and omit articles.
  2. ✓ B. Student Wins Scholarship at Local School
    Correct! This headline uses present tense ('Wins'), no articles, and is specific.
  3. C. Student Won Scholarship at Local School
    This headline uses past tense ('Won'). Headlines should use historical present tense for urgency.
  4. D. The Student Wins Scholarship at Local School
    This headline includes the article 'The', which should be omitted for conciseness.

    补救模块

    err-headline-full-sentence

    Students think headlines must be complete sentences with articles (a, an, the) and past tense verbs, instead of using historical present tense and omitting articles for conciseness.

    Explain: News headlines are not complete sentences. They use historical present tense (past events written in present tense) and active voice. Remove articles (a, an, the) and conjunctions (and → comma) to save space and increase impact.

    Example: Instead of 'The student won a scholarship at the school' → write 'Student wins scholarship at school'

    err-headline-passive-voice

    Students think passive voice is acceptable in headlines, but active voice creates more immediacy and is required for effective headlines.

    Explain: Headlines should use active voice because it is more direct and engaging. Passive voice often omits the doer or places it at the end, making the headline less immediate.

    Example: Passive (bad): 'Money was stolen by politician' -> Active (good): 'Politician steals money'

    err-lead-too-long

    Students think summary leads can be long and include unnecessary details, violating the 35-word maximum and the requirement for conciseness.

    Explain: A summary lead is the opening paragraph of a news story that must answer the 5W1H (Who, What, Where, When, Why, How) in 35 words or fewer. It should be concise and direct, avoiding word clutter like extra adjectives, adverbs, or redundant phrases. Long leads lose reader interest and fail to deliver key information quickly.

    Example: Long: 'Over 300 students from various schools in the city joined the Division Science Quiz B that was held at the Binney Elementary School last September 11th with great enthusiasm.' (27 words, but wordy and includes unnecessary phrases) Concise: '300 students join Division Science Quiz B at Binney Elementary School last September 11th.' (12 words, direct and covers 5W1H)

    err-lead-it-because-start

    Students think it is acceptable to begin news leads with filler words like 'It' or 'Because' instead of using a strong noun or verb to engage the reader immediately.

    Explain: News leads must grab the reader's attention instantly. Starting with 'It' or 'Because' wastes space and weakens the impact. Instead, begin with a specific person, action, or event. For example, 'It was a win for the team' should be 'The team won the championship.'

    Example: Weak lead: 'It was a day of celebration at the school.' Strong lead: 'Students celebrated their victory at the school.'

    err-lead-weak-verbs-adjectives

    Students think that adding adverbs and adjectives makes a lead more descriptive, but effective news leads use strong action verbs and specific nouns to create vivid imagery without extra modifiers.

    Explain: News leads should use strong action verbs (e.g., erupted, roared) instead of weak verbs with adverbs (e.g., cheered loudly) or adjectives (e.g., very exciting). Strong verbs make the writing concise and impactful.

    Example: Weak: 'The game was very exciting and the crowd cheered loudly.' Strong: 'The crowd erupted as their team scored.'

    err-lead-quotation-not-identify-speaker

    Students often include a quote from an event but forget to attribute it to a specific person, which reduces the credibility of the news story because readers don't know who said it.

    Explain: A quotation lead starts with a direct quote from someone involved in the news story, and it must immediately identify the speaker. The speaker's name and role provide context and credibility. Example from lesson: 'This victory is for our school, said Grade 6 pupil Andrea, after winning first place.' The speaker (Andrea) and her role (Grade 6 pupil) are clearly stated.

    Example: A correct quotation lead: 'We are thrilled to receive the grant,' said Principal Maria Santos. An incorrect one: 'We are thrilled to receive the grant.' (no speaker identification).
