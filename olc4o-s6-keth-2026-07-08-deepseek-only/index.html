补救模块

err-headline-passive-voice

Student may think passive voice is acceptable because it sounds formal or objective, but headlines require active voice for directness and power.

Explain: Headlines must use active voice to be direct and powerful. Active voice means the subject performs the action (e.g., 'Student wins award'), while passive voice means the subject receives the action (e.g., 'Award won by student'). Passive voice is weaker, wordier, and less immediate. Always choose active verbs.

Example: Passive headline: 'Library visited by students' → Active headline: 'Students visit library'

err-headline-past-tense

Student believes that because the event occurred in the past, past tense verbs should be used in headlines.

Explain: In news headlines, we use 'historical present tense': write past events in present tense to create urgency and immediacy. Even if the event happened yesterday, the headline makes it feel current and engaging.

Example: Correct: 'Mayor announces new park' (present tense for yesterday's announcement). Incorrect: 'Mayor announced new park' (past tense).

err-headline-articles

The student thinks articles are necessary for grammatical correctness in headlines, not realizing that headline writing rules prioritize conciseness.

Explain: News headlines must be concise and direct. Removing articles (a, an, the) saves space and improves flow. For example, 'The mayor opens a new library' becomes 'Mayor opens new library'.

Example: Original: 'The fire hits the Tondo area' → Improved: 'Fire hits Tondo area'

err-headline-and-comma

Student does not know the headline convention to replace the word 'and' with a comma to save space and improve flow.

Explain: In news headlines, the word 'and' should be replaced with a comma. This makes the headline more concise and avoids unnecessary words. For example, instead of 'Stores to sell rice and groceries', write 'Stores to sell rice, groceries'.

Example: Headline improved: 'Stores to sell rice, groceries' (comma replaces 'and').

err-lead-word-clutter

Student thinks adding more details or redundant phrases makes the lead more informative, but it actually buries key facts and violates the 35-word limit.

Explain: A news lead must be concise: max 35 words for this course. Word clutter includes redundant phrases like '2 p.m. Wednesday afternoon' (p.m. and afternoon repeat the same idea), excessive adjectives, or unnecessary details. Every word should answer one of the 5W1H elements. Cutting clutter makes the lead sharper and easier to read.

Example: Wordy: 'At 2 p.m. Wednesday afternoon, over 300 students from various schools joined the Division Science Quiz B which was held at the Binney Elementary School last September 11th and it was a big event.' (40+ words, redundant). Concise: 'Over 300 students join Division Science Quiz B at Binney Elementary School September 11.' (13 words, no clutter).

err-lead-start-it-because

Student thinks starting leads with 'it' or 'because' is acceptable or does not realize it wastes space

Explain: Leads must start with the most important information. Starting with 'It' or 'Because' delays the key point and wastes words. Readers want the core details immediately.

Example: Bad: 'It was a sunny day when the mayor announced the park.' Good: 'Mayor announces new park on sunny day.'

err-lead-adverbs-adjectives

Thinks adverbs/adjectives make the lead more descriptive, but they weaken the punch and add word clutter.

Explain: Leads should be concise and powerful. Adverbs and adjectives often add unnecessary words and can make the lead sound weak or subjective. Instead, use strong, specific nouns and active verbs to convey the facts directly.

Example: Weak: 'Mayor hastily announces new park.' Strong: 'Mayor announces new park.'

err-lead-5w1h-missing

Student may think that a lead can skip some W/H questions or that only the most interesting details are necessary, not realizing that a lead must be a complete fact capsule covering all key aspects.

Explain: A news lead is a fact capsule that must answer all 5W1H questions: who, what, where, when, why, and how. This ensures readers get the core event instantly. Omitting any element leaves readers confused or forces them to search for basic information. For this course, leads must be 35 words max, but still include all 5W1H.

Example: Complete lead: 'Over 300 students join Division Science Quiz B at Binney Elementary School September 11.' This answers: Who (300+ students), What (join quiz), Where (Binney School), When (Sept 11). Missing 'why' and 'how'? It implies 'why' (competition) and 'how' (joined). But a more explicit lead: 'Over 300 students compete in Division Science Quiz B at Binney Elementary School on September 11 to win regional spot.' Now 'why' (to win) and 'how' (compete) are clear.

err-quotation-lead-feelings

The student thinks a quotation lead can include quotes about personal emotions or feelings, but it should contain a quote that explains the event, provides context, or adds credibility.

Explain: A quotation lead uses someone's exact words to give color and credibility. However, the quote must explain the event—such as stating a reason, a result, or a key fact—not just express feelings. The speaker must be identified, and the quote should advance the story.

Example: Good quotation lead: 'This victory is for our school,' said Grade 6 pupil Andrea after winning first place. (Quote explains the event's significance.) Bad quotation lead: 'I feel so happy,' said Andrea. (Quote only expresses emotion without explaining why or what happened.)

err-question-lead-yes-no

Student thinks a yes-no question can engage readers, but such questions fail to spark curiosity because the answer is simply yes or no.

Explain: A question lead starts with a question to make the reader curious. It must be an open-ended question that the story answers, not a simple yes-no question. Yes-no questions give away the answer immediately and don't hook the reader. Instead, use who, what, where, when, why, or how to invite deeper thinking.

Example: Weak (yes-no): 'Did the school win the science quiz?' Better (open-ended): 'Who will represent the city in the regional contest after today’s Quiz B?'

err-inverted-pyramid-order

The student believes that all information is equally important or that it is acceptable to start with background information rather than the core event.

Explain: The inverted pyramid structure orders information from most to least important. The lead (first paragraph) contains the 5W1H – the essential facts. The body provides details, evidence, and quotes. The tail includes extra insights that can be removed without losing the main meaning. Always put the most critical information first.

Example: Lead: 'Over 300 students join Division Science Quiz B at Binney Elementary School September 11.' (5W1H) Body: 'Participants answered questions on physics and chemistry. Winners from Grade 6 received medals.' (details) Tail: 'The event was organized by the school’s science club.' (extra insight)
