Q1. A sole proprietor's business fails and owes $50,000 to creditors. Which statement about the owner's personal assets is correct?

errorId: err-sole-proprietor-liability | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: understand | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of markets and theories of the firm.

Official: C1 | Node: CIA4U-C-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student thinks sole proprietors have limited liability like corporations

Remediation → err-sole-proprietor-liability | Retest → err-sole-proprietor-liability

  1. A. The owner's personal assets are protected; only business assets can be used.
    That's limited liability, which applies to corporations, not sole proprietorships. Sole proprietors are personally liable for all debts.
  2. ✓ B. The owner's personal assets are at risk; creditors can claim them to cover the debt.
    Correct. Sole proprietorship carries unlimited personal liability.
  3. C. The owner is only liable up to the amount invested in the business.
    That describes limited liability. Sole proprietors face unlimited liability regardless of investment.
  4. D. The owner can declare bankruptcy to protect personal assets from business debts.
    Bankruptcy may affect both business and personal assets. It does not guarantee protection of personal assets from all creditors.

    补救模块

    err-sole-proprietor-liability

    You may think that sole proprietors have limited liability, but in fact they carry unlimited personal liability. This means that if the business cannot pay its debts, creditors can go after your personal assets like your house or savings.

    Explain: A sole proprietorship is a business owned by one person. While it is easy to set up and gives you full control, it also comes with unlimited personal liability. That means you are personally responsible for all debts and obligations of the business. Unlike a corporation which protects your personal assets, a sole proprietorship offers no such separation. If the business fails and owes money, creditors can legally claim your personal property to satisfy the debt.

    Example: Imagine you run a small bakery as a sole proprietor. You take out a $10,000 loan to buy new equipment. If the bakery goes out of business and you can't repay the loan, the bank can sue you personally and potentially take your personal car or even your house to recover the money.

    err-partnership-max-members

    Student may not recall that partnerships have specific member caps depending on the industry: 10 for banking, 20 for other sectors.

    Explain: A partnership is co-founded by two or more members. The maximum number of members depends on the industry: for banking businesses, the cap is 10 members; for all other sectors, the cap is 20 members. This limit is set by law and must be included in a formal partnership agreement.

    Example: If a group of 12 accountants wants to form a partnership, they can do so because accounting is a non-banking sector, allowing up to 20 members. However, a group of 12 bankers cannot form a partnership because banking partnerships are limited to 10 members.

    err-cooperative-voting

    Students often confuse cooperative voting with corporate voting, assuming that voting power is proportional to shares owned (one-share-one-vote) rather than the cooperative principle of one-member-one-vote.

    Explain: In a cooperative society, every member has equal voting rights regardless of how many shares they own or how much business they do with the cooperative. This is called the one-member-one-vote rule. It ensures democratic control: each member gets exactly one vote in decisions like electing the board or approving major changes. This differs from joint stock companies where voting is typically tied to the number of shares held.

    Example: Imagine a consumer cooperative grocery store with 200 members. Member A shops there every week and holds 10 shares. Member B just joined and holds 1 share. When voting on whether to expand the store, both Member A and Member B get one vote each. Their voting power is equal, not based on shares or purchases.

    err-private-company-shareholder-limit

    Students may think private limited companies have no shareholder limit or a different number than 50.

    Explain: In Canada, private limited companies are a type of joint stock company that restricts ownership to a maximum of 50 shareholders. This limit distinguishes them from public limited companies, which have freely transferable shares and no shareholder cap. The 50-shareholder limit helps maintain the private nature of the company, preventing widespread public trading of shares.

    Example: Consider a group of 60 investors wanting to form a company. They cannot use a private limited company because that would exceed the 50-shareholder maximum. Instead, they would need to form a public limited company if they want to have more than 50 shareholders and sell shares to the public.

    err-business-form-one-size-fits-all

    Students may believe there is a universally optimal business form, ignoring that choice depends on development stage, capital demand, and risk control preference.

    Explain: There is no single 'best' business form for all entrepreneurs. The optimal choice depends on three core factors: development stage (startup vs. mature), capital demand (low vs. high), and risk control preference (full control vs. limited liability). For example, a sole proprietorship suits a low-capital startup seeking full control but carries unlimited personal liability; a private limited company protects personal assets but requires complex compliance. Each form has trade-offs: fast decisions vs. resource pooling, simplicity vs. liability protection, stability vs. flexibility.

    Example: In a Group 1 discussion, students evaluated a hypothetical tech startup. The founder wanted quick decisions and minimal paperwork, but also needed to pool skills with a partner. They chose a partnership to balance control and shared resources, understanding the unlimited liability trade-off. A corporation would have been overkill at the startup stage due to compliance costs.
