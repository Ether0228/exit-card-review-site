Q1. A company is launching a new smartphone. The marketing team describes the audience as “the general public.” The diagram below illustrates different ways to think about the audience. According to the lesson, what is the main problem with this description?

Official: A3 | Node: OLC4O-A-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/english12curr.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students think 'general public' is acceptable and covers everyone.

Remediation → err-audience-general-public | Retest → err-audience-general-public

  1. A. It is correct because everyone is a potential customer.
    Not everyone uses smartphones the same way. Age, tech knowledge, and budget matter. You must segment the audience.
  2. B. It is fine because 'general public' is a standard term in marketing.
    Standard doesn't mean effective. The lesson said no universal 'general public' exists; writers must analyze specific groups.
  3. ✓ C. It is too vague; the team should specify demographics, knowledge level, and attitudes.
    Exactly. Effective communication targets a specific audience, not everyone.
  4. D. It is wrong because only experts should be targeted.
    That's too narrow. The problem is vagueness, not exclusivity.

Misconception: Students assume all news is purely informational and ignore persuasive elements.

Remediation → err-purpose-only-informational | Retest → err-purpose-only-informational

  1. A. Only informational — it reports a fact.
    But the mayor's quote pushes action. That's persuasive. The text has a mixed purpose.

  3. ✓ C. Mixed — it informs about the opening and persuades people to visit.

    补救模块

    err-audience-general-public

    Students believe that 'general public' is a valid and specific enough description for an audience.

    Explain: In effective communication, there is no single 'general public' audience. All audiences are composed of specific groups with unique demographics, knowledge levels, and attitudes. A vague label like 'general public' prevents you from tailoring your message appropriately. Instead, you must identify specific characteristics such as age range, education level, prior knowledge about the topic, and attitudes (supportive, neutral, hostile). This is essential for meeting official expectation A3: understanding meaning through reading strategies.

    Example: Consider a local election flyer. If the author says 'to the general public,' the message may fail. But if they specify 'to residents aged 18-35 who may not have voted before, with limited knowledge of local issues and a skeptical attitude,' they can craft a persuasive message that addresses those factors. A real lesson example was the CEO who sent a delay notice as a calendar invite because the audience (employees) needed urgent attention—that targeted specific knowledge and attitudes.

    err-purpose-only-informational

    Students believe all texts have a single, purely informational purpose, ignoring persuasive or mixed purposes.

    Explain: Purpose is not always just to inform. Many texts blend informational and persuasive elements. The exigence (core motivation) can drive mixed purposes. For example, a smartphone ad includes objective specs (informational) and persuasive copy to encourage purchase. Always ask: 'What does the author want the audience to know, feel, or do?'

    Example: A newspaper article about a new park includes quotes from the mayor encouraging citizens to visit. This is a mixed purpose: it informs about the opening and persuades people to go.

    err-context-only-time

    Students think context equals only the date or time of publication, ignoring cultural, legal, environmental, and medium factors.

    Explain: Context is the full setting in which a text is created or received. It includes not just time (date, era) but also environment (physical or virtual space), legal rules (regulations, laws), cultural background (norms, values), and the delivery medium (print, video, live speech). For example, a CEO’s speech during a crisis is shaped by the legal regulations of the industry, the company's reputation, the culture of the workplace, and whether it's a live address or a recorded message.

    Example: A company’s urgent delay notice sent as a calendar invite (rather than an email) uses the medium to force attention. This shows how context (delivery medium) influences message reception.

    err-relevance-all-info

    Students include excessive detail without distinguishing between known and needed information.

    Explain: Relevant information balances what the audience already knows with what they need to know. For example, when writing a summary for classmates who have already read a novel, focus on key themes and character development instead of retelling the entire plot. The rhetorical triangle framework helps you consider audience and context to decide what is relevant.

    Example: A CEO sent an urgent delay notice as a calendar invite to force attention. This action considered the audience's need for immediate information and avoided irrelevant details.

    err-headline-missing-verb

    Students think headlines can be noun phrases without verbs; they fail to include an active present-tense verb to convey urgency and action.

    Explain: Headlines must include an active present-tense verb to show immediate action and engage readers. A headline without a verb is a fragment that lacks impact. Keeping it under 5 words forces concise, powerful writing.

    Example: Weak: 'Airport Emergency Closure' (no verb) → Strong: 'Police Grab Criminal at Gate' (active verb 'Grab' in present tense).
