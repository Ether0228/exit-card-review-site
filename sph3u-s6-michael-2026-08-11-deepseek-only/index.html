Q1. A student heats ice at 0°C until it completely melts, while continuing to supply heat. They measure the temperature during this process. What will they observe?

errorId: err-latent-heat-confuse-temp-rise | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of work, efficiency, power, gravitational potential

Official: D3 | Node: SPH3U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-thermal-latent

Misconception: Mistaking the constant-temperature energy input during a phase change for a temperature increase.

Remediation → err-latent-heat-confuse-temp-rise | Retest → err-latent-heat-confuse-temp-rise

  1. A. The temperature rises steadily as heat is added.
    You assumed temperature always rises with heat. But during a phase change, the added energy goes into breaking bonds, not raising temperature. Remember latent heat.
  2. ✓ B. The temperature stays constant at 0°C until all the ice has melted.
    Correct. During melting at constant pressure, temperature remains at 0°C while latent heat of fusion is absorbed.
  3. C. The temperature drops because the ice cools its surroundings.
    You might be mixing up the effect on surroundings with the system. The ice-water system is absorbing heat, so its temperature does not drop; it stays constant.
  4. D. The temperature rises until all ice melts, then stays constant.
    This reverses the order. Temperature remains constant during the phase change, then rises once all ice has melted.

Q2. Ice at -10°C is heated until it becomes water at 20°C. Which expression represents the total heat absorbed?

errorId: err-latent-heat-ignore-phase-change | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of work, efficiency, power, gravitational potential

Official: D3 | Node: SPH3U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-thermal-latent

Misconception: Forgetting to add the latent heat term (Q=mL) when calculating total heat for a scenario crossing a phase boundary.

Remediation → err-latent-heat-ignore-phase-change | Retest → err-latent-heat-ignore-phase-change

  1. A. Q = mcΔT (from -10°C to 0°C)
    This only covers heating the ice to 0°C, but not the melting or heating of water. Total heat includes all steps.
  2. B. Q = mL_fusion
    This is only the latent heat of fusion, ignoring the temperature changes of ice and water.
  3. C. Q = mcΔT (from 0°C to 20°C)
    This only heats the water, missing both the ice heating and the phase change energy.
  4. ✓ D. Q = mcΔT (ice from -10 to 0) + mL_fusion + mcΔT (water from 0 to 20)
    Correct. The total heat is the sum of heating ice, melting, and heating water.

补救模块

err-latent-heat-confuse-temp-rise

The student thinks that adding thermal energy always increases temperature, not realizing that during a phase change (like melting or boiling) the temperature stays constant while latent heat is absorbed.

Explain: During a phase change, thermal energy is gained or lost but temperature stays constant. For example, pure water at standard pressure melts at 0°C and boils at 100°C. The energy added while ice melts at 0°C is called latent heat of fusion; it breaks bonds between molecules instead of raising the temperature. Similarly, latent heat of vaporization is needed to change liquid to gas at constant temperature. Only after the phase change is complete does additional heating cause a temperature rise.

Example: Ice at 0°C absorbs heat to become liquid water at 0°C. The temperature remains at 0°C until all ice is melted. Only then does further heating raise the temperature above 0°C.

err-latent-heat-ignore-phase-change

The student thinks all heat added to a substance always raises its temperature, so they forget to include the latent heat term (Q=mL) that is required during a phase change at constant temperature.

Explain: Heating a substance doesn't always raise its temperature. At a phase change, the temperature remains constant while the substance absorbs or releases latent heat. The total heat for a process that crosses a phase boundary must include both sensible heat (Q=mcΔT) and latent heat (Q=mL). For example, to go from ice below 0°C to liquid water above 0°C, you need: heat the ice to 0°C (Q=mcΔT), melt the ice at 0°C (Q=mL_fusion), and heat the water to the final temperature (Q=mcΔT). Do not skip the middle step.

Example: Pure water melts at 0°C. If you have ice at a temperature below 0°C and you want liquid water at 0°C, the total heat is: Q = mcΔT (heating ice to 0°C) + mL_fusion (melting the ice). The mL_fusion term is essential and is not included in mcΔT.

err-isotope-same-protons-different-neutrons

The student believes isotopes are atoms of the same element with different proton counts. Actually, isotopes share the same number of protons (atomic number) and differ only in the number of neutrons.

Explain: Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same proton count) with different numbers of neutrons, hence different mass numbers. Atomic number = number of protons; mass number = protons + neutrons. Changing the neutron count changes the mass number but not the element.

Example: Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Carbon-14 also has 6 protons but 8 neutrons. Both are carbon because they have 6 protons, but they are isotopes because their neutron numbers differ.

err-atomic-number-mass-number-confusion

The student confuses atomic number (number of protons) with mass number (protons + neutrons), so they may think that adding neutrons changes the atomic number or that atomic number identifies the total particle count.

Explain: Atoms consist of a nucleus containing protons (+) and neutrons (0), with electrons (–) orbiting in energy levels. The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus. The mass number is the number of protons plus neutrons. In a neutral atom, electrons = protons. Because neutrons have mass but no charge, changing the neutron count changes the mass number but not the atomic number.

Example: Carbon has atomic number 6, so every carbon atom has exactly 6 protons. Carbon-12 has mass number 12 = 6 protons + 6 neutrons. Carbon-13 has mass number 13 = 6 protons + 7 neutrons. Carbon-14 has mass number 14 = 6 protons + 8 neutrons.

err-electron-shell-max

Electrons can be placed in shells without any capacity limits, such as putting 3 or more electrons in the first shell or 9 or more in the second shell.

Explain: Electrons are arranged in energy levels called shells around the nucleus. Each shell has a strict maximum capacity: the first shell holds at most 2 electrons, and the second shell holds at most 8 electrons. For a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons. When filling shells, you fill the first shell completely before placing electrons in the second shell. For example, carbon has 6 electrons: 2 go in the first shell, and the remaining 4 go in the second shell.

Example: A neutral carbon atom has 6 protons, so it must have 6 electrons. The correct electron arrangement is 2 electrons in the first shell and 4 electrons in the second shell. It would be incorrect to draw 3 electrons in the first shell or 9 electrons in the second shell because those exceed the maximum capacities.
