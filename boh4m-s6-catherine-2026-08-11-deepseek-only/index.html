    补救模块

    err-competitive-advantage-permanent

    The student thinks that once a company builds a competitive advantage, it will last forever. In reality, all competitive advantages are temporary and can be surpassed by competitors over time. This misconception ignores the dynamic nature of markets, ongoing innovation, and the continuous efforts of rivals to close the gap.

    Explain: A competitive advantage is a core strength that allows an organization to outperform its industry peers. However, no advantage is permanent. All competitive advantages are temporary and can be surpassed by competitors. This happens because competitors innovate, technology evolves, and market conditions shift. Even a strong advantage like Tesla's self-driving technology and battery performance is temporary; it was built on earlier R&D investment, but other companies are now investing heavily in similar technologies and will eventually catch up.

    Example: Tesla currently has a competitive advantage in electric vehicles through its full self-driving technology and battery performance. This advantage allowed Tesla to lead the market. Yet other automakers such as GM and Ford are rapidly developing their own autonomous systems and batteries. As they close the technology gap, Tesla's edge will shrink. This demonstrates that even the strongest competitive advantage is only temporary.

    err-strategic-planning-universal

    The student believes formal strategic planning is required for all businesses, but the lesson emphasized that small community-focused operations often lack the scale and resources to conduct formal planning.

    Explain: Strategic planning is a valuable tool, but it is not mandatory for every organization. The lesson clearly stated that strategic planning is not required for all businesses; small community-focused operations lack the scale and resources for formal planning. Larger firms with more complex operations may need formal plans to coordinate across departments and long time horizons, but a small local shop can often succeed with intuitive, day-to-day decisions.

    Example: Think of a small community coffee shop. The owner likely focuses on daily tasks like ordering supplies, serving customers, and managing a tiny team. They do not need a multi-year strategic plan or tools like PEST or Porter's Five Forces. In contrast, a large company like Tesla needs formal planning to align R&D, production, and marketing. The lesson used the community coffee shop as an example of a business that can skip formal planning due to limited size and resources.

    err-process-steps-order

    The student likely thinks that the process can start with resource allocation or implementation, or that evaluation is the beginning, not the end. They may not realize that a strategy must be formulated first to guide all subsequent decisions.

    Explain: The standard strategic planning process follows a fixed order. First, strategy formulation uses tools like SWOT, PEST, and Porter’s Five Forces to define the organization’s current position and target. Second, resource allocation distributes the necessary funds, people, and materials to carry out the chosen strategy. Third, implementation executes the plan across the organization. Fourth, regular effectiveness evaluation checks whether the strategy is working and makes adjustments. Formulation always comes first because you cannot wisely allocate resources or take action without a clear strategy; evaluation is the final step that feeds back into future planning.

    Example: A healthy tea brand wants to enter the market. It starts by conducting a PEST and SWOT analysis to understand demand and competition (formulation). Then it assigns a budget and hires staff (resource allocation). Next, it launches the product and runs a marketing campaign (implementation). Finally, it reviews sales data and customer feedback to refine the strategy (evaluation).
