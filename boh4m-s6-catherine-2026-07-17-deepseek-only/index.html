      补救模块

      err-trait-theory-universal

      Believing that Leadership Trait Theory identifies a universal set of traits that all effective leaders possess, without considering situational factors and limitations.

      Explain: Leadership Trait Theory is the earliest systematic leadership research, evaluating leaders on 6 dimensions and identifying 9 core traits (e.g., achievement drive, self-confidence). However, it has limitations: bias from physiological attributes, subjective definition of 'effective leadership', and it is not the sole scientific basis. Traits alone do not guarantee effectiveness; situational factors matter.

      Example: Alex Lines video summarized 5 core traits from meta-analysis, but the lesson also emphasized that these traits do not apply universally. For instance, a leader with high self-confidence may still fail in a crisis without situational adaptability.

      err-leadership-management-same

      Students may treat leadership and management as interchangeable concepts, but the lesson emphasizes they are distinct with separate core functions.

      Explain: Leadership and management are distinct concepts. Management focuses on following existing processes and completing tasks correctly, while leadership focuses on creating a vision and motivating people. The skills are not inherently linked; a person can be a good manager but not a good leader, and vice versa.

      Example: A manager ensures that project deadlines are met by organizing schedules and resources. A leader inspires the team to work towards a shared future goal, even if it means stepping outside standard procedures.

      err-behavioral-theory-replaces-traits

      Students may think behavioral theory completely replaces trait theory, but it emerged as a response to trait theory's limitations and both frameworks are used.

      Explain: Behavioral Theory emerged from the limitations of Trait Theory, but it does not replace it. Trait Theory identifies 9 core traits that often accompany effective leaders, while Behavioral Theory focuses on 2 observable dimensions: concern for task and concern for people. The instructor endorses Behavioral Theory because leadership can be learned, but both frameworks remain useful. Traits describe the 'what' of a leader; behaviors describe the 'how'.

      Example: A leader might have the trait of self-confidence (from Trait Theory) and also demonstrate high concern for people (from Behavioral Theory). The Blake-Mouton Managerial Grid uses the two behavioral dimensions on a 1-9 scale, but a leader's traits like charisma or cognitive reasoning can influence how they apply these behaviors.

      err-grid-style-misidentification

      Students confuse the characteristics of the five grid styles, especially mixing up Authority-Obedience (high task, low people) with Country Club (low task, high people), or mistakenly believe Team style (high both) is the only effective style, ignoring situational factors.

      Explain: The Blake-Mouton Managerial Grid maps two dimensions: concern for task and concern for people, each on a 1-9 scale. It identifies five main styles: Impoverished (low task, low people), Authority-Obedience (high task, low people), Country Club (low task, high people), Middle-of-the-Road (moderate both), and Team (high both). Each style has a unique combination of these two concerns.

      Example: A manager who focuses only on getting work done (high task) and ignores employee needs (low people) is using Authority-Obedience style. A manager who prioritizes employee happiness (high people) but lets tasks slide (low task) is using Country Club style.
