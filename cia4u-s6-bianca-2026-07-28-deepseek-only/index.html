  1. A. The percentage of the population living below the poverty line

  2. ✓ B. The distribution of income across a population, showing inequality
    Correct. The Lorenz curve shows the cumulative share of income held by different percentiles, and the Gini index summarizes that inequality.

    Economic growth is usually measured by GDP growth, not by the Lorenz curve or Gini index.

Q2. What is the correct range of the Gini index, and what does a higher value indicate?

errorId: err-gini-range | Bloom: remember | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: remember | OSSD: Explain how economists measure and represent standards of living and distributio

Official: DD1.3 | Node: ? | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2015cws11and12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students may think the Gini index ranges from 0 to 100 (like a percentage) or that a higher value means more equality.

Remediation → err-gini-range | Retest → err-gini-range

  1. A. 0 to 100; higher value means more equality
    The Gini index is not on a 0–100 scale. It is a decimal between 0 and 1, and a higher value means more inequality, not more equality.
  2. ✓ B. 0 to 1; higher value means greater inequality
    Correct. The Gini index ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality). A higher value indicates a more unequal distribution.
  3. C. 1 to 100; higher value means more poverty
    The Gini index is not measured on that scale, and it measures inequality, not poverty.
  4. D. 0 to 1; higher value means more equality
    The range is correct but the interpretation is reversed. A higher Gini index means more inequality, not equality.

    补救模块

    err-income-inequality-confusion

    Students may confuse income inequality with wealth inequality, or think the Gini index measures absolute poverty rather than relative distribution.

    Explain: Income inequality measures the gap in income between different social wealth classes. It is distinct from wealth inequality, which measures assets (like property and investments). The Lorenz curve and Gini index are tools to measure income inequality: the Lorenz curve shows cumulative income shares by population percentiles, and the Gini index (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality) summarizes that distribution. The poverty rate, by contrast, is the percentage of the population with family income below a fixed poverty line. These concepts are related but different.

    Example: In the United States, the bottom 20% of families hold 4% of total pre-tax income, while the top 20% hold 48.1%. This is an example of income inequality. If we plotted this on a Lorenz curve, it would show a large bend away from the perfect equality line. The Gini index would be relatively high (around 0.39 for the US). Meanwhile, the US poverty rate (e.g., 31.1% for single-mother households) is a separate measure counting those below the poverty line.

    err-gini-range

    Students may incorrectly think the Gini index ranges from 0 to 100 instead of 0 to 1, or misinterpret higher values as indicating more equality.

    Explain: The Gini index is a numerical measure of income inequality derived from the Lorenz curve. It ranges from 0 (perfect equality, where everyone has the same income) to 1 (perfect inequality, where one person has all the income). A higher Gini index means greater inequality. It is not a percentage (0-100).

    Example: In the United States, the bottom 20% of families hold only 4% of pre-tax income, while the top 20% hold 48.1%. This large gap would result in a high Gini index (close to 0.5). In contrast, Japan and Germany have more equal distributions, meaning their Gini indices are lower.

    err-poverty-rate-vs-inequality

    Students may think poverty rate and income inequality measure the same thing, but poverty rate is a count relative to a fixed line, while inequality compares distribution across all groups.

    Explain: The poverty rate is the percentage of the population whose family income falls below a government-set absolute poverty line. It measures how many people are in poverty. Income inequality, on the other hand, measures the gap in income between different social wealth classes across the entire population, often using tools like the Lorenz curve and Gini index. A country can have a low poverty rate but high inequality (e.g., a few very rich and many just above the poverty line) or high poverty rate but low inequality (e.g., most people are poor but incomes are similar).

    Example: In the United States, the bottom 20% of families hold 4% of total pre-tax income, while the top 20% hold 48.1% – this shows high inequality. At the same time, the poverty rate varies by household type: 31.1% for single-mother households and 5.9% for married-couple households. These two statistics tell different stories: the poverty rate counts those below a fixed line, while the Gini index would reflect the overall distribution.

    err-canada-productivity-cause

    Students may overlook that the root cause is 40 years of stagnant productivity, as argued by economist Moshe Lander.

    Explain: Canada's affordability crisis is not simply a result of high housing costs or inflation. According to Moshe Lander of Concordia University, the core root is Canada’s 40-year stagnant productivity. When productivity growth is slow, wages and living standards do not keep pace with costs. This makes everyday expenses like food and housing harder to afford. For example, since 2019, Canadian food bank usage has doubled, and 1 in 5 food bank users have formal jobs. Homelessness nearly doubled from 2018 to 2024. These indicators show that the problem is deeper than temporary price spikes.

    Example: Imagine a factory that produces the same number of widgets per hour as it did 40 years ago. Even if the price of each widget rises, the workers' real income growth is limited. That's like Canada's economy: productivity stagnation means overall economic growth is weak, leading to an affordability crisis despite some temporary price drops (like the 3% housing price drop in 2025).

    err-libertarian-redistribution

    Students may think libertarianism supports some redistribution, but it opposes mandatory redistribution and champions individual property rights.

    Explain: Libertarianism is one of three main redistribution philosophies. Unlike utilitarianism (maximize total utility) and liberalism (maximin criterion to help the worst-off), libertarianism opposes mandatory redistribution because it violates individual property rights. It prioritizes voluntary transfers and believes that any forced redistribution is unjust, even if it reduces inequality or increases overall well-being. In practice, this means libertarians generally oppose welfare, progressive taxation, and other government anti-poverty programs that require taking from some to give to others.

    Example: Consider the fact that 4 out of 5 US millionaires built their wealth independently, while only 1 in 5 inherited it. A libertarian would argue that the wealth earned by the self-made millionaires is rightfully theirs, and the government should not forcibly redistribute it to others, even if inequality exists. Instead, any help should come from voluntary charity.

    err-effective-marginal-tax-rate-misunderstanding

    Students think effective marginal tax rate refers only to income tax, not including benefit reductions, and may not see it as a disincentive to work.

    Explain: The effective marginal tax rate (EMTR) measures the proportion of an additional dollar of income that is taken through income taxes plus the reduction in government benefits. For low-income individuals receiving anti-poverty benefits, as they earn more from work, they may lose benefits like food stamps, housing subsidies, or cash assistance. This loss acts like an implicit tax. When combined with income taxes, the EMTR can be very high—sometimes exceeding 50% or even 100%—meaning the individual keeps very little of each extra dollar earned. This creates a 'trap' that discourages seeking formal full-time employment because working more may not significantly increase net income.

    Example: Consider a single parent receiving $1,000/month in welfare and $200/month in food stamps. They start a part-time job earning $500/month. Their income rises by $500, but they lose $300 in welfare and $50 in food stamps. Their net gain is only $150. The marginal tax from benefits is 70% (($300+$50)/$500). If income taxes also apply, the effective marginal tax rate could be even higher. This illustrates why some low-income individuals may prefer not to work additional hours.
