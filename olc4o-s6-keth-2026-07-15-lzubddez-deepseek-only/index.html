补救模块

err-metacognition-no-self-questioning

Students think metacognition is just general thinking about the text, not actively questioning their own understanding to identify gaps.

Explain: Metacognition means thinking about your own thinking. During reading, it specifically involves asking yourself questions to check if you understand and to identify what you don't get. For example, while reading, pause and ask: 'Do I understand this sentence? What is the main idea? What confuses me?' Then adjust your reading strategies (reread, look up words, or ask for help).

Example: While reading Harvey Lock's speech 'Stand Your Ground and Live with Nature', a reader using metacognition might think: 'Why does he say 'stand your ground'? Do I understand the metaphor? What does he want the audience to do? I'm not sure about 'indigenous rights' - let me reread that part.' This active self-questioning helps deepen understanding.

err-reading-internal-voice-recite-active

Thinking that reciting (repeating words verbatim) is an active reading strategy, when it is actually passive. The conversational voice that asks questions and makes connections is the active one.

Explain: There are three internal voices when reading: the distracting voice (unrelated thoughts), the reciting voice (just repeating words), and the conversational voice (questioning, connecting, predicting). Only the conversational voice is active because it engages with the meaning. Reciting sounds active but doesn't deepen understanding.

Example: While reading Harvey Lock's speech 'Stand Your Ground and Live with Nature', a conversational voice might ask, 'Why does he say stand your ground? How does this connect to my life?' whereas a reciting voice just repeats the words without thinking.

err-SQ4R-skip-review

Students think that once they have read and recorded notes, the information is stored in memory without needing to revisit it.

Explain: Review is a crucial step in the SQ4R method because it helps transfer information from short-term to long-term memory. Without review, you may forget key points soon after reading. Review involves actively recalling main ideas, not just re-reading.

Example: Suppose you read a chapter about the water cycle. You survey, question, read, record, and recite. If you skip review, you might recall only fragments the next day. But if you spend 5 minutes reviewing by summarizing aloud or using flashcards, you can retain the entire cycle.

err-opinion-paragraph-no-topic-sentence

Students may think that any sentence can introduce an opinion paragraph, or that supporting facts alone can serve as the main opinion.

Explain: An opinion paragraph always begins with a topic sentence that clearly states the writer's opinion. This sentence sets up the argument and tells the reader what the paragraph will be about. Without it, the reader cannot understand the main point.

Example: For example, if you are writing about Harvey Lock's speech, a topic sentence could be: 'Harvey Lock's message about living with nature is inspiring because it encourages a simpler lifestyle.' This sentence clearly states your opinion. The following sentences would then provide supporting details.

err-opinion-paragraph-confuse-fact-opinion

Students think that reporting facts is enough for an opinion paragraph, not understanding that the paragraph must express a personal belief or judgment.

Explain: An opinion paragraph requires you to state and support your own opinion, not just list facts. Facts are verifiable statements (e.g., 'The temperature reached 30°C'), while opinions express beliefs or judgments (e.g., 'School uniforms should be mandatory'). In your assignment, you must give your opinion about Harvey Lock's speech, not just summarize it.

Example: Fact: 'Harvey Lock's speech is titled Stand Your Ground and Live with Nature.' Opinion: 'I believe Harvey Lock's speech effectively argues for living in harmony with nature.'
