Official: C1 | Node: MHF4U-C-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student thinks odd multiplicity causes bounce or even multiplicity causes crossing.

Remediation → err-multiplicity-confusion | Retest → err-multiplicity-confusion

  1. A. cross the x-axis
    Multiplicity 4 is even, so the graph bounces off the x-axis, not crosses.
  2. ✓ B. bounce off the x-axis
    Correct. Even multiplicity causes the graph to bounce (touch and turn) at the root.
  3. C. have a cusp
    A cusp is not a typical feature of polynomial graphs at roots; bounce or cross are the expected behaviors.
  4. D. approach the x-axis asymptotically
    Polynomials do not have horizontal asymptotes; the graph will either cross or bounce at the root.

Remediation → err-end-behavior-degree-sign | Retest → err-end-behavior-degree-sign

    This describes positive leading coefficient with odd degree. Our leading coefficient is negative, so the ends are reversed.
  2. ✓ B. as x → ∞, f(x) → -∞ ; as x → -∞, f(x) → ∞

  4. D. as x → ∞, f(x) → ∞ ; as x → -∞, f(x) → ∞
    Both ends up would require even degree with positive leading coefficient. Here degree is odd and leading coefficient is negative.

补救模块

err-multiplicity-confusion

The student thinks odd multiplicity causes bounce or even multiplicity causes crossing, reversing the correct rule.

Explain: Root multiplicity tells you how many times a factor appears. If the multiplicity is even, the graph touches and bounces off the x-axis at that root. If the multiplicity is odd, the graph crosses the x-axis. For example, for the factor (x - 2)^3, multiplicity 3 (odd) means the graph crosses at x=2. For (x + 1)^2, multiplicity 2 (even) means the graph bounces at x=-1.

Example: Consider f(x) = (x - 1)^2 (x + 2). Root at x=1 has multiplicity 2 (even) so the graph bounces. Root at x=-2 has multiplicity 1 (odd) so the graph crosses.

err-end-behavior-degree-sign

Student may think both ends go the same direction regardless of degree, or misinterpret the effect of a negative leading coefficient on odd degree polynomials.

Explain: End behavior describes what happens to f(x) as x → ∞ and x → -∞. It depends on two things: degree parity (even/odd) and leading coefficient sign (positive/negative). For even degree, both ends go the same direction: up if positive, down if negative. For odd degree, ends go opposite directions: with positive leading coefficient, left end goes down and right end goes up; with negative leading coefficient, left end goes up and right end goes down. Remember: 'Even same, odd opposite; positive right up, negative right down.'

Example: For f(x) = -3x^5 + 2x^3 - 4: degree = 5 (odd), leading coefficient = -3 (negative) → as x → ∞, f(x) → -∞; as x → -∞, f(x) → ∞ (left up, right down).

err-missing-terms-division

When performing long division, students often omit terms for degrees that are not present in the polynomial, leading to misalignment and incorrect quotients.

Explain: In polynomial long division, you must include all terms from highest degree to constant term, even if the coefficient is zero. This ensures proper alignment of like terms.

Example: For (x³ + 6x - 2) ÷ (x + 1), rewrite as x³ + 0x² + 6x - 2. Then divide: quotient x² - x + 7, remainder -9.

err-synthetic-divisor-type

Thinks synthetic division can be used for any divisor, including quadratic or higher-degree divisors.

Explain: Synthetic division is a shortcut for polynomial long division, but it only works when dividing by a linear polynomial of the form x - k or ax - k. This is because the algorithm relies on the divisor having degree 1, allowing a simplified coefficient manipulation. For divisors of degree 2 or higher, you must use polynomial long division.

Example: Dividing 3x^4 - x^3 + 2x^2 - 4x - 17 by x + 4 (linear) works with synthetic division. But dividing by x^2 + 1 (quadratic) requires long division.

err-quotient-degree-misalignment

Students often think the quotient has the same degree as the dividend or miscalculate the degree reduction by more than one.

Explain: When dividing a polynomial by a linear divisor using synthetic division, the quotient's degree is always one less than the dividend's degree. For example, dividing a degree 4 polynomial yields a degree 3 quotient.

Example: Dividing 3x⁴ - x³ + 2x² - 4x - 17 by x + 4 gives quotient 3x³ - 13x² + 54x - 220 (degree 3). The dividend degree 4 becomes quotient degree 3.
