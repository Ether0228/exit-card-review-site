Q1. Read the following short passage and decide whether it is an informational or persuasive text: 'Pompeii was an ancient Roman city buried under volcanic ash after Mount Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD. The ash preserved buildings, artifacts, and even human remains, giving archaeologists a unique glimpse into daily life.'

Official: A2 | Node: OLC4O-A-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/english12curr.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Believing that any text containing facts is automatically informational, ignoring whether it also presents a debatable opinion.

Remediation → err-distinction-misclassify | Retest → err-distinction-misclassify

  1. ✓ A. Informational, because it only presents facts about Pompeii without arguing a position.
    Correct! The text provides objective facts and does not try to persuade the reader of a debatable point.
  2. B. Persuasive, because it contains historical information.
    Historical information alone does not make a text persuasive. The key is whether the text takes a stance on a debatable topic.
  3. C. Informational, because all paragraphs about history are informational.
    A history text could be persuasive if it argues a particular interpretation. Here, there is no argument, just facts.
  4. D. Persuasive, because it tries to convince readers that Pompeii is interesting.
    The passage does not explicitly try to convince; it merely reports what happened. Look for a clear claim or opinion.

    补救模块

    err-distinction-misclassify

    Confusing informational and persuasive texts by believing any text with facts is automatically informational, ignoring the presence of a debatable thesis.

    Explain: A persuasive text does more than state facts; it takes a side on a debatable issue. Informational texts present objective facts without a debatable claim. Even if a persuasive text contains factual information, it remains persuasive because its purpose is to convince the reader of a particular opinion. To classify a text, ask: 'Is the author arguing a debatable point?' If yes, it's persuasive. If the text simply reports facts without trying to persuade, it's informational.

    Example: Consider two Pompeii texts: Text A describes the eruption and preserved ruins purely with facts. Text B argues that Pompeii is a better travel destination than Rome because it offers better value. Text A is informational; Text B is persuasive because it compares and argues a debatable preference.

    err-structure-omission

    The student believes that a persuasive essay only needs an introduction, claim, and supporting evidence; they do not realize that addressing and refuting a counterargument is a required structural component.

    Explain: A standard persuasive essay has four parts: introduction, clear claim, supporting evidence, and counterargument. The counterargument shows that you understand the opposing view and can refute it, making your argument stronger and more logical. Without it, the essay is one-sided and less credible.

    Example: Using the Pompeii example, if you write a persuasive text arguing that Pompeii is the better travel destination because it offers better value, you must also address why a competing destination might appeal to some travellers and then explain why Pompeii still wins. That is the counterargument.

    err-appeal-misapply

    The student confuses the three rhetorical appeals: pathos (emotion), ethos (credibility/ethics), and logos (logic/reason). They may label a statement that appeals to authority as logical, or use an emotional story when concrete evidence is required.

    Explain: Remember: pathos appeals to emotion, ethos to credibility or ethics, and logos to logical reasoning. When you see statistics, evidence, and factual reasoning, that's logos. When an author mentions their expertise, qualifications, or moral character to build trust, that's ethos. When a description provokes feelings such as pity, fear, or happiness, that's pathos. Ask yourself: 'Does this make me feel, trust, or think?'

    Example: A persuasive paragraph about public transit: 'Public transit reduced commute times by 25% in our city last year.' This is logos because it gives a measurable statistic. 'As a transportation engineer with 15 years of experience, I recommend expanding the bus network.' This is ethos because it relies on the author's expertise. 'Imagine a single mother missing her night shift because the last bus left early' is pathos because it evokes sympathy.

    err-mainidea-detail

    The student mistakes a specific fact or piece of evidence for the central claim, focusing on a single detail instead of the overall message.

    Explain: The main idea is the most important point a text makes. It is the big umbrella that covers all the details. Supporting details are the specific facts, examples, or statistics that explain, prove, or elaborate the main idea. To find the main idea, ask: 'What is this mostly about?' not 'What one fact does it mention?'

    Example: In the passage 'Regular exercise provides numerous health benefits. Studies show it reduces the risk of heart disease, improves mental health, and helps maintain a healthy weight. Incorporating just 30 minutes of walking each day can significantly increase overall well-being,' the main idea is that regular exercise has many health benefits. The mention of heart disease, mental health, or 30 minutes of walking are supporting details.

    err-firstperson

    The student believes that phrases like 'I think' make their argument more personal and persuasive, or does not recognize that first-person language weakens objectivity and credibility in formal persuasive writing.

    Explain: In formal persuasive writing, your goal is to convince readers through logic and evidence, not through personal feelings. Using 'I think' or 'I believe' shifts focus to the writer's personal opinion and makes the argument feel subjective. Instead, state your claim directly and support it with authoritative sources. This maintains a logical, objective tone and strengthens ethos, your credibility as a writer.

    Example: Weak: 'I think e-sports should be included in the Olympics because they require skill.' Strong: 'E-sports should be included in the Olympics because they demand strategic thinking, precise teamwork, and rigorous training, as shown by competitive gaming statistics.' Notice how the revised version removes first-person and lets the evidence carry the argument.
