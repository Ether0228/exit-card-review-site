Q1. Which statement could be used as a valid persuasive claim in an ECA essay?

errorId: err-debatability-fact-as-claim | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Use knowledge of writing forms, and of the connections between form, audience, a

Official: B2 | Node: OLC4O-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/english12curr.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: The student treats universally accepted facts or low-debate comparative statements as arguable claims.

Remediation → err-debatability-fact-as-claim | Retest → err-debatability-fact-as-claim

  1. A. Water is necessary for human survival.
    This is a scientifically undisputed fact, so there is little room for disagreement.
  2. B. Toronto has a larger population than Ottawa.
    This is a low-debate comparative statement that invites no real argument.
  3. ✓ C. Public surveillance cameras in Canadian cities should be increased to reduce crime.
    Correct. This is debatable because people can reasonably disagree about the tradeoff between privacy and security.
  4. D. Video games were invented in the 20th century.
    This is a factual statement, not a persuasive claim.

补救模块

err-debatability-fact-as-claim

The student believes that any true statement can serve as a persuasive claim, without recognizing that persuasive claims must be debatable and open to disagreement.

Explain: A valid persuasive claim must be debatable, not a universally undisputed fact or a low-debate comparative statement. The core purpose of persuasive writing is to convince readers and shift their existing perspectives. If a statement is a well-known fact (e.g., water is necessary for survival) or a simple comparison (e.g., Toronto has a larger population than Ottawa), there is little room for disagreement, so it cannot drive a persuasive essay. Instead, a strong claim takes a position that reasonable people could argue against, such as 'Public surveillance cameras in Canadian cities should be increased to reduce crime.'

Example: Weak claim (fact): 'Water is necessary for human survival.' Nobody can debate this, so it fails as a persuasive claim. Strong claim (debatable): 'Public surveillance cameras in Canadian cities should be increased to reduce crime.' This invites disagreement about privacy, effectiveness, and cost, making it a valid persuasive claim.

err-infovspers-confusing-features

Confusing the neutral, fact-focused nature of informational text with the opinion-driven nature of persuasive text.

Explain: Informational text is neutral, objective, and fact-focused; its purpose is to educate. Persuasive text is opinion-driven and aims to convince readers to shift their existing perspectives. Persuasive text may use facts as supporting evidence, but the overall purpose is persuasion, not education. Persuasive text is often more engaging than informational text.

Example: Pompeii archaeological history is informational: it neutrally educates about the past. An Amalfi Coast travel argument is persuasive: it argues that the Amalfi Coast is worth visiting, using opinion and reasons to convince.

err-pronouns-using-personal

The student believes personal pronouns are acceptable in academic persuasive writing, not realizing that 'I think' weakens subjective opinions and 'you' creates defensive distance between writer and audience.

Explain: In academic persuasive writing, eliminate first-person (I, we) and second-person (you) pronouns to enhance credibility and authority. Personal pronouns make arguments sound subjective and can put the reader on the defensive. Instead, use neutral phrasing and support claims with evidence from credible external sources, such as peer-reviewed journals or institutional data.

Example: Instead of 'I think public surveillance makes cities safer,' write 'Public surveillance cameras are associated with reduced crime rates, according to institutional data.'

err-eca-skipping-counterargument

The student believes persuasive writing only needs to present their own supporting arguments and that mentioning the opposing side would weaken their position, so they skip the Counterargument & Refutation step of the 5-step ECA (ICCER) framework.

Explain: A strong persuasive argument must do more than state your own claim and evidence. It must acknowledge a valid opposing view and refute it. In the 5-step ECA (ICCER) framework, Counterargument & Refutation is Step 2. You can place it in a separate paragraph or embed it in each body paragraph. Use transition phrases like 'Opponents argue that' to introduce the counterargument and 'however' to signal your refutation. Skipping this step makes your essay one-sided and less credible, because it ignores the audience's likely objections.

Example: For an essay arguing that public surveillance cameras in Canadian cities should be increased, you could write: 'Opponents argue that surveillance cameras invade citizens' privacy. However, when cameras are used in public spaces for crime prevention, the measurable reduction in crime and increased public safety justify this careful limitation on privacy.' This shows you have considered and refuted the opposing view.

err-eca-evidence-without-rationale

Evidence alone is enough to prove the claim; readers will automatically see how it connects to the argument.

Explain: In the ECA (ICCER) framework, the 5 steps are Introduction/Thesis, Counterargument & Refutation, Core Claim, Evidence, and Rationale. Evidence must come from credible external sources, but it does not work alone. Rationale development builds a logical bridge between evidence and thesis, explaining real-world relevance to avoid evidence dumps. Without a rationale, the evidence feels dropped in and the argument loses persuasive force.

Example: Thesis: Public surveillance cameras in Canadian cities should be increased to reduce crime. Evidence: A peer-reviewed study found that neighbourhoods with cameras reported fewer property crimes. Rationale: This matters because it shows cameras are not just theoretical; they actually make neighbourhoods safer. Since protecting citizens is a core government duty, this evidence justifies supporting increased surveillance despite privacy concerns.
