    1. A. Physiological needs, because money is always motivating.

    2. B. Safety needs, because financial security is now guaranteed.
      Safety needs are also largely satisfied now, so they are less motivating according to the deficit principle.
    3. ✓ C. Belongingness needs, because lower needs are satisfied.
      Correct. The raise satisfies physiological and safety needs, so according to the deficit principle, they no longer motivate; the next level (belongingness) becomes the primary motivator.
    4. D. Self-actualization needs, because raises lead to personal growth.
      According to the progression principle, self-actualization is a higher need that may become motivating only after lower needs are met, but typically belongingness and esteem come first.

    补救模块

    err-traditional-money-only

    Believing that Taylor's Scientific Management is universally applicable today and that money alone is the only motivator, without considering that the early 1900s had extreme resource scarcity and that modern content theories recognise a broader range of human needs.

    Explain: Taylor's Scientific Management treated money as the sole incentive. That was partially reasonable in the early 1900s when extreme resource scarcity meant workers' primary need was survival. However, content motivation theories show that once basic needs are met, other needs become motivators. Maslow's hierarchy has five levels: after physiological and safety needs are satisfied, belonging, esteem, and self-actualization emerge as motivators. Mayo's Human Relations Approach similarly showed that feeling valued is a separate, important motivator. In today's workplaces, most employees have basic needs met, so relying only on money fails to address higher-level motivators like recognition, achievement, and social connection.

    Example: A company pays its employees a good salary but still sees low engagement. According to Maslow's deficit principle, the satisfied salary no longer motivates them; they may now be seeking belonging or esteem. Mayo's experiments demonstrated that workers responded strongly to being noticed and valued, not just to pay. Thus, while Taylor's approach was reasonable for his time, it is insufficient as a sole strategy today.

    err-content-vs-process

    The student confuses content motivation theories with process theories, believing that content theories analyze cognitive decision-making rather than focusing on individual needs.

    Explain: Motivation theories are divided into three categories: content, process, and reinforcement. Content motivation theories focus on individual needs and demands that influence work behavior. They ask WHAT motivates people. Process theories, in contrast, focus on the cognitive decision-making process—HOW people evaluate choices and decide to act. For example, Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs is a content theory because it identifies five needs (physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, self-actualization) that motivate behavior. A process theory would look at how an employee decides whether effort will lead to a reward.

    Example: When a manager asks 'What needs does my employee have?' they are using a content theory. For instance, Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory says motivators like achievement and recognition drive satisfaction. If a manager instead asks 'How does my employee decide whether to work harder based on the likelihood of a bonus?' they are examining the cognitive decision-making process, which is a process theory.

    err-maslow-deficit

    Thinking that a satisfied need continues to motivate behavior, violating the deficit principle.

    Explain: Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs places five needs in order: physiological, safety, belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. The deficit principle states that satisfied needs no longer motivate. The progression principle states that lower needs must be met before higher needs activate. So once a need is fulfilled, it stops pushing behavior; a higher-level need becomes the new motivator.

    Example: An employee who just received a large raise has satisfied physiological and safety needs. According to the deficit principle, those needs no longer motivate. The employee now feels belongingness needs as the next motivator, because lower needs are satisfied.

    err-erg-frustration

    Assumes unmet higher needs cause persistence at that level, rather than regression to lower needs as described by ERG theory's frustration-regression principle.

    Explain: Alderfer's ERG Theory condenses needs into exactly three levels: existence, relatedness, and growth. Its frustration-regression principle states that when higher needs (growth) are unmet, individuals regress to lower needs (relatedness or existence) instead of staying focused on the blocked level.

    Example: An employee denied growth opportunities stops pursuing challenging projects and instead invests more energy in friendships with coworkers or demands better pay and job security. The blocked growth need causes a shift to lower-level needs.

    err-herzberg-hygiene

    The student believes that improving hygiene factors (e.g., salary, work conditions, company policies) will motivate employees. In Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory, these factors only prevent dissatisfaction when present; they cannot create satisfaction. Motivation comes from intrinsic motivator factors like achievement, recognition, and growth.

    Explain: Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory separates workplace factors into 2 types. Hygiene factors (extrinsic, such as salary, work conditions, and company policies) prevent dissatisfaction but do not produce motivation. If they are absent, they cause job dissatisfaction; if adequate, they merely keep employees from being unhappy. Motivator factors (intrinsic, such as achievement, recognition, and growth) are what actually drive satisfaction and motivation. Therefore, paying a good salary does not make employees motivated; it only removes a source of dissatisfaction. To boost motivation, you must provide meaningful motivators.

    Example: Imagine an employee earning a competitive salary in a clean, safe office. That employee may not complain, but they are also not enthusiastic. If the same employee is given a challenging project and receives genuine recognition for completing it, they feel satisfaction and motivation. Raising salary further would still not make the work itself motivating; it would only avoid dissatisfaction.
