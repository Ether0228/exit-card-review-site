Q1. In a survey of 100 students, 60 are in Math club, 45 are in Science club, and 20 are in both. What is the probability that a randomly selected student is in either Math club or Science club?

errorId: err-union-forgot-intersection | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Solve problems involving the probability of an event or a combination of events

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student adds probabilities without subtracting intersection, thinking P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B) always.

Remediation → err-union-forgot-intersection | Retest → err-union-forgot-intersection

  1. ✓ A. 0.85
    Correct! P(Math ∪ Science) = (60+45-20)/100 = 85/100 = 0.85.
  2. B. 1.05
    You added only P(Math)+P(Science) without subtracting the overlap. Remember: P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A∩B).
  3. C. 0.20
    0.20 is the probability of being in both clubs, not the union.
  4. D. 0.65
    Check your counts. Did you perhaps use only one club's probability?

Q2. When rolling a standard six-sided die, which pair of events are mutually exclusive?

errorId: err-mutually-exclusive-assume-all | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Solve problems involving the probability of an event or a combination of events

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student thinks all events are mutually exclusive or misidentifies overlapping events as mutually exclusive.

Remediation → err-mutually-exclusive-assume-all | Retest → err-mutually-exclusive-assume-all

  1. A. Rolling an even number and rolling a number greater than 3.
    These events overlap (e.g., 4 and 6 are both even and greater than 3), so they are not mutually exclusive.
  2. B. Rolling an odd number and rolling a multiple of 3.
    3 is both odd and a multiple of 3, so these events overlap.
  3. ✓ C. Rolling a 3 and rolling an even number.
    Correct! A 3 is odd, so it cannot be even; they have no outcomes in common.
  4. D. Rolling a 2 and rolling an even number.
    2 is an even number, so these events overlap (rolling a 2 satisfies both).

    补救模块

    err-union-forgot-intersection

    Believes that P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) for any two events, ignoring the overlap that gets counted twice.

    Explain: When finding P(A or B), we add P(A) and P(B), but outcomes in both A and B are counted twice. To correct, subtract P(A and B) once: P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A∩B).

    Example: For a six-sided die: P(even) = 3/6, P(<3) = 2/6, P(even and <3) = 1/6 (outcome 2). So P(even or <3) = 3/6 + 2/6 - 1/6 = 4/6 = 2/3.

    err-mutually-exclusive-assume-all

    Students may incorrectly assume all events are mutually exclusive and skip the subtraction term when calculating P(A∪B).

    Explain: Not all events are mutually exclusive. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur simultaneously (e.g., rolling a 2 and rolling an even number are not mutually exclusive because 2 is even). When events are mutually exclusive, P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B). When they overlap, you must subtract the intersection: P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A∩B). Always check if events share outcomes before choosing the formula.

    Example: Blood donation: type O and type A are mutually exclusive (a person can only have one blood type), so P(O or A) = P(O) + P(A). But consider hockey stick inventory: 20 Bauer sticks, 28 left-handed sticks, 8 Bauer and left-handed. Bauer and left-handed are not mutually exclusive (some sticks are both). So P(Bauer or left-handed) = 20/60 + 28/60 - 8/60 = 40/60.

    err-venn-overlap-counting

    Students often double-count the elements that belong to both categories when finding the total for the union, or they forget to subtract the overlap when calculating the number in each category individually.

    Explain: When using a Venn diagram for two overlapping sets A and B, the total number in the union is: (only A) + (only B) + (both). Alternatively, total in A plus total in B minus both. To find only A, subtract both from total A. Similarly for B. Always verify that the sum of all regions equals the total sample size.

    Example: In a survey of 60 hockey sticks, 20 are Bauer, 28 are left-handed, and 8 are both Bauer and left-handed. Draw a Venn diagram: Bauer only = 20-8=12, left-handed only = 28-8=20, both = 8. Then union = 12+20+8=40. Check: 20+28-8=40.
