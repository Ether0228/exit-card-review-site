Q1. A ball is thrown horizontally from a cliff. Which statement about its horizontal motion is correct? (Ignore air resistance.)

errorId: err-proj-horizontal-accel | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of uniform and non-uniform linear motion, in one an

Official: B3 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students may think horizontal velocity changes due to gravity or that horizontal acceleration is 9.8 m/s².

Remediation → err-proj-horizontal-accel | Retest → err-proj-horizontal-accel

  1. ✓ A. Horizontal acceleration is 0 m/s² and horizontal velocity is constant.
    This is correct.
  2. B. Horizontal acceleration is 9.8 m/s² downward, so horizontal velocity increases.
    Gravity only affects vertical motion, not horizontal. Horizontal acceleration is zero.
  3. C. Horizontal acceleration is 9.8 m/s² to the right, so horizontal velocity increases.
    There is no horizontal force (ignoring air resistance), so horizontal acceleration is zero.
  4. D. Horizontal velocity decreases because the ball is slowing down.
    Without air resistance, horizontal velocity remains constant. The ball slows vertically, not horizontally.

Q2. A soccer ball is kicked upward at an angle. At the highest point of its trajectory, what is the vertical acceleration? (Ignore air resistance.)

errorId: err-proj-vertical-accel-zero-at-top | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of uniform and non-uniform linear motion, in one an

Official: B3 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-4

Misconception: Students often think vertical acceleration becomes zero at the top because velocity is zero momentarily.

Remediation → err-proj-vertical-accel-zero-at-top | Retest → err-proj-vertical-accel-zero-at-top

  1. A. 0 m/s² because the ball is momentarily at rest.
    Although vertical velocity is zero, acceleration due to gravity is constant at 9.8 m/s² downward.
  2. ✓ B. 9.8 m/s² downward.
    Correct! Gravity acts constantly.
  3. C. 9.8 m/s² upward because the ball is about to go down.
    Gravity always points downward. Acceleration doesn't change direction at the top.
  4. D. Depends on the initial launch angle.
    Vertical acceleration is always 9.8 m/s² downward regardless of angle.

Q3. A projectile is launched with speed 20 m/s at an angle of 30° above horizontal. What are the horizontal and vertical components of initial velocity? (Use sin30°=0.5, cos30°=0.87)

errorId: err-proj-component-swap | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Investigate uniform and non-uniform linear motion in qualitative and quantitativ

Official: B2 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Students often swap sine and cosine, using sine for horizontal and cosine for vertical.

Remediation → err-proj-component-swap | Retest → err-proj-component-swap

  1. A. Horizontal = 10 m/s, Vertical = 17.4 m/s
    You swapped sine and cosine. Horizontal uses cosine (20 × 0.87 = 17.4 m/s) and vertical uses sine (20 × 0.5 = 10 m/s).
  2. ✓ B. Horizontal = 17.4 m/s, Vertical = 10 m/s
    Correct: v_x = v cosθ = 20×0.87 = 17.4 m/s, v_y = v sinθ = 20×0.5 = 10 m/s.
  3. C. Horizontal = 17.4 m/s, Vertical = 17.4 m/s
    Components must add up vectorially; they can't both be 17.4 m/s with a 30° angle.
  4. D. Horizontal = 10 m/s, Vertical = 10 m/s
    Check your trigonometric values for 30°.

Q4. A ball is launched from ground level at an angle and returns to the same height. The time to reach maximum height is 1.2 s. What is the total flight time? (Ignore air resistance.)

Official: B2 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students may think total time equals time to max height, or they may try to recalculate from scratch instead of doubling.

Remediation → err-proj-time-up-down | Retest → err-proj-time-up-down

    Rubric: Full credit: 2.4 s (or 2.4 seconds). Accept reasoning that upward and downward times are equal. Partial credit: 1.2 s (only upward time). No credit: other numbers.

    Sample: The total flight time is 2.4 s because the time to go up equals the time to come down when starting and ending at the same height.

    补救模块

    err-proj-horizontal-accel

    In projectile motion without air resistance, there is no horizontal force, so horizontal acceleration is zero. Horizontal velocity remains constant throughout the flight.

    Explain: In projectile motion, horizontal and vertical motions are independent. Vertically, gravity causes constant downward acceleration of 9.8 m/s². Horizontally, there is no force (ignoring air resistance), so acceleration is zero. This means horizontal velocity never changes.

    Example: Consider a bean bag thrown horizontally from a height. It lands after 1.43 s, and the horizontal displacement is 4.3 m. The horizontal speed is constant (4.3 m / 1.43 s = 3.0 m/s) throughout the flight. It does not speed up or slow down horizontally.

    err-proj-vertical-accel-zero-at-top

    Student thinks that because vertical velocity is momentarily zero at the apex, the acceleration must also be zero. In reality, acceleration due to gravity is constant at 9.8 m/s² downward throughout the flight.

    Explain: In projectile motion, the vertical acceleration is always 9.8 m/s² downward, regardless of the object's position. At the maximum height, the vertical velocity is zero, but the acceleration remains constant. This is because gravity is a continuous force. Even at the top, the ball is still being pulled downward, which is why it begins to descend.

    Example: Consider a soccer ball kicked at 40° with speed 9.4 m/s. It reaches a maximum height of 2.12 m. At that instant, its vertical velocity is 0 m/s, but its vertical acceleration is still 9.8 m/s² downward. This constant acceleration causes the ball to then accelerate downward.

    err-proj-component-swap

    The student confuses the trigonometric functions for component decomposition, thinking that horizontal component is v sinθ and vertical is v cosθ.

    Explain: When launching a projectile at an angle θ above horizontal, the initial velocity v is split into horizontal and vertical components. The horizontal component is along the x-axis (adjacent to the angle) so it uses cosine: v_x = v cosθ. The vertical component is along the y-axis (opposite to the angle) so it uses sine: v_y = v sinθ. Remember: x = adjacent = cos, y = opposite = sin.

    Example: A soccer ball is launched at 40° with speed 9.4 m/s. Using cos40° ≈ 0.766 and sin40° ≈ 0.643, we get v_x = 9.4 × 0.766 ≈ 7.2 m/s and v_y = 9.4 × 0.643 ≈ 6.0 m/s.

    err-proj-time-up-down

    The student thinks that the time to reach maximum height is the total flight time, failing to account for the symmetric descent.

    Explain: For a projectile that launches from and lands at the same height (symmetric launch), the upward and downward parts of the flight are mirror images. Because vertical acceleration is constant (9.8 m/s² downward), the time to slow to zero at the top equals the time to accelerate back down to the launch speed. Therefore, total flight time = 2 × time to maximum height.

    Example: In the soccer ball example from class (launched at 40° with speed 9.4 m/s), the total flight time was 1.3 s. Since it lands at the same height, the time to reach maximum height is exactly half: 0.65 s.
