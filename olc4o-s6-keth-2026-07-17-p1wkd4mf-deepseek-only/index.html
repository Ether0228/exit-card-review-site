Misconception: Student may make a prediction without referencing personal experience, relying only on visual clues or irrelevant experience.

Remediation → err-prediction-no-personal-experience | Retest → err-prediction-no-personal-experience

    补救模块

    err-prediction-wild-guess

    Students may think any plausible guess is valid as a prediction, even if it lacks basis in the text or visual clues. They do not understand that predictions must be evidence-based educated guesses combining personal background, experience, and explicit clues.

    Explain: Predictions are not wild guesses. They are educated guesses based on evidence from the text or visual material, your own experiences, and cultural background. You must point to specific clues that support your prediction. A valid prediction combines personal cultural background, lived experience, and explicit clues from the text/visual.

    Example: For instance, seeing dark clouds and a character grabbing an umbrella, you can predict it will rain because the visual gives you direct clues. That's evidence-based, not a wild guess.

    err-prediction-inference-confusion

    The student does not understand that inference draws conclusions about past/present events while prediction anticipates future outcomes. They may think the two processes are identical or interchangeable.

    Explain: Prediction and inference both use evidence from the text or visuals, but they differ in time focus. Inference draws a conclusion about what is already implied or has happened (past/present). Prediction anticipates what will happen next (future). For example, seeing dark clouds and an umbrella, you can infer that the character is prepared for rain, and predict that it will rain soon.

    Example: From the short film 'Lather and Nothing Else', an inference is 'the barber is a secret revolutionary' (based on his internal thoughts). A prediction would be 'the barber will not kill the captain' (based on his character and the situation).

    err-prediction-no-cultural-factor

    The student assumes their own cultural norms apply universally, not recognizing that the story's cultural background may lead to different outcomes.

    Explain: Predictions must consider the cultural background of the story's setting. Cultural norms, traditions, and values influence characters' actions and story outcomes. The three factors are: cultural background, personal experience, and text/visual clues. Ignoring cultural context can lead to inappropriate predictions.

    Example: Children playing on a log in a river: if you ignore cultural background, you might predict they will fall (based on your own experience). But in some cultures, children are trained to balance on logs from a young age, so they might not fall.

    err-prediction-no-personal-experience

    Personal lived experience is unnecessary for making predictions; only text/visual clues matter.

    Explain: Valid predictions combine three factors: cultural background, personal lived experience, and explicit clues from the text/visual material. Your personal experience helps you interpret clues and imagine realistic outcomes. For example, if you see children playing on a wet log in a river, your experience with slippery surfaces helps you predict someone might slip.

    Example: In the 'Children Playing on a Log in River' image, a student who has fallen from a wet log (personal experience) would predict a child slipping, while a student who has only seen stable logs might predict they stay safe.

    err-inference-description-only

    The student thinks that simply restating what is visible already counts as an inference, not realizing that inference requires combining observed details with background knowledge to form a new conclusion that goes beyond the literal text or image.

    Explain: An inference is not just describing what you see; it’s using what you see plus what you already know to figure out something new. For example, if you see a person holding an umbrella and dark clouds, you don’t just say ‘I see an umbrella and clouds’—you infer ‘It’s going to rain.’ The inference combines the visual clues (umbrella, dark clouds) with personal knowledge (umbrellas are for rain, dark clouds mean rain) to reach a new conclusion.

    Example: Look at the firefighter image from class. Observations: the firefighter is crouching, holding a hose, and there is smoke in the background. Personal knowledge: firefighters extinguish fires. Inference: the firefighter is about to put out a fire. Notice the inference is NOT ‘I see a firefighter with a hose’ (that’s just description). It’s the new idea that goes beyond what’s directly visible.

    err-graphic-text-missing-detail

    The student thinks that simply naming the location (e.g., 'it's a park') is sufficient for analysis, without referencing specific visual elements (colors, objects, people's actions) and personal knowledge to make a valid inference.

    Explain: When analyzing a graphic text, a location description is not enough. You must include specific visual details you see and connect them with your own knowledge to form a deeper conclusion. For example, instead of saying 'a family room', say 'the room has a large TV showing a Spring Festival Gala and the family is seated together smiling, suggesting a cultural celebration.' Use the three types of visual clues: emphasized content (what stands out), body language/movement (postures, actions), and unusual details (anything that seems odd or significant). Then combine with your cultural background or personal experience to explain what the image means.

    Example: Look at the image of a family watching the Spring Festival Gala. Instead of just saying 'It's a living room,' a detailed inference would be: 'The large TV prominently shows the Spring Festival Gala (emphasized content), the family members are sitting close to each other with smiles (body language), and there are red decorations (unusual detail). These clues, combined with my knowledge of Chinese New Year traditions, tell me this is a family celebrating together during the holiday.'
