Q1. Aggregate demand (AD) is composed of which of the following sets of spending components?

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Aggregate demand only refers to household consumption spending.

Remediation → err-AD-onlyC | Retest → err-AD-onlyC

  1. ✓ A. Household consumption (C), business investment (I), government spending (G), net exports (X - M)
    Correct! AD equals C + I + G + (X - M), matching the expenditure approach to GDP.
  2. B. Business investment (I) and government spending (G) only
    This omits consumption and net exports. AD includes all spending on final goods and services.
  3. C. Household consumption (C) and government spending (G) only
    Investment and net exports are also part of AD. Think of the expenditure formula for GDP.
  4. D. Household consumption (C) only
    Consumption is the largest component, but AD includes business investment, government spending, and net exports as well.
  5. E. Business investment (I) and net exports (X - M) only
    Consumption and government spending are also part of AD. AD is the total of all spending sectors.

Learning Goal: lg-1

  1. ✓ A. Increase the real value of money and financial assets, leading to more consumption
    Correct! A lower price level increases real wealth, encouraging households to spend more.

补救模块

err-AD-onlyC

Aggregate demand is only household consumption spending; the other components—business investment, government spending, and net exports—are ignored.

Explain: Aggregate demand (AD) is the total spending in the economy, composed of household consumption (C), business investment (I), government spending (G), and net exports (X - M). This matches the GDP expenditure approach. Each of the four components contributes to total demand for final goods and services. AD is not just consumption; it includes spending by all sectors of the economy.

Example: When economists measure AD, they add up what households spend (C), what businesses invest in equipment and facilities (I), what governments purchase (G), and net exports (X - M). For instance, in our group discussion, students noted that an increase in AD raises employment and stimulates economic growth—that increase is driven by all four components together.

err-AD-wealth-misconception

The student believes the wealth effect refers only to stock market wealth or fails to understand that a higher price level reduces real wealth.

Explain: The wealth effect is one of the three reasons the AD curve slopes downward. When the overall price level rises, the real value of money and financial assets falls, making households feel poorer and causing them to reduce consumption. This effect applies broadly to wealth, not just to stock market wealth. A lower price level increases real wealth, which boosts consumption.

Example: When the price level rises, the same amount of money buys fewer goods and services, so households feel that their real wealth has decreased. As a result, they cut back on spending, which is why higher price levels lead to lower consumption.

err-AD-slope-vs-shift

Student confuses a movement along the AD curve (caused by a change in the price level) with a shift of the entire AD curve (caused by non-price factors such as government spending, money supply, or taxes).

Explain: The AD curve is downward sloping because of the wealth effect, the interest rate effect, and the foreign trade effect. These three effects describe how a change in the price level moves the economy along the same AD curve: a lower price level increases the quantity of real GDP demanded, and a higher price level decreases it. A shift of the AD curve occurs only when there is a change in a non-price factor—that is, a change in government expenditure, the money supply, or tax rates. When one of these factors changes, the entire curve moves right or left, meaning that at every price level the total spending (C + I + G + X - M) changes.

Example: Consider the federal government increasing infrastructure spending. This is an increase in the G component of AD, so the entire AD curve shifts right—more real GDP is demanded at every price level. In contrast, suppose the price level falls because of increased productivity. Consumers feel wealthier, interest rates fall, and net exports rise. These are movements along the same AD curve, not a shift.

err-LRAS-demand

The student confuses short-run demand-driven gains with long-run supply constraints, thinking AD can permanently boost real GDP. In reality, LRAS is vertical at full-employment output, so demand changes only affect the price level in the long run.

Explain: Aggregate demand can push output above potential in the short run, but the long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve is vertical at the full-employment level of real GDP. Long-run output is determined only by supply-side factors: capital, labour, and technology. When AD rises and output exceeds potential GDP, competition for labour and raw materials drives up wages and prices, shifting SRAS upward until the economy returns to long-run equilibrium at full employment. Therefore, a permanent increase in AD does not raise real GDP in the long run—it only raises the price level.

Example: Suppose the government increases infrastructure spending (G). In the short run, AD shifts right, firms hire more workers, and output rises above potential along the relatively flat SRAS curve. But, with output above potential, labour and raw materials become scarce, so wages and input prices are bid up. This shifts SRAS upward, and the economy moves back to the vertical LRAS at full employment. The final result is a higher price level, not a higher level of real GDP in the long run.

err-SRAS-upward

The student believes the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve is steeply upward-sloping or even vertical, confusing it with the long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve. This ignores the Keynesian flat range of SRAS, where prices and wages are sticky and changes in aggregate demand primarily affect output and employment.

Explain: In the short run, the SRAS curve is relatively flat because prices and wages are sticky. Sticky prices mean that when aggregate demand changes, firms adjust production (output and employment) rather than immediately changing prices. Therefore, an increase in AD mainly raises output and employment with only a small increase in the price level. This is different from the LRAS curve, which is vertical at full-employment output; LRAS is determined only by capital, labour, and technology, not by the price level. Drawing SRAS as a steep upward-sloping line incorrectly implies that price-level changes cause large price movements and little output response, which contradicts the Keynesian short-run behaviour.

Example: In the Group 1 discussion, students described how an increase in AD raises employment, pushes up the price level, and stimulates economic growth. On a correctly drawn SRAS curve, the increase in AD moves along a relatively flat curve, so the main effect is higher output and employment, with only a small upward movement in the price level. If the SRAS were drawn as a steep upward-sloping line, the same increase in AD would show mostly higher prices and little output gain—which is not the short-run outcome.

err-shock-types

The student believes all supply shocks affect output and prices the same way, or reverses the outcomes: thinking an adverse shock increases output and a favourable shock raises unemployment.

Explain: Supply shocks are sudden changes in production conditions that shift the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve. Adverse shocks, such as oil price spikes, reduce output, increase unemployment, and raise the price level. Favourable shocks, such as technology breakthroughs, increase output and typically do not cause inflation. In the short run, SRAS is relatively flat because wages and prices are sticky, so changes in aggregate demand or supply mainly show up in output and employment.

Example: An oil price spike (adverse shock) shifts SRAS left: real GDP falls, unemployment rises, and the price level rises. A technology breakthrough (favourable shock) shifts SRAS right: real GDP rises, unemployment falls, and the price level stays stable or moves lower.

err-adjustment-mechanism

The student cannot explain why higher output above potential leads to rising costs and a leftward shift of SRAS. They may think output can stay above potential indefinitely or that wages and prices do not adjust.

Explain: In the short run, the SRAS curve is relatively flat because wages and prices are sticky. If aggregate demand rises enough to push output above potential GDP, the economy overheats. At output above potential, there is intense competition for labour and raw materials. This competition drives up wages and input prices, which raises production costs. Higher production costs shift the SRAS curve upward (to the left). As SRAS shifts up, the price level rises and real GDP falls back toward potential. Long-run equilibrium is restored at full employment. The LRAS curve is vertical at the full-employment level of real GDP and is determined only by capital, labour, and technology, not by the price level.

Example: Suppose an increase in government spending shifts AD right, pushing real GDP above potential. In the short run, because wages are sticky, firms hire more workers and output rises. But with unemployment very low, firms compete for workers and bid up wages. Higher wages raise production costs, shifting SRAS upward/left. The price level rises while output falls back to potential. This adjustment can be seen in three steps: (1) output above potential, (2) rising wages and costs, (3) SRAS shifts up until output returns to potential.
