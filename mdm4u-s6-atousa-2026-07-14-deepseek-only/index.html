Q1. Which of the following best describes selecting a president, vice-president, and secretary from a club of 12 members?

errorId: err-perm-vs-comb | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Solve problems involving the application of permutations and combinations to det

Official: A2 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student thinks that any selection of a group is a combination, ignoring the distinct roles (order).

Remediation → err-perm-vs-comb | Retest → err-perm-vs-comb

  1. ✓ A. It is a permutation because the positions are distinct.
  2. B. It is a combination because it is a selection from a larger group.
    Order matters here because president, VP, and secretary are different roles.
  3. C. It is neither a permutation nor a combination.
    This is a standard permutation problem.
  4. D. It is a combination because the order of selection does not affect the outcome.
    The order affects who gets which role, so order matters.

Q2. Simplify: 10! / 7!

errorId: err-factorial-simplify | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: apply | OSSD: Solve problems involving the application of permutations and combinations to det

Official: A2 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student subtracts denominators or multiplies all factors without canceling.

Remediation → err-factorial-simplify | Retest → err-factorial-simplify

  1. ✓ A. 720
  2. B. 5040
    You may have computed 7! instead of simplifying. Cancel the common terms: 10!/7! = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720.
  3. C. 120
    Check your multiplication: 10×9×8 = 720, not 120.
  4. D. 3600
    You may have multiplied 10×9×8×7? Remember to only keep the factors from 10 down to 8.

Q3. How many distinct arrangements can be made using all the letters in the word 'BANANA'?

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: apply | OSSD: Solve problems involving the application of permutations and combinations to det

Official: A2 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Student forgets to divide by the factorials of repeated letters, simply using 6!.

Remediation → err-distinguishable-forget-divide | Retest → err-distinguishable-forget-divide

  1. A. 720
    You calculated 6! but forgot to account for repeated letters (A appears 3 times, N appears 2 times). Divide by 3!2! to get 60.
  2. B. 120
    Close, but you need to divide by both 3! and 2!. The correct count is 6!/(3!2!) = 60.
  3. ✓ C. 60
  4. D. 30
    You may have divided by too much. The correct formula is 6!/(3!2!) = 60.

Misconception: Student might use 15×14×13 wrong or use a different formula.

Remediation → err-npr-wrong-multiply | Retest → err-npr-wrong-multiply

  1. ✓ A. 2730
  2. B. 3375
    You may have calculated 15^3 = 3375. Remember, nPr is n×(n-1)×...×(n-r+1), not n^r.

Q5. A password is made of 3 digits, each chosen from 0 to 9. Digits may be repeated. Which expression correctly gives the number of possible passwords?

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Solve problems involving the application of permutations and combinations to det

Official: A2 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-5

Misconception: Student applies the permutation formula P(10,3) even though repetition is allowed.

Remediation → err-replacement-assume-perm | Retest → err-replacement-assume-perm

  1. ✓ A. 10^3
  2. B. P(10,3)
    P(10,3) is for permutations without replacement. Since digits can repeat, use the multiplication rule: 10 choices each time, so 10^3.
  3. C. 10!
    10! counts ways to arrange all 10 digits, not relevant here.
  4. D. 3^10
    This would be if you had 3 choices for each of 10 positions, but it's 10 choices for each of 3 positions.

补救模块

err-perm-vs-comb

Thinks that any selection of a group is a combination, ignoring the distinct roles (order).

Explain: Permutations count ordered arrangements; combinations count unordered groups. If order matters (e.g., different roles, ranking), use permutations. If order doesn't matter (e.g., just selecting a team), use combinations.

Example: For 3 distinct objects A, B, C: permutations (arrangements) = 3! = 6 (ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA). Combinations (groups) = 1 (just {A,B,C}).

err-factorial-simplify

Incorrectly simplifying factorial divisions, e.g., not canceling overlapping terms.

Explain: Factorials represent the product of all positive integers from 1 to n. When dividing factorials, you can cancel the common factorial chain. For example, 6!/4! = (6×5×4×3×2×1)/(4×3×2×1) = 6×5 = 30. You only multiply the numbers from the larger factorial that are not cancelled by the smaller factorial.

Example: Simplify 10!/7!. Write 10! = 10×9×8×7×6×... and 7! = 7×6×... Cancel 7! from 10! to leave 10×9×8 = 720.

err-distinguishable-forget-divide

The student counts arrangements of letters or objects with repeated items as if all items are distinct, using n! without dividing by the factorials of the identical items.

Explain: When some objects are identical, simply using n! overcounts because swapping identical objects does not create a new arrangement. To correct, divide n! by the factorial of each group of identical objects.

Example: Consider the word 'BANANA'. There are 6 letters: B (1), A (3), N (2). Number of distinct arrangements = 6!/(3!2!) = 720/(6×2) = 720/12 = 60.

err-npr-wrong-multiply

The student may have used n^r (15^3=3375) or the combination formula (C(15,3)=455) instead of the correct nPr multiplication: starting at 15 and multiplying three descending factors (15×14×13=2730).

Explain: The nPr formula counts ordered selections without replacement. For P(n,r), multiply r factors starting from n and decreasing by 1 each time: n×(n-1)×...×(n-r+1). Do not use n^r (which allows repetition) or combination formula nCr.

Example: P(15,3) = 15×14×13 = 2730.

err-replacement-assume-perm

The student thinks that permutation applies to any ordered selection, but fails to recognize that permutation is only for selection without replacement. When repetition is allowed, each selection is independent and the multiplication principle gives n^r.

Explain: Permutations count ordered arrangements without repetition. If repetition is allowed (e.g., passwords where digits can repeat), each position has n choices, so total = n^r. Do not use P(n,r).

Example: How many 3-digit codes using digits 0 and 1 with repetition? Each digit has 2 choices, so 2^3 = 8.
