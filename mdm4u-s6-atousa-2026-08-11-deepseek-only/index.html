Q1. A news article shows a bar graph where the y-axis starts at 34 and increases by 2, while the actual data values range from 34 to 50. Why might this graph mislead viewers?

errorId: err-misleading-truncated-axis | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of the applications of data management used by the

Official: D3 | Node: MDM4U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Assuming any graph with a non‑zero start is automatically misleading without context.

Remediation → err-misleading-truncated-axis | Retest → err-misleading-truncated-axis

  1. ✓ A. It exaggerates differences because the baseline is not zero, making small changes appear large.
    Correct. A truncated axis can visually magnify minor differences.
  2. B. It is not misleading because the y-axis is clearly labeled.
    Even with labels, starting the axis far from zero misrepresents the relative size of differences.
  3. C. The data values are too small to be shown on a graph.
    The data values are not too small; the issue is the axis range being cut off.
  4. D. It shows a perfect positive correlation.
    This has nothing to do with correlation; the problem is the axis scaling.

Q2. A company conducts an employee aptitude vs. productivity study using only 3 employees and finds a very high positive correlation. They conclude that aptitude strongly predicts productivity for all employees. What is the main flaw in this conclusion?

errorId: err-misleading-sample-size | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Application | Action: evaluate | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of the applications of data management used by the

Official: D3 | Node: MDM4U-D-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Believing a strong correlation from a tiny sample is reliable.

Remediation → err-misleading-sample-size | Retest → err-misleading-sample-size

  1. ✓ A. The sample size is too small, making the correlation unreliable and possibly spurious.
    Correct. A sample of only 3 does not provide reliable evidence for a general claim.
  2. B. The correlation coefficient must be negative.
    There is no rule that aptitude and productivity have a negative correlation; the flaw is the tiny sample.
  3. C. Aptitude and productivity cannot be measured.
    They can be measured; the issue is the sampling, not measurement.
  4. D. The company should have used a bar graph.
    A bar graph would not fix the underlying problem of an insufficient sample size.

Q3. For a bivariate dataset, the correlation coefficient r = 0.95. What does the value r² = 0.9025 tell us?

errorId: err-regression-r-vs-r2 | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Analyse, interpret, and draw conclusions from two-variable data using numerical,

Official: D2 | Node: MDM4U-D-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Confusing r with r², thinking r² measures correlation strength.

Remediation → err-regression-r-vs-r2 | Retest → err-regression-r-vs-r2

  1. ✓ A. It is the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable.
    Correct. r² indicates that about 90% of the variability is accounted for by the linear model.
  2. B. It is the strength of the correlation, indicating a very strong relationship.
    r² is not the strength; r measures the strength. r² is the explained variance.
  3. C. It tells us the slope of the regression line is 0.9025.
    The slope (b) is a separate parameter, not equal to r².
  4. D. It is the p-value for the correlation test.
    A p-value would be used for hypothesis testing, not r².

Q4. In the formula for the slope of the least-squares regression line, b = [nΣxy - ΣxΣy] / [nΣx² - (Σx)²], a student mistakenly uses Σx instead of Σx² in the denominator. Explain how this error would affect the calculated slope and why it is essential to use Σx².

errorId: err-regression-calculation | Bloom: analyze | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Thinking | Action: analyze | OSSD: Analyse, interpret, and draw conclusions from two-variable data using numerical,

Official: D2 | Node: MDM4U-D-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Mishandling summation pre‑computations, such as forgetting to square x before summing.

Remediation → err-regression-calculation | Retest → err-regression-calculation

    Rubric: Answer should explain that using Σx instead of Σx² changes the denominator dramatically (it would not match the sum of squares) and therefore yields a slope that does not minimize the sum of squared residuals. The correct denominator captures the variance of the independent variable.

    Sample: Using Σx instead of Σx² would produce an incorrect denominator because Σx² is the sum of squared x‑values, which measures the spread of x. Without squaring, the denominator no longer reflects the total variation in x, resulting in a slope that does not fit the data properly. This violates the least‑squares criterion.

    补救模块

    err-misleading-truncated-axis

    Assuming any graph with a non-zero starting y-axis is automatically misleading, without considering whether the context justifies the cut and labels are clear.

    Explain: A truncated y-axis begins at a value other than zero and can exaggerate the visual difference between data points. For example, a bar graph showing percentages from 34% to 50% on a scale that starts at 34 makes the difference from 34% to 36% look huge, even though it is only a 2-point change. However, a truncated axis is not always misleading if the cut is clearly labeled, the context explains why the axis was cut, and the reader understands that the visual scale begins at the cut. The problem arises when the truncation is hidden or used to make small differences appear large.

    Example: In a Fox News graph, the y-axis was truncated starting at 34% instead of 0%, which exaggerated the visual difference in support for renewing tax cuts. The actual data values were close, but the graph made the difference look much larger than it really was.

    err-misleading-sample-size

    The student believes that a strong correlation from a very small sample is reliable evidence for a general trend, ignoring that small samples can produce spurious patterns that often disappear with complete data.

    Explain: Sampling flaws such as very small sample sizes or selective sampling can produce spurious correlations or inflated positive metrics that vanish with complete data. A high correlation from a tiny sample is not reliable because it may be due to chance and not represent the broader population.

    Example: In the lesson, a 3-person systematic sample falsely indicated a strong aptitude–productivity correlation; when the full data were considered, the correlation disappeared. This shows how a tiny sample can invent a pattern that is not real.

    err-regression-r-vs-r2

    The student confuses the correlation coefficient r with the coefficient of determination r², believing that r² describes the strength or direction of the linear relationship rather than the proportion of variance in the dependent variable explained by the independent variable.

    Explain: The correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship, from -1 to +1. The coefficient of determination r² is r squared and tells us the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that is explained by the independent variable in the linear regression model. r² is always between 0 and 1, and when expressed as a percentage, it describes explanatory power. For example, r = 0.9486 indicates a strong positive linear correlation. Its square is r² = 0.8998, which means that 89.98% of the variance in the dependent variable is explained by the independent variable. Do not say r² is the strength of the correlation; strength is judged by |r|.

    Example: For the Great Sand Dunes wind velocity vs. sand drift rate data, the regression line was y = -79.92 + 1.07x. We found r = 0.9486 and r² = 0.8998. The r value close to +1 tells us that the linear relationship is strong and positive. The r² value of 0.8998 tells us that wind velocity explains 89.98% of the variance in sand drift rate. The other 10.02% of the variance is due to other factors or random variation.

    err-regression-calculation

    I can just add up the x-values and use that total in the denominator; I do not need to square each x before summing.

    Explain: Manual linear regression requires computing the sums of x, y, x², y², and xy. The slope is b = (nΣxy − ΣxΣy) / (nΣx² − (Σx)²), and the y-intercept is a = (Σy − bΣx)/n. The key is that Σx² means you square every x-value first, then add those squares. It is not the same as (Σx)², which is the total of x-values squared after adding. A common mistake is to forget the squaring step before summing, which makes the denominator too small and the slope incorrect.

    Example: For the Great Sand Dunes wind velocity vs. sand drift dataset, the correct regression line was y = −79.92 + 1.07x. That slope and intercept were obtained by correctly summing all x, y, x², y², and xy values. If a student mistakenly used Σx rather than Σx² in the denominator, the slope would not come out to 1.07 and the line would not minimize the squared residuals. Once the calculations are correct, r = 0.9486 and r² = 0.8998, meaning 89.98% of the variance in drift rate is explained by wind velocity.
