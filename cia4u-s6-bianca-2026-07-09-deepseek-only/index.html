        补救模块

        err-substituion-income-confusion

        Students confuse the substitution effect and income effect, thinking they are the same or swapping their definitions.

        Explain: When the price of a good rises, two effects reduce the quantity demanded. The substitution effect: consumers switch to substitute goods because the original good is now relatively more expensive. The income effect: the price rise reduces consumers' real income (purchasing power), so they buy less of the good. Both effects together cause the demand curve to slope downward.

        Example: Suppose the price of coffee rises. The substitution effect leads consumers to buy tea instead (a substitute). Simultaneously, the income effect means consumers feel poorer, so they buy less coffee overall. Both effects explain why quantity demanded falls.

        err-demand-curve-slope

        Students may mistakenly think the demand curve slopes upward (positive relationship between price and quantity demanded).

        Explain: The law of demand states an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded: as price rises, quantity demanded falls; as price falls, quantity demanded rises. This is why the demand curve slopes downward from left to right. Two key reasons are the substitution effect (consumers switch to substitutes when price rises) and the income effect (higher price reduces purchasing power, so consumers buy less). Together, these effects ensure that higher prices lead to lower quantity demanded.

        Example: Consider strawberries. If the price of strawberries rises, consumers may buy more apples (substitution effect) and also feel poorer (income effect), so they buy fewer strawberries overall. Thus, the quantity demanded decreases as price increases.

        err-supply-curve-slope

        The student believes that as price increases, quantity supplied decreases, possibly confusing supply with demand or misunderstanding producer incentives.

        Explain: The law of supply states that, ceteris paribus, there is a positive relationship between price and quantity supplied. When the price of a good rises, producers are willing to supply more of it because they can earn higher revenue. This is why the supply curve slopes upward from left to right. In contrast, a downward-sloping curve would imply that producers supply less when price increases, which contradicts profit-seeking behavior.

        Example: Consider the market for gasoline. If the price of gasoline rises, oil companies have an incentive to drill more and refine more gasoline to sell at the higher price. So the quantity supplied increases. Similarly, if the price falls, they reduce production. This positive relationship is the law of supply.

        err-equilibrium-mechanism

        Students may think that prices are set by sellers rather than determined by supply and demand interaction.

        Explain: Market equilibrium price is determined by the intersection of supply and demand curves. Neither sellers nor buyers alone set the price. When the market price is above equilibrium, there is excess supply (surplus), and sellers lower prices. When the market price is below equilibrium, there is excess demand (shortage), and buyers bid up prices. The price adjusts until quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.

        Example: Consider the market for strawberries. If sellers set the price at $5 per pint when equilibrium is $3, a surplus occurs. Sellers reduce the price to $3, where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded. Buyers alone cannot set a lower price because sellers would not supply enough. The equilibrium price emerges from the interaction of both sides.

        err-shift-vs-move-along

        Students confuse a shift of the supply or demand curve (caused by non-price factors) with a movement along the curve (caused by a change in the good's own price).

        Explain: A movement along a demand or supply curve occurs only when the good's own price changes. It represents a change in quantity demanded or quantity supplied. A shift of the entire curve occurs when a factor other than price changes (e.g., technology, consumer preferences, input costs). A shift means at every price, a different quantity is demanded or supplied.

        Example: Consumer income increases: this shifts the demand curve for strawberries to the right (shift). In contrast, if the price of strawberries falls, consumers move down along the existing demand curve to a higher quantity demanded (movement).

        err-excess-supply-demand-direction

        Students often reverse the conditions: they think a price above equilibrium leads to a shortage and a price below leads to a surplus. Actually, above equilibrium causes a surplus, below causes a shortage.

        Explain: Excess supply (surplus) occurs when the market price is above the equilibrium price. At this price, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, causing a surplus. Sellers respond by lowering prices to attract buyers, moving the market toward equilibrium. Excess demand (shortage) occurs when the market price is below equilibrium. Here, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating a shortage. Buyers bid up the price, moving the market toward equilibrium.

        Example: Imagine a market for strawberries. If the price is set at $5 per basket, but the equilibrium is $4, then at $5, farmers supply more than consumers want. That's a surplus. If the price is $3, consumers demand more than farmers supply, creating a shortage.
