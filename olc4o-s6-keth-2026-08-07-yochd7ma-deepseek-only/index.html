补救模块

err-logos-misclassifying

The student assumes that any mention of a credible expert or organization is a logical appeal, but logos requires verifiable facts, statistics, or causal reasoning—not just authority.

Explain: Logos is the appeal to logic through facts, statistics, and causal reasoning. Ethos is the appeal to credibility through authority, certification, or endorsement. When an ad says 'Recommended by the Canadian Dental Association,' it is using the organization's credibility to persuade you—that's ethos, not logos. To be logos, the ad must give verifiable data, like 'reduces plaque by 85%.' Always ask: is this a fact I can check, or is this a person/group I trust?

Example: Toothpaste ad: 'Recommended by the Canadian Dental Association' = ethos. The same ad claiming 'reduces plaque by 85%' = logos. The car ad showing braking distance data = logos; the safety certification badge = ethos.

err-pathos-overgeneralizing

The student believes any human presence or personal story automatically counts as pathos, ignoring the requirement that pathos deliberately evokes emotions like joy, empathy, or anxiety.

Explain: Pathos is one of the three appeals in Aristotle's rhetorical triangle. It works by evoking emotions such as joy, empathy, or anxiety. Not every image of a person is pathos. To count as pathos, the creator must intentionally try to make the audience feel an emotion. For example, a car ad that shows family travel footage is using pathos to create warmth, but the same ad also uses braking-distance data (logos) and a safety certification (ethos). If you see an authority figure giving a testimonial, that is ethos because it builds trust through credibility, not pathos, unless a specific emotion is being deliberately triggered. Audiences reject content if they sense forced emotional manipulation, so real pathos must be both intentional and subtle.

Example: A toothpaste ad claiming 85% plaque reduction is logos (a statistic). A safety certification in a car ad is ethos (authority). A family laughing together in a car ad is pathos only because it is designed to trigger joy. If an ad simply has a person standing there with no emotional pull, it is not pathos.

err-ethos-confusion

Students believe ethos is a standalone appeal, not recognizing that credibility is constructed through logical qualifications (logos) and emotional character (pathos).

Explain: Ethos is not isolated; it gains strength by integrating logical evidence (logos) and emotional connection (pathos). For example, in the car ad, the safety certification (ethos) is supported by braking distance data (logos) and family travel footage (pathos). The certification alone may be dismissed, but when combined, it builds genuine trust. Audiences reject forced manipulation, so effective ethos must feel authentic and earned through both facts and feeling.

Example: Car ad: safety certification badges (ethos) rely on braking distance data (logos) and family footage (pathos) to create trust.

err-synthesis-oversimplifying

The student assumes an advertisement uses only one rhetorical appeal and does not recognize that effective persuasion often synthesizes multiple appeals (logos, pathos, and ethos).

Explain: Persuasive texts, especially advertisements, rarely rely on a single appeal. Aristotle's rhetorical triangle shows that logos (facts/statistics), pathos (emotion), and ethos (credibility) can all work together in one message. For example, a car ad might show a family having fun (pathos), include braking distance data (logos), and display a safety certification (ethos). When you analyze an ad, ask which appeals are present, not just one.

Example: In the lesson's car ad: family travel footage appeals to pathos, braking distance data appeals to logos, and the safety certification appeals to ethos. All three are combined in the same advertisement.
