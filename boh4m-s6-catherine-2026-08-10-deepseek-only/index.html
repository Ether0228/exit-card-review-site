补救模块

err-mission-vision-confusion

The student confuses a mission statement (what the organization does now, including its current business, purpose, and operations) with a vision statement (the organization's long-term direction and future aspirations).

Explain: A mission statement describes an organization's actual business, including what it does, how it operates, its purpose, target audience, service scope, and operation channels. A vision statement defines the long-term direction and future aspirations of an organization; it should be concise, future-oriented, stable, challenging, and avoid generic language. The key distinction: mission focuses on present business, vision on future goals. In our lesson, we examined mission statements from Pepsi, Big Brothers Sisters of Canada, Tesla, LinkedIn, and PayPal – these all describe what those companies do today. We also examined vision statements from Microsoft, IBM, and Nike – these all describe where those companies aspire to be in the future.

Example: Consider a company that makes and sells bicycles. Its mission statement might say: 'We manufacture and sell quality bicycles to commuters through our local dealers.' This describes the present business: what they do, for whom, and how. Its vision statement might say: 'To create a future where every city resident uses a bicycle for daily transport.' This describes a desired future state. The mission is anchored in today's operations; the vision points towards tomorrow's goal.

err-mission-vs-values

You may think value statements are the same as mission statements because both seem to describe what the organization is about. Actually, values define principles and behavioral norms, not the day-to-day business operations.

Explain: A mission statement describes an organization's actual business: purpose, target audience, service scope, and operation channels. A value statement articulates core principles and philosophical concepts that guide internal decisions and external value orientation. Mission = what we do now; values = how we behave and decide. They are distinct tools in strategic planning.

Example: Take Tesla’s mission statement: it focuses on accelerating sustainable energy through electric vehicles (what it does). A value statement—like those used by Disney or Airbnb—names principles such as creativity, belonging, or integrity that guide behavior. The mission tells the business; values tell the beliefs behind it.

err-vision-generic

The student believes a vision statement only needs to be ambitious, so they write generic phrases like 'to be the best' or 'to be a global leader' without identifying the organization's unique future direction or path.

Explain: A vision statement defines the long-term direction and future aspirations of an organization. It should be concise, future-oriented, stable, challenging, and avoid generic language. Generic visions give no clear guide for strategic decisions and could apply to almost any organization. A strong vision names the specific future state the organization wants to create and the unique direction it will take to get there.

Example: Compare a generic vision, 'We aim to be the most successful company,' with the lesson's examples such as the Microsoft, IBM, or Nike vision statements. Each of those names a concrete future condition tied to the organization's own industry and identity. The generic version does not. It could apply to any company and gives no guidance for decision-making or strategy.

err-mission-components

A mission statement is incomplete if it only lists what an organization makes or does. It must also state who it serves, why it exists, how it operates, where it operates, and through which channels it delivers.

Explain: According to the lesson definition, a mission statement describes an organization's actual business, including what it does, how it operates, its purpose, target audience, service scope, and operation channels. That means a complete mission must answer all of these: What do we offer? Who is it for? Why do we do it? How does it work? Where is it available? Through which channels do we deliver? If any of these is missing, the statement is incomplete and does not fully describe the business.

Example: Complete example: 'We provide affordable, eco‑friendly bicycles to urban commuters, operating through local retail stores and online direct sales, to promote healthy and sustainable transportation.' This includes the product (bicycles), audience (urban commuters), how (local stores and online sales), why (promote sustainability), scope (urban), and channels (retail and online).
