Q1. Which of the following best describes motivation?

errorId: err-motivation-force | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Compare theories of how to motivate individuals and teams in a productive work e

Official: C3 | Node: BOH4M-C-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/business1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: Define motivation and explain its internal nature.

Misconception: Thinking motivation can be imposed by managers.

Remediation → err-motivation-force | Retest → err-motivation-force

  1. A. It is an external force that managers can impose on employees to increase productivity.
    Incorrect. Motivation is an internal state; it cannot be imposed externally. Even the best manager can only encourage it, not create it.
  2. ✓ B. It is an internal driving force that determines the level, direction, and persistence of effort.
    Correct. Motivation comes from within and shapes how hard, in what direction, and for how long a person works toward a goal.
  3. C. It is the same as ability; people are motivated when they have the skills to do a job.
    Incorrect. Motivation and ability are distinct; someone can have skills but lack the drive to use them.
  4. D. It is a temporary emotional state that fluctuates daily and has no lasting impact on performance.
    Incorrect. Motivation can be sustained and is a key predictor of long-term performance.

补救模块

err-motivation-power

The student thinks extrinsic motivation is more powerful than intrinsic because external rewards are visible and tangible. In reality, intrinsic motivation—coming from personal satisfaction and active initiative—is the most powerful form of motivation.

Explain: Motivation is the invisible driving force that determines the level, direction, and persistence of effort. There are two key types: intrinsic motivation comes from within (personal satisfaction, active initiative) and is the most powerful; extrinsic motivation comes from external factors such as material rewards, peer pressure, or evaluations. Because intrinsic motivation is self-generated, it lasts longer and drives effort even when external rewards are absent.

Example: Consider a caregiver who works because they genuinely love helping others (intrinsic) versus one who works only for a paycheck (extrinsic). When the paycheck is reduced or removed, the extrinsic motivation drops sharply, but the intrinsic caregiver keeps going because the satisfaction comes from the work itself.

err-motivation-force

The student believes motivation is something outside a person that can be directly given or forced by another person, such as a manager threatening or commanding. In reality, motivation is an internal state that each person must generate for themselves; others can only create supportive conditions or incentives.

Explain: Motivation is the invisible driving force inside a person that determines the level, direction, and persistence of effort they put into work, study, or daily life. Because it is internal, it cannot be imposed from the outside. A manager, teacher, or parent can offer extrinsic rewards or create a positive environment, but the actual desire to act must come from within the individual. Intrinsic motivation, which comes from personal satisfaction and active initiative, is the most powerful form of motivation. This is why two employees in the same workplace with the same pay can have very different levels of motivation—one may be driven by genuine interest or purpose, while the other is just going through the motions.

Example: In a company, a manager cannot make a caregiver feel motivated by simply ordering them to work harder. The caregiver might be encouraged by extra vacation days because that addresses a personal need, but the drive to complete tasks still comes from inside them. Similarly, an employee with a mortgage may respond well to a salary increase, but that pay raise is just an external condition; it does not replace the internal motivation needed to sustain effort over time.

err-factors-performance

Performance is caused by a single factor (like ability or salary) or by factors outside the individual's control; the student does not recognize that motivation, ability, and environment interact together to determine performance.

Explain: Individual performance is shaped by three interacting factors: motivation (internal desire), ability (current skills, improvable through training), and external environment (social circles, tools, information, support). All three work together. If any one of them is weak, performance suffers. For example, a highly motivated and skilled employee may still underperform if the workspace lacks the necessary tools or support; conversely, a great environment cannot compensate for lack of motivation or ability.

Example: A skilled and highly motivated employee (high ability + high motivation) may still underperform if the workplace lacks necessary tools, information, or support (weak environment). Conversely, even with a great environment, a person without skills or motivation cannot perform well.

err-sources-biological-vs-social

The student confuses the four sources of motivation, especially biological (basic needs like food) with social (media, culture, peers), and may overlook cognitive sources such as interests and career goals.

Explain: Motivation flows from four primary sources, which are distinct yet sometimes overlapping. Biological sources come from basic survival needs like food and water. Emotional sources include feelings like fear, anger, love, and hate. Cognitive sources arise from your thoughts—interests, self-worth, and career goals. Social sources come from the people around you, media, culture, and religion. To avoid mixing them up, remember BECS: Biological, Emotional, Cognitive, Social.

Example: Feeling hunger while studying is biological motivation; scrolling social media to see what friends are doing influences you socially; planning a career in accounting is cognitive; being motivated by love for your family is emotional.

err-application-individualization

Assuming a one-size-fits-all reward strategy, neglecting that employees have different personal needs, job security priorities, and cultural backgrounds that affect motivation.

Explain: Effective motivation is not one-size-fits-all. In the workplace, employees have different personal circumstances and cultural backgrounds, so a single reward, like money, will not motivate everyone equally. Managers must tailor rewards to each person's needs. In the current economic downturn, employees often care more about job security than pay raises. For example, a caregiver may value extra vacation days, while an employee with a mortgage may be more motivated by a salary increase. Also, motivation strategies must respect cross-cultural differences.

Example: A company has two employees: a caregiver who needs more time at home and a homeowner paying a mortgage. The caregiver may be motivated by extra vacation days, while the homeowner may respond better to a salary increase. A manager should adapt rewards to each person's situation and cultural context.
