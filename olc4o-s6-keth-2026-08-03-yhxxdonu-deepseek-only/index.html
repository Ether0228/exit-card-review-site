Q1. Which statement best describes reflective writing in this course?

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Confusing reflective writing with an event log or diary because both use first person.

Remediation → err-reflective-narrative-confusion | Retest → err-reflective-narrative-confusion

  1. A. A diary entry that records what happened during an event
    A diary entry can be chronological and non-analytic. Reflective writing requires you to look inside the experience and draw learning from it.
  2. ✓ B. A structured piece that connects a lived experience to learning and uses first-person internal analysis
    Correct. Reflective writing is first-person and analytic; it turns an experience into a learning outcome.
  3. C. An argumentative essay that proves a point with outside evidence
    Argumentative essays defend a thesis with evidence. Reflective writing is personal and internal, not built around external proof.
  4. D. A news report that summarizes facts about a community event
    News reports are objective and third-person. Reflective writing centers the writer's own interpretation and growth.

补救模块

err-reflective-narrative-confusion

Reflective writing is the same as a personal narrative or diary entry: just retell what happened using 'I' and no analysis of the experience is needed.

Explain: Reflective writing is a structured tool for connecting lived experience to learning. It uses first-person pronouns, but more importantly it requires internal analysis. A simple narrative lists events; a reflection examines those events to draw out meaning, using stages like description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan. In the Gibbs Reflective Cycle, analysis is the non-negotiable core. Without analysis, the writing stays a diary entry, not a reflection.

Example: A diary entry might say: 'I did the interview and felt nervous.' A reflection would say: 'I did the interview and felt nervous. I analyzed that nervousness and realized it came from being unprepared, so my action plan is to prepare more questions next time.' The second version connects experience to learning.

err-gibbs-omit-analysis

The student believes the Gibbs Reflective Cycle is complete after describing events and naming feelings, and treats analysis as optional or less important than the earlier stages.

Explain: The Gibbs Reflective Cycle has six stages: description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, and action plan. Analysis is the non-negotiable core. This is the stage where you ask why the experience unfolded the way it did, identify patterns, and connect the experience to your learning. Without analysis, the writing is only a diary entry, not a reflection.

Example: Imagine you write: 'I described the group presentation and I felt nervous.' That is description and feelings. Analysis would then ask: 'Why did I feel nervous? What assumptions did I bring into the group? What did the group's communication pattern reveal about my own habits?' Answering those questions is what turns the event into a learning experience.

err-interview-biography-instead-of-story

Treating the Unit 2 Interview Assignment as a full chronological biography of the interviewee instead of focusing on one unique, interesting personal story. This leads to a summary of the whole life rather than a narrative built around a single lived incident that reveals character or insight.

Explain: The interview assignment requires you to identify one unique personal story, not a full biography. A biography covers the entire life span with general facts (birth, education, career), while a story zooms in on a single turning point or event. The assignment asks for a 400-word interview narrative that uses pre-planned W5H questions and spontaneous follow-ups to draw out that one incident. If your final piece contains only biographical content without a unique story, it triggers an automatic zero. Think of it as capturing the most memorable 'chapter' or 'turning point' rather than listing the whole book of someone's life.

Example: A biography would say: 'Mr. Kim was born in Seoul, moved to Toronto in 1995, worked as an engineer, and retired in 2020.' A story would say: 'On the afternoon of March 12, 2011, Mr. Kim's apartment building shook as the earthquake hit. He grabbed his neighbour's toddler and ran down five flights of stairs just before the exit collapsed. He later said that was the moment he understood what courage really meant.' The story is focused, specific, and reveals a single meaningful experience.
