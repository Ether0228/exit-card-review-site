    补救模块

    err-topic-mainidea-confusion

    The student equates topic with main idea, believing any overarching phrase that names a subject is enough to be a main idea, and does not recognize that a main idea must be a debatable claim needing evidence.

    Explain: A topic is broad, unarguable subject. A main idea is focused, debatable assertion needing evidence. In academic texts, the main idea is the claim that organizes the paragraph; supporting evidence backs it up. Deductive paragraphs begin with a clear topic sentence that states the main idea, then add supporting sentences with details, and end with a synthesis. Signal words help: 'primarily' often introduces a main idea, while 'according to' introduces evidence.

    Example: Topic: 'Climate change' (broad, cannot argue). Main idea: 'Climate change is the defining challenge of this decade' (debatable assertion requiring evidence). Supporting evidence would then provide verifiable dates, statistics, or expert citations—but the main idea itself is the claim.

    err-macro-micro-confusion

    The student thinks any statement that sounds factual counts as micro evidence, without checking whether it contains a verifiable date, statistic, or authoritative citation. They treat macro claims and micro evidence as interchangeable.

    Explain: Macro claims are broad, source-free generalizations; they often state the main idea. Micro evidence is verifiable and includes dates, statistics, and authoritative citations. Use signal words as clues: 'primarily' signals a main idea, while 'according to' signals supporting evidence. When reading or writing, ask: Can I verify this with a specific source, number, or date? If yes, it is micro; if not, it is macro.

    Example: Macro claim: 'Primarily, remote work increases productivity.' Micro evidence: 'According to Smith (2020), 90% of remote workers reported higher productivity.' The first is the argument; the second is the proof.

    err-paragraph-structure-incomplete

    Students believe that providing supporting evidence is enough and that a closing synthesis sentence is optional or simply a restatement. They do not realize it must explicitly connect the evidence back to the main idea.

    Explain: A deductive paragraph has three required parts: a topic sentence (the main idea or claim), supporting sentences (evidence and details), and a closing synthesis sentence. The synthesis sentence is crucial because it ties the evidence back to the main idea, demonstrating how the proof supports the claim. It is not an optional summary; it is the logical glue that shows the reader why the evidence matters. Think of it as the 'therefore' that connects your proof to your promise.

    Example: Consider the start of a paragraph: 'Remote work increases productivity due to fewer distractions. A 2022 Stanford study showed a 13% performance increase for telecommuters.' Without a synthesis, the paragraph feels unfinished. A correct closing synthesis could be: 'This 13% increase suggests that reduced distractions in home offices directly boost work output, reinforcing the argument that remote work is more productive.'

    err-evidence-types-wrong

    The student assumes any persuasive sentence can serve as evidence, even if it lacks verifiable data, a credible source, or a concrete real-world example.

    Explain: In academic writing, a claim must be supported by evidence, not just personal feeling. There are three acceptable categories of evidence: (1) empirical measurable data – verifiable statistics, dates, or numbers; (2) authoritative expert opinion – a statement from someone with recognized credentials; (3) descriptive illustrative case studies – a detailed, real-world example. Phrases like 'I think' or 'many people say' are unsupported opinions because they cannot be checked or cited. When you see 'according to' in a sentence, it is a signal that the writer is giving supporting evidence from a source, not stating the main idea.

    Example: Claim: Remote work improves productivity. Weak evidence: 'I feel more focused at home.' Strong evidence: 'According to Dr. Smith, an economist, 90% of remote employees report higher productivity.' The second sentence uses a credible expert and a specific statistic, making it acceptable evidence.

    err-annotation-colors-switched

    The student treats the two-color annotation system as optional or interchangeable, thinking any key sentence can be highlighted in either color. They do not recognize that yellow and green have fixed, standardized roles: yellow = main idea/topic sentence, green = supporting details/citations/statistics.

    Explain: Use the two-color system exactly: yellow means the main claim or topic sentence; green means the evidence that supports it (details, citations, statistics). This makes the structure of a text visible at a glance. Always use these colors consistently, not by personal preference.

    Example: For the sentence 'Primarily, the study suggests...' the word 'primarily' signals a main idea, so highlight it yellow. For the sentence 'According to Smith (2020)...' the phrase 'according to' signals supporting evidence, so highlight it green.

    err-signal-words-misread

    The student thinks any sentence that begins with a phrase like 'according to' expresses the author's main argument, not realizing it introduces cited evidence.

    Explain: Signal words are cues to text structure. 'Primarily' signals a main idea or central claim. 'According to' signals supporting evidence because it attributes information to an outside source. The main idea is the author's own assertion; evidence is what others say or data that supports it.

    Example: In the sentence 'According to Smith (2020), the study found a 20+ increase in productivity,' the phrase 'according to' tells you this is Smith's evidence, not the author's main point. The main idea might be stated as 'Primarily, remote work boosts productivity.'

    err-integrity-minimizing

    Thinking minor plagiarism or AI use is acceptable or undetectable, or that only exact copying is plagiarism.

    Explain: Academic integrity applies to all work, including small borrowed phrases or AI-generated text. Detection is holistic: teachers compare writing consistency, style, and structure to spot sudden shifts in proficiency, AI-hallucinated details, copy-paste spacing, or faked grammar errors. Consequences are severe: zero score, official warnings, and possible expulsion.

    Example: A student copies a sentence from a source and slightly rewrites it, assuming it is too small to matter. The teacher detects it because the sentence suddenly shows a different vocabulary level and style from the rest of the work. This is plagiarism and triggers a zero score.
