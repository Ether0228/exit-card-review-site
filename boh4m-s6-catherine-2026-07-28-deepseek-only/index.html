  1. A. The sender speaks clearly and confidently.

    While noise is a barrier, effective communication requires more than a quiet environment.

    In an all-channel network, everyone can communicate directly with anyone else, no sequence.

补救模块

err-communication-effectiveness-as-sender

Students may think communication is effective if the sender speaks clearly, ignoring receiver understanding and barriers.

Explain: Effective communication occurs when the receiver fully understands the sender's intended meaning. It is a two-way process that involves encoding, channel, decoding, and feedback. Barriers such as language gaps, technical distractions, cultural differences, and lack of feedback can prevent successful communication. Simply speaking clearly does not guarantee understanding; the receiver must interpret the message as intended.

Example: A manager tells an employee, 'Please finalize the report by Friday.' The employee hears 'finalize' as 'review and polish,' but the manager meant 'complete all pending work.' Despite clear words, the message was not effective because the receiver's understanding differed from the sender's intent.

err-communication-network-chain-wheel

Students often confuse chain and wheel communication network structures in organizations. They may think a chain network has a central hub like a wheel, or that a wheel network is linear.

Explain: In organizational communication, networks define how information flows. The chain network is a linear sequence where each person communicates only with the next person in line. There is no single leader; information passes step by step. The wheel network has a central person who communicates with all others; all messages go through this hub. Understanding the structure helps predict speed and accuracy of communication.

Example: For a chain network, think of a production line where each worker tells the next one a task. For a wheel network, imagine a team leader who gives instructions to each team member individually.

err-grapevine-negative

Students think grapevine communication is always negative or inaccurate, ignoring its potential utility and accuracy.

Explain: Grapevine refers to informal, casual communication among employees. It can carry rumors but also accurate information and helpful insights. It supplements formal channels, allowing quick information flow and social bonding.

Example: Imagine a team hears through grapevine that a colleague is leaving; this may prompt a farewell gathering. Even if not official, it helps team cohesion. The information might be accurate or not, but it's not inherently negative.

err-written-better-everywhere

Students may assume written communication is always better than oral because it is accurate and traceable, ignoring situations where immediate feedback or quick resolution is needed.

Explain: While written communication provides accuracy and a permanent record, oral communication offers immediate feedback and is easier to use for quick exchanges. The best method depends on the context: for detailed, formal instructions, written is preferred; for urgent matters or when clarification is needed, oral is more effective. Communication richness also matters: face-to-face is richest, while written is lower.

Example: Consider a manager needing to resolve a conflict between two employees. Written email would be slow and lack tone, whereas a face-to-face conversation allows immediate feedback and understanding. Alternatively, a contract must be written to ensure legal accuracy.

err-richness-ordering

Students think email is richer than video call because it is written and detailed, ignoring that richness depends on nonverbal cues and immediate feedback.

Explain: Communication richness refers to the amount of information transmitted through a channel, including verbal and nonverbal cues. The hierarchy from richest to least rich is: face-to-face, video call, telephone, email, public social media. Face-to-face offers tone, body language, and immediate feedback; video adds visuals but less immersive; telephone has tone only; email lacks tone and instant feedback; public social media has minimal feedback.

Example: Catherine framed the school as a mini workplace. Imagine you need to discuss a sensitive issue with a colleague. Face-to-face lets you read reactions; video call next; phone call captures tone; email may be misunderstood; public social media is worst.
