Q1. If you roll a standard 6‑sided die, what is the theoretical probability of rolling a number greater than 4?

errorId: err-theoretical-probability | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: apply | OSSD: Determine the theoretical probability, Pi (i.e., a value from 0 to 1), of each o

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-theoretical

Misconception: Student may incorrectly count favorable outcomes (e.g., only one number greater than 4) or include 4 itself.

Remediation → err-theoretical-probability | Retest → err-theoretical-probability

  1. A. 1/6
    1/6 would be the probability of rolling a single specific number, not numbers greater than 4.
  2. ✓ B. 1/3
    Correct. The numbers greater than 4 are 5 and 6 (2 favorable outcomes out of 6 total), so the probability is 2/6 = 1/3.
  3. C. 1/2
    1/2 would be correct if 3 out of 6 outcomes were favorable, but here only 2 are favorable.
  4. D. 2/3
    2/3 would require 4 favorable outcomes out of 6, but only 2 are favorable.

Q2. The Venn diagram below shows the number of students in three after-school clubs: Sports, Music, and Art. If a student is selected at random, what is the probability that they are in exactly one club?

errorId: err-three-set-venn-probability | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Recognize and describe an event as a set of outcomes and as a subset of a sample

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-venn

Misconception: Student may misinterpret counts (e.g., sum all nonzero regions without distinguishing exact one) or forget to include 'none' region in total.

Remediation → err-three-set-venn-probability | Retest → err-three-set-venn-probability

  1. ✓ A. 20/38
    Correct. Exactly one club includes only Sports (13), only Music (3), only Art (4); total 20 out of 38 students.
  2. B. 13/38
    13/38 accounts only for students in only Sports, ignoring those in only Music and only Art.
  3. C. 17/38
    17/38 might come from adding only Sports and only Art but forgetting only Music.
  4. D. 7/38
    7/38 corresponds to the sum of students in exactly two clubs, not exactly one.

Q3. From a standard 52-card deck, let event A be drawing a heart and event B be drawing a king. Are events A and B mutually exclusive?

errorId: err-mutually-exclusive-events | Bloom: analyze | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Thinking | Action: analyze | OSSD: Recognize and describe an event as a set of outcomes and as a subset of a sample

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-mutually-exclusive

Misconception: Student thinks that because something is a heart and also a king are different categories, they cannot happen together; or that drawing a king always excludes hearts.

Remediation → err-mutually-exclusive-events | Retest → err-mutually-exclusive-events

  1. A. Yes, because P(A∩B)=0
    Actually, the king of hearts belongs to both events, so P(A∩B) = 1/52, not 0.
  2. ✓ B. No, because P(A∩B)=1/52
    Correct. The king of hearts is both a heart and a king, so the intersection is non-empty; events are not mutually exclusive.
  3. C. Yes, because they are different suits
    Different suits do not guarantee mutual exclusivity; a card can be both a heart and a king.
  4. D. No, because P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A∩B) is not zero
    The formula itself is always true; mutual exclusivity is determined by whether P(A∩B)=0, not by the union sum being zero.

Q4. Two-way table of elementary students' goals: | | Popular | Sports | Good Grades | |------------|---------|--------|-------------| | Female | 30 | 30 | 40 | | Male | 20 | 10 | 20 | What is the probability that a student who is female chose Sports?

errorId: err-theoretical-probability | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: apply | OSSD: Determine the theoretical probability, Pi (i.e., a value from 0 to 1), of each o

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-theoretical

Misconception: Student uses total sample space as denominator (150) or uses the intersection of female and sports over total sports (30/40).

Remediation → err-theoretical-probability | Retest → err-theoretical-probability

  1. ✓ A. 30/100
    Correct. There are 100 females total, and 30 of them chose Sports.
  2. B. 30/150
    30/150 would be the probability that a random student is female and chose Sports, not the conditional probability given female.
  3. C. 30/40
    30/40 uses total number of students who chose Sports (40) as denominator; that would be P(female | Sports).
  4. D. 10/50
    10/50 is the probability that a male student chose Sports, not female.

补救模块

err-theoretical-probability

Student fails to correctly identify the number of favorable outcomes and total outcomes in a discrete sample space with equally likely outcomes.

Explain: Theoretical probability is the ratio of the number of favorable outcomes to the total number of equally likely outcomes in a discrete sample space. The formula is: P(event) = (number of favorable outcomes) / (total number of outcomes). Always ensure all outcomes are equally likely and count them correctly.

Example: Roll a standard 6-sided die: probability of rolling a 4 is 1/6 because there is 1 favorable outcome (rolling a 4) out of 6 total possible outcomes.

err-three-set-venn-probability

Students often misinterpret the Venn diagram regions, for example counting students in 'only one set' as including those in intersections, or forgetting to include the 'none' region in the total sample space.

Explain: A three-set Venn diagram divides the sample space into 8 regions: three individual sets (A only, B only, C only), three pairwise intersections (A∩B only, A∩C only, B∩C only), one triple intersection (A∩B∩C), and the complement (none of them). To find the probability of an event, sum the counts in all regions that satisfy the event, then divide by the total number of subjects (including those in none).

Example: Consider a survey of after-school club participation: Sports (S), Music (M), Art (A). Counts: S only 13, M only 3, A only 4, S∩M only 6, S∩A only 4, M∩A only 2, all three 1, none 5. Total = 38. To find the probability a student is in exactly one club, sum the counts for S only (13), M only (3), A only (4) to get 20. So P = 20/38 = 10/19.

err-mutually-exclusive-events

Students often think that events from different categories (e.g., suit vs rank) are automatically mutually exclusive because they are 'different' types, ignoring that a single outcome can belong to both events if it satisfies both conditions. They fail to check the intersection probability.

Explain: Two events are mutually exclusive if they cannot happen at the same time. This means their intersection is empty: P(A∩B)=0. To check, ask: Can both events occur in a single trial? If yes, they are not mutually exclusive.

Example: Drawing a heart and drawing a king from a deck: There is one card that is both (king of hearts), so P(A∩B)=1/52. Since this is not zero, the events are not mutually exclusive.
