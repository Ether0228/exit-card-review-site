Q1. An article explains that a local park was renovated to include a new playground and a community garden. The main purpose is to create a safe space for children and families. Which of the following details from the article should be included in a summary?

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: analyze | OSSD: Use knowledge of writing forms, and of the connections between form, audience, a

Official: B2 | Node: OLC4O-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/english12curr.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student thinks every specific descriptive detail is important.

Remediation → err-summary-irrelevant-details | Retest → err-summary-irrelevant-details

  1. A. The playground has a blue slide and a red climbing frame.
    This is a specific trivial detail that does not support the main idea. Summaries should focus on core functional details.
  2. B. The park renovation cost $2 million.
    Cost is not the core idea here; the main purpose is safety and recreation. Include only explicitly stated core details.
  3. ✓ C. The new playground and community garden provide safe recreational areas for children and families.
    Correct. This captures the core purpose of the renovation.
  4. D. The park is located near the downtown area.
    Location is not the core idea; the article's focus is on the renovation's purpose.

Q2. When writing a summary of a news article, which of the following sentences would be acceptable?

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student thinks including personal opinion is acceptable in summaries.

Remediation → err-summary-personal-opinion | Retest → err-summary-personal-opinion

  1. A. The government's decision to fund the project is a great move.
    This is a personal opinion. Summaries should only present facts from the source, not judgments.

补救模块

err-summary-irrelevant-details

The student believes that every specific descriptive detail from the text is important to include in a summary, even if it is trivial or unrelated to the main idea.

Explain: A summary must include only the explicit core main idea and the supporting details that directly validate it. Trivial details—like a creation year or color of equipment—do not belong because they do not help the reader understand the text's central point. Ask yourself: 'Does this detail help explain the main idea? If I remove it, does the main idea still make sense?' If yes, it may be important; if no, leave it out.

Example: Consider an article about a sculpture that helps preserve sand dunes against rising seas. A correct summary would state the sculpture's core function (preserving dunes). An incorrect summary would mention the year it was created—that detail is interesting but irrelevant to the main idea.

err-summary-personal-opinion

Students think it is acceptable to add their own judgments, feelings, or inferred ideas beyond what the source explicitly states.

Explain: Summaries must be objective and only include information explicitly stated in the source text. Personal opinions (e.g., 'The author makes a great point') and unstated inferences (e.g., assuming the creator is an environmentalist) are prohibited. Use a formal, neutral tone without first-person pronouns.

Example: In class, the correct summary stated 'the sculpture's core function is preserving dunes against rising seas.' The incorrect version said 'the creator must be an environmentalist,' which is an unstated inference.

err-summary-external-connections

The student mistakenly includes connections to other texts or personal background knowledge that are not present in the source text, violating the rule that summaries must only contain information explicitly stated in the original.

Explain: In non-fiction summaries, you must include only information that is directly stated in the source text. Do not add connections to other texts, personal experiences, or outside knowledge. For example, if an article discusses the benefits of urban green spaces, you cannot mention that 'similar parks exist in Japan' unless that fact is in the article. Summaries should be self-contained and objective.

Example: Original text: 'Urban green spaces improve air quality and provide recreational areas.' Incorrect summary addition: 'This is similar to the park near my house.' Correct summary: 'The article states that urban green spaces improve air quality and offer recreation.'

err-summary-failure-to-paraphrase

Students believe that copying the original wording is acceptable, as long as it is accurate. They do not understand that a summary must restate ideas in their own words to demonstrate understanding and avoid plagiarism.

Explain: A summary must be entirely in your own words. Paraphrasing means rephrasing the original idea using different vocabulary and sentence structure while keeping the same meaning. Simply changing a few words or swapping synonyms is not enough; you must restructure the sentence and use your own language.

Example: Source: 'The study concluded that regular exercise reduces the risk of heart disease.' Poor paraphrase (too close): 'The study concluded that regular exercise reduces heart disease risk.' Good paraphrase: 'According to the research, engaging in physical activity on a regular basis lowers the likelihood of developing heart conditions.'

err-summary-first-person-tone

The student uses first-person or second-person pronouns (e.g., I, you, we) in summaries instead of a formal, objective tone.

Explain: Summaries must be written in a formal, objective tone. This means you must avoid using first-person pronouns (I, me, my, we, our) and second-person pronouns (you, your). The focus should be on the source text's information, not your perspective or the reader's. Including 'I think' or 'you can see' makes the summary subjective and informal, which is not allowed. Only use third-person (he, she, it, they) or refer to the source itself.

Example: Incorrect: 'I believe the article shows that the sculpture is important.' Correct: 'The article argues that the sculpture serves a key function.' Incorrect: 'You can see that the park has many benefits.' Correct: 'The park provides several benefits, according to the source.'

err-assignment-ai-translation

Students may think that using AI or translation tools saves time and still produces original work, not realizing the assignment requires a genuine English conversation and that any AI assistance violates academic integrity.

Explain: Unit 2 Assignment 1 requires you to conduct an original interview in English with an English-speaking school community member. The interview must be recorded, and you must ask at least three unplanned follow-up questions based on the W5H framework. Using AI translation tools or AI-generated content goes against the assignment's purpose of developing authentic English communication and writing skills. It is considered academic dishonesty and can result in a score of zero. The interview must be spontaneous and in English, not pre-translated or AI-assisted.

Example: Incorrect: Writing interview questions in Chinese, translating them with Google Translate, and then reading them to the interviewee. Correct: Preparing English questions, conducting the interview in English, and asking unplanned follow-up questions naturally during the conversation.

err-interview-no-recording

Students may think they can rely on memory or notes, or that the recording is optional, but the assignment explicitly requires audio/video recording as proof of an authentic interview. Without it, the assignment cannot be evaluated and receives a zero.

Explain: Recording is mandatory because it provides evidence that the interview was conducted in English with a real person, that you asked follow-up questions, and that no AI or translation was used. A missing recording results in an automatic score of zero, as per the assignment rubric.

Example: A student named Maya completed her interview and wrote a great narrative, but forgot to record. She submitted her work, but the teacher could not verify that the interview happened or that it was in English. Maya received a zero for the assignment.

err-interview-not-in-english

I thought it was okay to switch to Chinese when the interviewee struggled, as long as most of the interview was in English.

Explain: The assignment requires the entire interview to be conducted in English. Using any other language, even partially, violates the assignment rules and results in a score of zero. This is because the task assesses your ability to communicate in English and produce an original narrative based on an English-only interaction.

Example: A student interviewed a school librarian. The first two questions were in English, but when the librarian paused, the student asked the next question in Chinese. The recording showed this language switch, and the student received zero credit for the assignment.

err-interview-no-follow-up-questions

Students think that simply following a script or only asking pre-planned questions is sufficient, or they do not know how to generate follow-up questions that deepen the interview.

Explain: Follow-up questions are spontaneous questions you ask based on what the interviewee just said. They show active listening and help uncover deeper stories. The W5H framework (Who, What, When, Where, Why, How) is a useful tool for generating them. For example, if the interviewee says 'I moved to Canada in 2015,' you could ask 'Why did you decide to move?' or 'How did you feel about the move?' The assignment requires at least 3 unplanned follow-up questions using W5H.

Example: Interviewee: 'I started volunteering at the animal shelter because I love dogs.' Follow-up: 'What kind of dogs do you work with?' or 'How has volunteering changed your perspective?'
