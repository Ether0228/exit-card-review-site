补救模块

err-stress-vs-stressor

Students often think stress and stressors are the same thing or use the terms interchangeably, not recognizing that stress is the psychological/physical response and stressors are the triggers.

Explain: Stress is the tension state you experience when facing extraordinary demands, constraints, or opportunities. Stressors are the specific causes that trigger that stress. For example, an upcoming IELTS exam is a stressor; the anxiety you feel about it is stress. Stressors can come from work, personal life, or non-work scenarios.

Example: Common student stressors include IELTS requirements, homework piles, interpersonal barriers, and parental expectations. The resulting feeling of being overwhelmed is stress.

err-gas-stages-order

Students believe resistance occurs before alarm, or that exhaustion comes before resistance, mixing up the order of the GAS stages.

Explain: The General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) describes how the body responds to stress in three sequential stages: alarm, resistance, exhaustion. First, the body recognizes a stressor and activates the 'fight-or-flight' response (alarm). If stress continues, the body adapts and tries to cope (resistance). Prolonged stress without relief depletes resources, leading to exhaustion and potential health problems.

Example: Imagine a student facing final exams. Initially, they feel anxious and alert (alarm). As they study consistently, their body adapts—they feel more focused and energetic (resistance). If they keep cramming without breaks, eventually they become exhausted, get sick, or feel burnout (exhaustion).

err-all-conflict-bad

The student believes all conflict is harmful and should be avoided, failing to recognize that conflict can be functional (constructive) and improve group performance.

Explain: Not all conflict is negative. Conflict has a dual attribute: functional (constructive) conflict can enhance performance by challenging ideas, encouraging creativity, and improving decision-making. Dysfunctional (destructive) conflict hinders progress. The key is to manage conflict effectively to harness its benefits.

Example: In a team project, two members disagree on the best marketing strategy. They debate ideas rigorously but respectfully, leading to a stronger final plan. This is functional conflict because it improves the outcome. In contrast, if they insult each other, it becomes dysfunctional.

err-collaboration-vs-compromise

Students may think compromise is the best method because it involves give and take, but collaboration aims for a win-win solution with high assertiveness and cooperativeness.

Explain: Collaboration and compromise are both conflict resolution methods, but they differ in assertiveness and cooperativeness. Collaboration is high in both, aiming for a win-win solution where all parties fully satisfy their interests. Compromise is moderate in both, where each party gives up something to reach a middle ground. Collaboration is more effective for complex or important issues.

Example: Two departments disagree on a project deadline. Using collaboration, they work together to find a new deadline that meets both needs. Using compromise, they might split the difference, but neither is fully satisfied.

err-tuckman-always-forward

Assuming that Tuckman's team development stages are always sequential and progressive, and that teams never revert to earlier stages.

Explain: Tuckman's team development model includes five stages: forming, storming, norming, performing, and adjourning. A common misconception is that teams always move forward through these stages in a linear fashion. However, teams can and do revert to earlier stages when facing significant changes, such as resource cuts or personnel changes. For example, if a new member joins a performing team, the team might drop back to storming as they integrate the new person. Understanding this flexibility is key to managing team dynamics effectively.

Example: A project team was performing efficiently. After a key member left and was replaced, the team struggled with roles and communication, effectively regressing to the storming stage. This illustrates that progress is not always forward.
