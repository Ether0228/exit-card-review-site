Q1. Omar rolls a fair die 1000 times and records the number of times he gets a 4. He calculates the experimental probability as 0.17. Which of the following is most accurate?

Official: A1 | Node: MDM4U-A-OE01 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Students may think experimental probability must exactly equal theoretical probability even after many trials.

Remediation → err-prob-theoretical-vs-experimental | Retest → err-prob-theoretical-vs-experimental

  1. A. The experimental probability should be exactly 1/6, so Omar must have made a counting error.
    That's not correct. Even after many rolls, experimental probability is unlikely to be exactly 1/6 due to random variation; it's expected to be close.
  2. ✓ B. The experimental probability of 0.17 is close to the theoretical probability of 1/6, and as the number of rolls increases, it should get even closer.
    Correct! This illustrates the law of large numbers: experimental probability tends to approach theoretical probability with more trials.
  3. C. The experimental probability is always less than the theoretical probability because the die is fair.
    No, experimental probability can be either above or below theoretical; it's not always less.
  4. D. The theoretical probability changes based on the number of rolls.
    Theoretical probability is fixed for a fair die; it does not change with the number of rolls.

    补救模块

    err-prob-theoretical-vs-experimental

    Students think experimental probability must exactly equal theoretical probability, ignoring random variation and the law of large numbers.

    Explain: Theoretical probability is the expected probability calculated from equally likely outcomes. Experimental probability is the observed frequency from actual trials. Due to random variation, experimental probability rarely matches theoretical exactly, especially with few trials. The law of large numbers states that as the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability.

    Example: For a fair die, theoretical P(even) = 1/2 = 0.5. After 20 rolls, you might get 11 evens (0.55). After 50 rolls, maybe 26 evens (0.52). After 100 rolls, maybe 44 evens (0.44). After 1000 rolls, it would likely be very close to 0.5, like 0.498. The experimental probability is not exactly 0.5 even after many trials, but it approaches it.

    err-prob-complement-miscalculation

    Students may compute P(A') as 1 + P(A) or forget to subtract from 1.

    Explain: The complement of an event A is the set of outcomes not in A. The probability of the complement is P(A') = 1 - P(A). This is because the total probability of all outcomes is 1.

    Example: From a bag of 20 marbles (4 blue, 6 orange, 7 green, 3 purple), P(green) = 7/20. So P(not green) = 1 - 7/20 = 13/20.

    err-prob-favorable-count-cards

    Students often miscount the number of favorable outcomes in a deck of cards, e.g., thinking there is only 1 ace or confusing aces with face cards, leading to incorrect probabilities.

    Explain: A standard deck of 52 cards has 4 suits (clubs, hearts, diamonds, spades), each with 13 cards: Ace, 2–10, Jack, Queen, King. Face cards are Jack, Queen, King – 3 per suit, total 12. Aces are not face cards. To count favorable outcomes, identify exactly how many cards match the event (e.g., 4 aces, 12 face cards).

    Example: Probability of drawing a face card: favorable outcomes = 12 face cards; total outcomes = 52. So P(face) = 12/52 = 3/13.

    err-prob-sample-space-size

    Students may incorrectly determine the total number of outcomes in a sample space (e.g., for two dice, using 11 instead of 36).

    Explain: The sample space is the set of all possible outcomes. Its size is found by counting all distinct outcomes. For a single die, there are 6 outcomes. For two dice, since each die is independent, you multiply the number of outcomes for each die: 6 × 6 = 36 ordered pairs. Common mistakes include adding (6+6=12) or using only combinations (21). Always consider if order matters — here order matters because (die1,die2) is recorded.

    Example: Roll a single die: sample space = {1,2,3,4,5,6}, size = 6. Roll two dice: sample space has 36 ordered pairs, from (1,1) to (6,6).
