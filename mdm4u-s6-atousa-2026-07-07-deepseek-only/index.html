Q3. Which statement about experimental probability is true?

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student may think experimental probability always equals theoretical or is completely unpredictable.

Remediation → err-experimental-probability-law-large-numbers | Retest → err-experimental-probability-law-large-numbers

  1. A. Experimental probability always equals theoretical probability after 10 trials.
    Not always; it varies but tends to stabilize with many trials.
  2. ✓ B. Experimental probability becomes more reliable as the number of trials increases.
    Correct! As trials increase, experimental probability approaches theoretical probability.
  3. C. Experimental probability is always greater than theoretical probability.
    It can be greater or less, but tends to approach the theoretical value.
  4. D. Experimental probability is the same as theoretical probability if the experiment is fair.
    No, experimental probability is based on actual outcomes and may differ from theoretical even in a fair experiment.

Remediation → err-theoretical-probability | Retest → err-theoretical-probability

    Sample: The probability of not drawing an ace is 1 minus the probability of drawing an ace. Since P(ace) = 4/52 = 1/13, then P(not ace) = 1 - 1/13 = 12/13. This uses the complement rule.

    补救模块

    err-theoretical-probability

    The student may think the probability is just the number of favorable outcomes without considering the total, or may invert the fraction favorable/total.

    Explain: Theoretical probability is calculated using the formula: P(event) = number of favorable outcomes / total number of possible outcomes. All outcomes must be equally likely. For example, in a fair die, each face has probability 1/6.

    Example: For a fair six-sided die, the probability of rolling a 3 is 1/6 because there is 1 favorable outcome (rolling a 3) and 6 total possible outcomes.

    err-experimental-probability-law-large-numbers

    Students often think experimental probability is always equal to theoretical probability or does not change as trials increase.

    Explain: Experimental probability is the ratio of the number of times an event occurs to the total number of trials. According to the Law of Large Numbers, as the number of trials increases, the experimental probability tends to get closer to the theoretical probability. For example, when rolling a fair die many times, the experimental probability of rolling a 3 will approach 1/6.

    Example: If you roll a die 10 times, you might get a 3 twice (experimental probability = 2/10 = 0.2). After 1000 rolls, the experimental probability will likely be very close to 1/6 ≈ 0.167.
