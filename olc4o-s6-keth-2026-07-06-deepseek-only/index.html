Q1. When you see a table of contents and headings in a textbook, what is their main purpose?

errorId: err-text-features-decorative | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Reading for Meaning: read and demonstrate an understanding of a variety of infor

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Belief that features like headings and charts are only for decoration.

Remediation → err-text-features-decorative | Retest → err-text-features-decorative

  1. A. To make the page look more interesting and break up the text.
    Text features are not just decorative; they help readers navigate and understand the structure of the text.
  2. ✓ B. To quickly locate sections and understand the organization of the content.
    Correct! Table of contents and headings are functional tools that help readers find and organize information.
  3. C. To add decoration between paragraphs.
    That is not their main purpose. They serve a navigational function.
  4. D. To replace the need to read the full text.
    They support reading, not replace it. You still need to read the text carefully.

Q2. A student always uses Google Translate for every unfamiliar word when reading an English informational text. Which statement best critiques this strategy?

errorId: err-translation-reliance | Bloom: analyze | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Thinking | Action: analyze | OSSD: Reading for Meaning: read and demonstrate an understanding of a variety of infor

Learning Goal: lg-5

Misconception: Belief that translation tools are always beneficial with no drawbacks for learning.

Remediation → err-translation-reliance | Retest → err-translation-reliance

  1. A. It is efficient and helps you understand everything immediately.
    While it may give quick answers, it prevents you from developing the ability to infer meaning from context.
  2. ✓ B. It prevents you from practicing skills like inferring meaning from context and building inference skills.
    Correct! Relying on translation circumvents the active process of figuring out meaning, which is essential for language growth.
  3. C. It is the best way to learn new vocabulary because you see the translation immediately.
    Seeing a translation does not necessarily lead to long-term retention or correct usage. Active engagement is more effective.
  4. D. It is only bad if the translation is inaccurate; otherwise it is fine.
    Even accurate translations can create dependency and reduce opportunities for deep processing of the language.

Learning Goal: lg-4

Misconception: Belief that memorizing definitions is sufficient for vocabulary learning.

Remediation → err-memorization-over-use | Retest → err-memorization-over-use

  1. ✓ A. They will quickly forget most of the words because they are not used in context.
    Correct! Without active use, words fade from memory. Practice in reading and writing reinforces learning.
  2. B. They will have a large, permanent vocabulary that they can use correctly.

  4. D. They should only memorize words they see often; that would be more efficient.

    补救模块

    err-text-features-decorative

    Students think that text features like headings, charts, and indices are primarily for decoration and do not serve a functional role in comprehension.

    Explain: Text features in informational texts are functional tools that help readers navigate, locate, and understand information. They include text organizers (table of contents, index, glossary), graphic aids (maps, charts, diagrams), and typographic effects (bold, italics, font size). Ignoring them makes reading harder.

    Example: In your Grade 9 geography textbook, the table of contents helps you find the chapter on Canada. A diagram of a river shows how it erodes landforms. These are not decorations.

    err-translation-reliance

    Belief that translation tools always help learning with no drawbacks, ignoring the importance of developing inference skills.

    Explain: Using translation tools for every unknown word prevents you from practicing the skill of inferring meaning from context. This skill is critical for reading informational texts where you often encounter new vocabulary. Over-reliance on translation also reduces your ability to think in English and can slow down your reading speed. Instead, try to guess the meaning from surrounding words and sentences before reaching for a dictionary. Remember the feedback formula: like, didn't work, different. In this case: I liked that I understand the word; it didn't work because I didn't practice inference; next time I will try to guess first.

    Example: When reading a science article about climate change, you see the word 'precipitation'. Instead of immediately translating, look at context clues: 'The amount of precipitation, such as rain and snow, has increased.' From 'rain and snow', you can infer precipitation means water falling from the sky. This builds your ability to understand English naturally.

    err-memorization-over-use

    Many students believe that memorizing definitions alone is sufficient for vocabulary learning. They underestimate the importance of using new words in context (reading, writing, speaking) to retain and apply them.

    Explain: Memorizing vocabulary without using it in context is ineffective because the brain does not form strong connections. Words that are not used are quickly forgotten. To truly learn a word, you need to encounter it multiple times in meaningful contexts and practice using it actively. Learning a word is like learning a new skill: you have to practice it.

    Example: For example, a student memorizes a list of 8 new words from a science article but never uses them in sentences. A week later, they recall very few. Instead, if they write a short paragraph using 5-6 of the new words, they are more likely to remember them and use them correctly.

    err-reading-stance-same

    Treating informational texts like fiction without adjusting reading stance.

    Explain: Informational texts provide information related to a particular idea, distinct from fiction. They use text features like organizers, graphic aids, and typographic effects to help locate and understand information. Unlike fiction, where you focus on plot and characters, reading informational texts requires you to extract information, evaluate evidence, and ask strategic questions: who is the author, what is their perspective, are there other justifiable conclusions?

    Example: Compare reading a short story (focus on feelings and plot) vs. a Grade 9 geography textbook (use index, headings, maps, and ask about the author's perspective on climate data).

    err-background-knowledge-ignore

    Background knowledge is not important for understanding informational texts; the text itself should be sufficient.

    Explain: Background knowledge significantly impacts comprehension of informational texts. It helps you connect new information to what you already know, making it easier to understand and remember. Without activating prior knowledge, you may miss deeper meanings or struggle to evaluate the author's claims.

    Example: When reading a Grade 9 geography textbook, your knowledge of basic geography (like map reading and climate zones) helps you grasp new concepts. For the New Forest National Park text, recalling what you know about forests and parks allows you to better visualize and evaluate the information.
