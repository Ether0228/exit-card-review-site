Q1. A car starts at a reference point and moves to a point 10 m east. Which statement is correct?

errorId: err-position-displacement-confusion | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of uniform and non-uniform linear motion, in one an

Official: B3 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Students may think displacement equals position or that position requires displacement to be measured.

Remediation → err-position-displacement-confusion | Retest → err-position-displacement-confusion

  1. ✓ A. The car's final position is 10 m east of the reference point.
  2. B. The car's displacement is 0 m because it started at the reference point.
    Displacement is change in position, not absolute location. The car moved, so displacement is 10 m east.
  3. C. The car's position cannot be determined without knowing the distance traveled.
    Position is defined relative to a reference point; here the final position is known as 10 m east from the origin.
  4. D. The car's final position is 10 m, and displacement is also 10 m so they are the same quantity.
    Though numerically equal in this case, position is a coordinate, and displacement is a vector change. They are conceptually different.

Q2. A car has a velocity of -5 m/s. What does this tell you about its motion?

errorId: err-velocity-sign-speed | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of uniform and non-uniform linear motion, in one an

Official: B3 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Thinking a negative velocity means the object is decelerating or moving slowly.

Remediation → err-velocity-sign-speed | Retest → err-velocity-sign-speed

  1. ✓ A. The car is moving backwards at 5 m/s.
  2. B. The car is moving slowly because the value is negative.
    The magnitude is 5 m/s, so speed is 5 m/s. Negative sign indicates direction, not slowness.
  3. C. The car is decelerating.
    Velocity sign does not indicate acceleration. Velocity is constant here; deceleration requires a change in velocity.
  4. D. The car's speed is -5 m/s.
    Speed is a scalar and is always non-negative. The speed is 5 m/s, not -5 m/s.

    补救模块

    err-position-displacement-confusion

    Confusing position with displacement; thinking position is the same as displacement or that it requires movement.

    Explain: Position is an object's specific location relative to a reference point. It is like a coordinate on a number line. Displacement is the change in position, a vector from start to finish. They are only equal if the object starts at the reference point and moves in a straight line without changing direction, but they are different concepts.

    Example: A student stands at the classroom door (reference point). They walk 5 m east to a desk. Their final position is 5 m east of the door; displacement is also 5 m east. If they then walk back to the door, final position is 0 m (at door), but displacement is -5 m east (or 5 m west) because they returned to start.

    err-velocity-sign-speed

    The student believes that a negative sign on velocity indicates a slower speed, when actually the sign only indicates direction and speed is the absolute value (magnitude).

    Explain: Velocity is a vector: its sign (+ or -) tells you the direction of motion, not how fast. Speed is the magnitude (absolute value) of velocity. For example, a velocity of -2 m/s means the object is moving at 2 m/s in the negative direction – it is not 'slow' just because it's negative. A velocity of 5 m/s has a speed of 5 m/s, which is faster than 2 m/s regardless of sign.

    Example: A car moving at -2 m/s has a speed of 2 m/s. A car moving at 5 m/s has a speed of 5 m/s. The car with velocity 5 m/s is faster, even though its sign is positive. The negative sign only tells you the direction is opposite to the chosen reference.

    err-acceleration-dv-over-dt

    Misapplying acceleration as change in position over time (Δx/Δt) instead of change in velocity over time (Δv/Δt).

    Explain: Acceleration is a vector quantity that measures how quickly velocity changes. It is calculated using the formula a = Δv/Δt, where Δv is the change in velocity and Δt is the time interval. This is different from velocity, which is the rate of change of position (Δx/Δt). So, acceleration tells you how much your speed or direction changes each second.

    Example: A car's velocity changes from 5 m/s to -2 m/s in 3 s. The acceleration is a = ( -2 m/s - 5 m/s ) / 3 s = -7 m/s / 3 s ≈ -2.33 m/s². The negative sign indicates acceleration opposite to the initial direction.

    err-pt-graph-slope-interpretation

    The student thinks the sign of the slope on a position-time graph indicates speed, rather than direction. They may believe a negative slope means the object is moving slowly, when speed is given by the absolute value of the slope.

    Explain: On a position-time graph, the slope represents velocity. The steepness of the slope (magnitude) tells you the speed: a steeper slope means higher speed. The sign of the slope (+ or -) tells you the direction of motion: positive slope means forward, negative slope means backward. A common mistake is to think a negative slope means slow motion; actually, a steep negative slope means fast backward motion.

    Example: Consider a graph with two segments: from t=0 to 5 s, the slope is +5 m/s (steep positive). From t=5 to 8 s, the slope is -2 m/s (gentle negative). The speed during the first segment is 5 m/s, during the second is 2 m/s. The second segment has a negative slope but lower speed. So sign indicates direction, not speed.

    err-vt-area-misuse

    The student mistakenly thinks the area under a velocity-time graph always gives the total distance traveled, ignoring that areas below the axis (negative velocity) represent motion in the opposite direction and should be subtracted to find displacement.

    Explain: The area under a velocity-time graph represents displacement, which is the net change in position, not the total distance traveled. Displacement accounts for direction: areas above the time axis are positive (forward motion), areas below are negative (backward motion). Distance would be the sum of the absolute values of these areas.

    Example: Consider an object moving at 5 m/s for 30 s (forward) then at -2 m/s for 30 s (backward). The displacement is (5 m/s × 30 s) + (-2 m/s × 30 s) = 150 m - 60 m = 90 m. The total distance traveled is |5|×30 + |-2|×30 = 150 + 60 = 210 m. Notice displacement (90 m) is not the same as distance (210 m).

    err-at-constant-only

    Assuming acceleration-time graphs in Grade 11 can show non-constant acceleration.

    Explain: In Grade 11 Physics, acceleration-time graphs only cover scenarios where acceleration is constant. The graph is a horizontal line. The area under the graph equals the total change in velocity.

    Example: Consider the segment where an object moves at constant velocity -2 m/s for 3 s. Its acceleration is constant at 0 m/s². The a-t graph is a horizontal line at a=0. The area under that line (height 0 times width 3 s) is 0 m/s, which equals the change in velocity (since velocity is constant).

    err-equations-applicability

    The five core equations for uniform acceleration (v = v₀ + at, Δd = v₀t + ½aΔt², etc.) are applied to situations where acceleration is not constant, leading to incorrect results. Students may think these equations always work regardless of the motion's nature.

    Explain: The five kinematic equations are derived assuming constant acceleration (uniform). If acceleration changes over time, these equations no longer accurately describe the motion. For example, in a velocity-time graph, the equations assume a straight line (constant slope). If acceleration varies, the graph curves, and the simple formulas fail.

    Example: Consider an object moving at 5 m/s for 3 s, then instantly switching to -2 m/s for 3 s. The acceleration is not constant (infinite at the switch). Using Δd = v₀t + ½aΔt² for the entire 6 s would be invalid. Only the total displacement of 19 m can be computed piecewise (5 m/s for 3 s gives 15 m, then -2 m/s for 3 s gives -6 m, total 9 m? Wait, 15 + (-6) = 9 m, but lesson says total displacement 19 m? Actually from lesson: 5 s at 5 m/s then 3 s at -2 m/s gives 25 - 6 = 19 m. But using 3 s each: 15 - 6 = 9 m. So the example is different. I'll use the lesson's two-phase: 5 s at 5 m/s gives 25 m, 3 s at -2 m/s gives -6 m, sum 19 m. The acceleration is zero in each phase but changes at the boundary, so not uniform overall.
