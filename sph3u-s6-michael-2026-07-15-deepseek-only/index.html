Q1. Which of the following is a core assumption in the definition of projectile motion?

errorId: err-projectile-motion-forgetting-air-resistance | Bloom: understand | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Knowledge and Understanding | Action: interpret | OSSD: Demonstrate an understanding of uniform and non-uniform linear motion, in one an

Official: B3 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Student may think air resistance is included or not relevant.

Remediation → err-projectile-motion-forgetting-air-resistance | Retest → err-projectile-motion-forgetting-air-resistance

  1. ✓ A. Air resistance is negligible.
    Correct. Projectile motion assumes no air resistance so the only force is gravity.
  2. B. Air resistance is significant and must be included.
    In standard projectile motion models, air resistance is ignored to simplify calculations.
  3. C. The object has a constant horizontal acceleration.
    Horizontal acceleration is zero, not constant non-zero.
  4. D. The object's mass affects its vertical acceleration.
    In free fall (ignoring air resistance), all objects accelerate at g regardless of mass.

Q2. A flower pot falls from rest (v₀ = 0) from a height of 10 m. Which kinematic equation is best to find the time to reach the ground?

errorId: err-kinematic-equation-selection | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Investigate uniform and non-uniform linear motion in qualitative and quantitativ

Official: B2 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Student may use an equation that doesn't include initial velocity or misidentifies given variables.

Remediation → err-kinematic-equation-selection | Retest → err-kinematic-equation-selection

  1. A. v = u + at
    That equation uses final velocity, not displacement – insufficient given info.
  2. ✓ B. s = ut + ½at²
    Correct. With u=0, s=-10 m, a=-9.8 m/s², this equation directly yields t.
  3. C. v² = u² + 2as
    This equation would find final velocity, not time.
  4. D. s = ( (u+v)/2 ) t
    This requires both u and v; v is unknown.

Q3. A ball is launched horizontally from a cliff. Which approach is correct for finding the time of flight?

errorId: err-independent-motion-combining | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L2

Official: B3 | Node: SPH3U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/2009science11_12.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Student believes a single kinematic equation can incorporate both horizontal and vertical variables simultaneously.

Remediation → err-independent-motion-combining | Retest → err-independent-motion-combining

  1. ✓ A. Use the vertical displacement and vertical acceleration in a kinematic equation.
    Correct. Time of flight depends only on vertical motion; horizontal motion does not affect it.
  2. B. Use the horizontal velocity and vertical displacement in the same equation.
    Horizontal and vertical quantities must be kept separate; they cannot be combined in one equation.
  3. C. Use the horizontal displacement and horizontal acceleration.
    Horizontal acceleration is zero, so that gives no information about time of fall.
  4. D. Use the magnitude of initial velocity (the launch speed) directly.
    The initial speed is split into components; the time depends on the vertical component, not the magnitude.

补救模块

err-projectile-motion-forgetting-air-resistance

The student does not internalize that the standard definition of projectile motion assumes negligible air resistance, which simplifies analysis to only gravitational force.

Explain: Projectile motion is defined as the motion of an object in free fall under gravity, assuming no air resistance. This assumption means the only force acting is gravity, so horizontal acceleration is zero and vertical acceleration is constant at -9.8 m/s². Without this assumption, calculations would become much more complex.

Example: In the flower pot example from class, we calculated the time to fall from 10 m using equations that ignore air resistance. The result was 1.43 s. If air resistance were significant, the actual time would be longer because air drag opposes motion.

err-kinematic-equation-selection

The student may not understand how to choose the appropriate kinematic equation based on known and unknown variables, often picking an equation that does not contain the needed unknown or that requires a variable not given.

Explain: When solving projectile motion problems, you must select the right kinematic equation from the five standard ones: v = u + at, s = ut + ½at², s = ((u+v)/2)t, v² = u² + 2as, and s = vt - ½at². To choose, list what you know (given) and what you need (unknown). Then pick the equation that includes all knowns and the unknown. For example, if you know u, s, a and need t, use s = ut + ½at² because it directly relates those four variables.

Example: For the falling flower pot from rest (u=0) from a height of 10 m (s = -10 m) with a = -9.8 m/s², to find the time t you use s = ut + ½at². Substituting: -10 = 0·t + 0.5·(-9.8)·t² gives t² ≈ 2.04, so t ≈ 1.43 s.

err-independent-motion-combining

Student believes that horizontal and vertical components can be combined in one equation, not recognizing that they are independent and must be treated separately.

Explain: In projectile motion, the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. This means you must use separate equations for each direction. The horizontal acceleration is zero, so horizontal velocity is constant. The vertical acceleration is constant at -9.8 m/s². Never mix horizontal displacement with vertical acceleration or vice versa.

Example: A ball is thrown horizontally at 5 m/s from a height of 10 m. To find the time to hit the ground, you only use vertical motion: initial vertical velocity = 0 m/s, displacement = -10 m, acceleration = -9.8 m/s². The horizontal speed does not affect the time.

err-sign-convention-negative

Student does not apply a consistent sign convention for direction, often treating downward quantities as positive instead of negative.

Explain: In kinematic equations, direction matters. We define a coordinate system: typically upward is positive, downward is negative. This means displacement downward (e.g., falling) and acceleration due to gravity (always downward) are negative numbers. Using the wrong sign leads to incorrect answers. For example, a flower pot falling 10 m has displacement s = -10 m, and acceleration a = -9.8 m/s². If you treat these as positive, you would get a different (wrong) time.

Example: Flower pot: released from rest (u=0) at height 10 m. Using s = ut + ½at² gives -10 = 0*t + ½(-9.8)t² → -10 = -4.9t² → t² = 10/4.9 ≈ 2.04 → t ≈ 1.43 s. If you incorrectly used a = +9.8, you would get t² = -10/4.9, impossible.

err-constant-horizontal-velocity

The student thinks that horizontal velocity changes during projectile motion, possibly due to gravity or other forces.

Explain: In ideal projectile motion, there is no horizontal force acting on the object (assuming no air resistance). Therefore, horizontal acceleration is zero. When acceleration is zero, velocity remains constant. This means the horizontal component of velocity does not change throughout the flight.

Example: A ball is thrown horizontally with an initial speed of 5 m/s. At the moment it leaves the hand, its horizontal velocity is 5 m/s. Even after 2 seconds of falling, the horizontal velocity is still 5 m/s because there is no horizontal force to speed it up or slow it down.

err-vertical-acceleration-zero

The student mistakenly believes that vertical acceleration is zero in free fall, possibly confusing it with the absence of horizontal acceleration.

Explain: In projectile motion near Earth's surface, the only force acting (ignoring air resistance) is gravity, which causes a constant downward acceleration of 9.8 m/s². This acceleration is always present throughout the motion, regardless of whether the object is moving up or down. Horizontal acceleration is zero, but vertical acceleration is not.

Example: Consider a flower pot dropped from rest at height 10 m. Using the equation s = ut + ½at², with s = -10 m, u = 0, and a = -9.8 m/s², we find the fall time is about 1.43 s. If vertical acceleration were zero, the pot would never accelerate downward, contradicting the observed motion.
