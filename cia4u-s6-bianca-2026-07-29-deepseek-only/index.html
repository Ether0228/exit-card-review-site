补救模块

err-worker-rights-moral-vs-legal

Moral rights are based on human dignity and conscience but are not necessarily codified in law or contracts. Legal rights are explicitly written in employment contracts or legislation and are enforceable by law.

Explain: Worker rights fall into two categories: moral rights and legal rights. Moral rights are non-contractual entitlements rooted in human nature and public conscience, such as the right to respect, safe conditions, and fair compensation. These are not always legally enforceable unless incorporated into law. Legal rights are codified in employment contracts, covering paid benefits, resignation, negotiation, and refusal of unsuitable contracts. They are enforceable through legal processes. It is important to distinguish between what is morally expected and what is legally required from employers and the government.

Example: Consider the right to fair compensation. Morally, a worker deserves fair pay that matches their skills and qualifications. Legally, minimum wage laws guarantee a baseline compensation, but an employer may pay above that. If an employer pays less than minimum wage, that is a legal violation. However, if they pay minimum wage but the worker feels it is unfair given their skills, that is a moral issue that may not be legally actionable unless there is a contract specifying higher pay.

err-employment-at-will-absolute

The student believes employment at will gives employers an absolute right to fire employees for any reason, ignoring legal exceptions that protect employees from discrimination and retaliation.

Explain: Employment at will means employers can generally hire, fire, or promote freely, and employees can resign at any time. However, this is not absolute. Exceptions include: (1) public policy – cannot fire for reporting violations or exercising legal rights; (2) implied contract – if employment manuals or promises create an expectation of job security; (3) good faith and fair dealing – cannot fire to avoid paying benefits. Dismissals based on protected characteristics (e.g., age, gender, pregnancy, union membership) are automatically wrongful discharge.

Example: An employer fires an employee for reporting a safety violation to the regulator. That is illegal under the public policy exception – the employee is protected from retaliation.

err-strike-requirements

Misunderstanding that strikes can be called without a vote or notice, or that all strikes are illegal.

Explain: In Canada, a legal strike requires two steps: first, a secret ballot vote where a majority of union members must approve the strike; second, the union must give the employer at least 7 days' advance notice before the strike begins. This ensures democratic decision-making and gives the employer time to prepare.

Example: A union wants to strike for better wages. They hold a secret ballot; 60% vote yes. Then they notify the employer in writing. The strike can start no earlier than 7 days after that notice.

err-income-inequality-definition

Equating income inequality with poverty; not distinguishing between unequal distribution and absolute deprivation.

Explain: Income inequality measures the distribution of income across a population (e.g., the gap between high and low earners). Poverty measures the percentage of people whose income falls below a fixed threshold (poverty line). They are distinct concepts: high inequality can exist with low poverty, and vice versa. For example, a country may have a large gap between rich and poor but most people earn above the poverty line, while another may have a small gap but many fall below the line.

Example: Consider two countries: one has a large income gap but few in poverty; the other has a small gap but many in poverty. This shows inequality and poverty are different. The Unit 2 test covers income inequality definitions and government policies like minimum wage, progressive tax, employment insurance, child tax credits, and public education.
