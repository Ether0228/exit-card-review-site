Q1. Given f(x) = 2 sin(3(x – π/4)) + 1, which mapping correctly generates points on the transformed graph from the base sine function?

Official: B2 | Node: MHF4U-B-OE02 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-1

Misconception: Applying the horizontal shift before factoring k, leading to an incorrect phase shift.

Remediation → err-transformation-order | Retest → err-transformation-order

  1. ✓ A. (x/3 + π/4, 2y + 1)
    Correct. The mapping factors k=3: (x/k + d, ay + c) = (x/3 + π/4, 2y + 1).
  2. B. (x/3 – π/4, 2y + 1)
    The horizontal shift is +π/4, not –π/4. Check the sign inside the brackets.
  3. C. (x/3 + π/4, 2y – 1)
    The vertical shift is +1, so the y-coordinate should be 2y+1, not 2y–1.
  4. D. (3x + π/4, y/2 + 1)
    The horizontal factor should be x/3, not 3x, and the amplitude is 2 not 1/2.

Q2. Which statement correctly describes the required conventions for a complete graph of a trigonometric function?

Learning Goal: lg-2

Misconception: Believing that arrows are optional or that axis labels do not require units.

Remediation → err-graph-arrow-labels | Retest → err-graph-arrow-labels

  1. ✓ A. Ends of the graph must have arrows, and axes must be labelled with variable and unit.
    Yes. Arrows indicate infinite behaviour, and labels provide context.
  2. B. Ends do not need arrows; axes must be labelled with variable only.
    Trigonometric functions extend infinitely; arrows are essential. Units are also needed when real-world quantities are involved.
  3. C. Ends must have arrows; axes must be labelled with variable but unit is optional.
    Units provide meaning to numerical values; they are required.
  4. D. Arrows are only needed if the function continues; axes must be labelled with variable and unit.
    The function always continues; arrows must always be drawn.

Q3. A sinusoidal function models daily temperature. The maximum temperature is 28°C and the minimum is 14°C. What are the midline and amplitude?

errorId: err-midline-amplitude | Bloom: apply | Scaffold: L1

Achievement: Application | Action: apply | OSSD: Solve problems involving trigonometric equations and prove trigonometric identit

Official: B3 | Node: MHF4U-B-OE03 | Source: https://www.edu.gov.on.ca/eng/curriculum/secondary/math1112currb.pdf

Learning Goal: lg-3

Misconception: Using (max+min)/2 for amplitude or using max−min without halving.

Remediation → err-midline-amplitude | Retest → err-midline-amplitude

  1. ✓ A. Midline 21°C, amplitude 7°C
    Correct. Midline = (28+14)/2 = 21, amplitude = (28−14)/2 = 7.
  2. B. Midline 21°C, amplitude 14°C
    14°C is the total range, not the amplitude. Amplitude is half the range.
  3. C. Midline 14°C, amplitude 7°C
    The midline is the average, not the minimum.
  4. D. Midline 7°C, amplitude 21°C
    These numbers are swapped. Midline is the average, amplitude is half the difference.

补救模块

err-transformation-order

Applying the horizontal shift before factoring the coefficient k, leading to an incorrect phase shift (e.g., shifting x without dividing by k).

Explain: Transformations of trigonometric functions are applied in two stages. First apply amplitude and horizontal stretch/compression (affecting x). Then apply phase shift and vertical shift. The mapping rule for y = a sin(k(x-d)) + c is (x/k + d, ay + c). If you shift before dividing by k, you change the phase shift by a factor of k.

Example: For f(x) = 3 sin 2(x + π/6) – 1, first build the intermediate function 3 sin 2x (amplitude 3, period π). Then map each point (x, y) on that intermediate graph to (x/2 – π/6, 3y – 1). This correctly applies the left shift π/6 and the vertical shift –1 after the horizontal compression.

err-graph-arrow-labels

Arrows are optional extras, or axis labels only need the variable name and not the unit.

Explain: A complete trigonometric graph must be drawn on a standard four‑quadrant Cartesian plane. Arrows at both ends indicate that the curve continues infinitely, which is true for all sine and cosine functions. Labels with the variable and its unit tell the reader what the numbers represent. Without these, the graph is incomplete and cannot be used for modelling or communication.

Example: For the temperature model T(t)=6cos(π/12(t–17))+19, when t is in hours and T is in °C, the horizontal axis must be labelled 't (hours)' and the vertical axis 'T (°C)'. Both ends of the curve must have arrows, because the temperature pattern repeats before t=0 and after any finite interval.

err-midline-amplitude

The student confuses the formula for midline with the formula for amplitude, using (max + min)/2 for amplitude and (max – min)/2 for midline. In reality, midline is the average value (middle of the range) and amplitude is half the vertical distance between max and min.

Explain: For any sinusoidal function, the midline is the horizontal line halfway between the maximum and minimum values: C = (max + min)/2. The amplitude is the vertical distance from the midline to the maximum (or minimum), which is half the total range: A = (max – min)/2. These two parameters describe different features: midline tells you the vertical shift, amplitude tells you how far the curve moves above and below that shift.

Example: For the temperature model T(t) = 6 cos(π/12 (t – 17)) + 19, the maximum temperature is 25°C and the minimum is 13°C. Then midline = (25+13)/2 = 19°C, amplitude = (25−13)/2 = 6°C. Notice the +19 in the equation is the midline and the coefficient 6 is the amplitude.

err-sine-vs-cosine

The student does not base the sine-vs-cosine choice on the graph's starting behaviour. They pick one function arbitrarily instead of checking whether the maximum occurs near the y-axis (cosine) or whether the midline is crossed near the origin (sine).

Explain: When choosing between sine and cosine for a sinusoidal model, look at the graph at t = 0. Without a phase shift, cosine begins at a maximum, while sine begins at the midline. In the lesson, a temperature model used cosine because the maximum occurred at t = 17, so after the phase shift the crest was near the y-axis. If data cross the midline near the origin and then rise, sine is the better match. There is no fixed rule; the starting point of the period determines the function type.

Example: For T(t) = 6 cos(π/12(t - 17)) + 19, the maximum of 25°C occurs at t = 17, so cosine is chosen because the crest is shifted 17 hours right. If the graph instead crossed the midline at t = 0 and then rose, a sine model would be more natural.

err-calculator-mode

Assuming the calculator automatically handles radian/degree regardless of mode; forgetting to check mode when solving trigonometric equations with radians.

Explain: When working with trigonometric functions in mathematics, angles are often measured in radians. For example, the function h(t) = 10 sin(2πt + 1.5π) + 15 uses 2π and 1.5π, which are radian measures. If your calculator is set to degree mode, it interprets these numbers as degrees (e.g., 2π ≈ 6.28 degrees instead of 6.28 radians), leading to dramatically incorrect results. Always check that your calculator is in radian mode when the argument includes π or when the period is expressed in terms of π.

Example: Consider solving h(t) = 18 for the spring model. The equation becomes 10 sin(2πt + 1.5π) + 15 = 18, so sin(2πt + 1.5π) = 0.3. In radian mode, arcsin(0.3) ≈ 0.3047 rad, then solving for t gives t ≈ 0.3 seconds. In degree mode, arcsin(0.3) ≈ 17.46 degrees, and mixing degrees with radian terms like 2πt leads to t ≈ 17 seconds (as seen in the error). This is why radian mode is crucial.

err-intersection-direction

The student treats any solution of h(t)=18 as valid and ignores the monotonic direction (increasing vs decreasing) required by the problem.

Explain: Some problems ask for times when an object is moving upward. On a sinusoid, a horizontal line like y=18 crosses the curve on both rising and falling parts. Only intersections where the function is increasing (derivative > 0) are valid. For h(t)=10 sin(2πt+1.5π)+15, rewrite as h(t)=10 sin(2π(t+0.75))+15. Intersections with y=18 occur periodically; on the upward segments the accepted times are t≈0.3, 1.3, 2.3.

Example: The line y=18 meets the spring curve many times. At t=0.3 the curve is rising; at t=1.3 and 2.3 it is rising again one period later. If you also identify an intersection on a falling section, reject it because derivative < 0.
