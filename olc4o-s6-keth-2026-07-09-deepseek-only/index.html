补救模块

err-summary-personal-opinion

Students may think emotional adjectives, personal evaluations, or subjective language are acceptable in summaries, failing to recognize that summaries must be neutral 'clean mirrors' reflecting only facts and the original author's views with zero personal opinion.

Explain: Summarization is a systematic, mechanical process of distilling long texts to core essentials, not creative rewriting. Summarizers must be neutral 'clean mirrors', reflecting only facts and original author's views, with zero personal opinions. Emotional adjectives, personal anecdotes, conversational asides, and subjective evaluations are forbidden. For example, in a news summary, you report facts like 'The new bridge opened July 12, connecting the east and west sides of the valley' without adding 'wonderful' or 'terrible'.

Example: Consider the objective language side: 'The new bridge opened July 12, connecting the east and west sides of the valley.' The subjective side is: 'This wonderful new bridge finally opened to stop the terrible traffic.' The first is neutral; the second includes personal opinion and emotion. In the Calgary Aerospace case, you extract only the facts: the neurodivergent intern groups, specific tasks, Calgary headquarters, documented timeline – no additional praise or criticism.

err-summary-direct-quotes

The student believes that including direct quotes adds credibility or authority to a summary, not realizing that summaries must be entirely paraphrased in the writer's own neutral academic voice.

Explain: In formal academic summaries, direct quotes are almost never used. The summarizer must rephrase all key points and evidence in their own neutral academic voice. Quotations break the flow, introduce unnecessary narrative, and violate the principle that a summary should be a 'clean mirror' reflecting only facts and the original author's views without any added content or stylistic interference.

Example: Original text: 'The CEO stated, "Our profits have doubled this quarter, which is an outstanding achievement."' Poor summary (with quote): 'The CEO stated, "Our profits have doubled."' Good summary (paraphrase): 'The CEO reported a doubling of quarterly profits.'

err-5w-incomplete

Students may think 'How' is one of the Ws or may forget to include all five categories, leading to incomplete fact extraction.

Explain: The 5 W Framework (Who, What, Where, When, Why) is a mandatory structured data collection tool for objective fact extraction. It ensures you capture all essential information. For example, in the Calgary Aerospace case, you must identify: Who (neurodivergent intern groups), What (specific tasks), Where (Calgary headquarters), When (documented timeline), Why (purpose).

Example: For a news report with headline 'Local High School Wins State Championship', the 5 Ws are: Who=The Wildcats, Where=City Stadium, When=May 26th, Why=Record-breaking performance, How=Coordinated team effort (note: 'How' is extra, not a W).

err-summary-type-confusion

The student mistakenly applies the word count standard for lay summaries (100–1000 words) to news summaries, not recognizing that each summary type has distinct formatting requirements.

Explain: There are three main summary types: news, scholarly, and lay. Each has unique standards. News summaries require geopolitical context and a strict line limit of exactly 6 lines. Scholarly summaries must name the source and date, use neutral reporting verbs, and avoid personal evaluation. Lay summaries translate jargon for non-experts, use active voice and vivid analogies, and have a word limit of 100–1000 words. Applying the wrong standard (e.g., using a 6-line limit for a scholarly summary or a 1000-word length for a news summary) is a common error.

Example: For a news article, a correct summary might be exactly 6 lines covering the Who, What, Where, When, Why. For a scholarly article, a correct summary would open with 'In a 2023 study, Smith identifies...' without evaluating. For a lay summary, a correct version might use 200 words with active voice and analogies.

err-news-lead-misidentification

Students often scan the body paragraphs first and miss the lead, believing the main idea is buried in details or quotes rather than at the very beginning.

Explain: In news reports, the main idea is almost always presented upfront in the headline or the lead paragraph (the first paragraph). The headline gives the core event, and the lead expands with the most essential facts. Body paragraphs provide supporting details, quotes, and background, but the main idea should be identified from the headline or lead. This is a key reading strategy for efficient summarization.

Example: Consider the layout: Headline: 'Local High School Wins State Championship'. Lead: 'Intense match on Saturday, May 26th, against city rivals at City Stadium.' The main idea (the Wildcats won the championship) appears in the headline; the lead adds key details (when, where, opponent). Body paragraphs would add player quotes and game stats—those are not the main idea.

err-lay-summary-passive-voice

Learners may use passive voice in lay summaries, thinking it sounds more academic or objective. However, lay summaries require active voice and positive phrasing to make the writing clear, direct, and engaging for non-expert readers.

Explain: Lay summaries are written for non-expert readers who may not be familiar with complex terminology or passive constructions. Active voice makes it clear who is performing the action, improving readability and engagement. Passive voice can obscure the subject and make sentences longer and harder to follow. For example, 'The experiment was conducted by the researchers' (passive) is less direct than 'Researchers conducted an experiment' (active). The rule: always use active voice unless there is a very specific reason not to.

Example: Passive: 'The new drug was tested on patients by the team.' → Active: 'The team tested the new drug on patients.' The active version is shorter, clearer, and tells the reader exactly who did what.

err-scholarly-evaluation

Learners think they should evaluate the quality or significance of the research in a scholarly summary, but a scholarly summary must only report the author's findings and claims using neutral language, with zero personal opinion or evaluation.

Explain: In scholarly summaries, you are a neutral reporter. Do not include any personal opinions, judgments, or evaluations about the research's quality, importance, or validity. Only present what the author states. Use neutral reporting verbs such as 'identifies', 'articulates', 'demonstrates', 'argues', or 'suggests'. Avoid words like 'importantly', 'interestingly', 'flawed', 'promising', 'groundbreaking'.

Example: Incorrect: 'In his groundbreaking 2023 study, Smith convincingly argues that climate change is accelerating, a vital finding for future policy.' (contains evaluation) Correct: 'In his 2023 study, Smith argues that climate change is accelerating.' (neutral, only reporting the claim)
