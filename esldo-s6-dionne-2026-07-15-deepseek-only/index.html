Remediation → err-thesis-points-missing | Retest → err-thesis-points-missing

补救模块

err-expository-narrative-confusion

Student confuses expository and narrative writing, thinking expository includes storytelling or aims to entertain.

Explain: Expository writing is factual, clear, and structured to inform, explain, or teach. It does not tell a story with characters or plot. Narrative writing, in contrast, tells a story with characters, setting, and plot to entertain. For example, a passage about a weekend trip with friends is narrative, while a passage explaining planetary science facts is expository.

Example: Sample passages from class: 'weekend anecdote (narrative)' vs 'planetary science facts (expository)'. Expository: 'The planet Mercury is the closest to the sun and has no atmosphere.' Narrative: 'On Saturday, I woke up early and drove to the park with my friends.'

err-thesis-points-missing

The student may think that one or two points are enough, or they forget that the thesis must preview all three body paragraphs.

Explain: In a 5-paragraph expository essay, the thesis statement must state the main argument and list exactly three supporting points. Each point will be developed in one body paragraph. For example, if your essay is about benefits of technology in school, your thesis could be: 'Technology tools improve student learning by providing easy access to information, enabling collaboration, and offering personalized practice.' This lists three points: access to information, collaboration, and personalized practice.

Example: Weak thesis: 'Technology is helpful in school.' (missing points) Better thesis: 'Technology tools improve student learning by providing easy access to information, enabling collaboration, and offering personalized practice.' (three points)

err-introduction-missing-hook

The student thinks the introduction only needs a thesis statement or background, underestimating the hook's role in engaging readers.

Explain: In an expository essay, the introduction must include three components: a hook to grab the reader's attention, background information to provide context, and a thesis statement that states the main argument and lists three supporting points. Without a hook, the reader may not feel motivated to continue reading. The hook can be a surprising fact, a question, a quote, or a vivid description that relates to the topic.

Example: In the model essay on technology tools for school, the hook is: 'Imagine a classroom where every student has a device to access information instantly.' This hook engages the reader by painting a picture related to the topic.

err-body-not-teec

The student does not know that each body paragraph must start with a topic sentence, then present evidence, explain it, and end with a closing/transition sentence. The student may think the order can be changed or that some components are optional.

Explain: In expository writing, every body paragraph must follow the TEEC structure: Topic sentence (states the main idea of the paragraph), Evidence (provides specific facts, examples, or data), Explanation (analyzes or interprets the evidence), and Closing/transition sentence (summarizes the point and smoothly moves to the next paragraph). This structure ensures clarity and logical flow.

Example: Consider a body paragraph about how technology improves student research. The topic sentence could be: 'Technology greatly enhances students' ability to conduct research.' Then evidence: 'According to a 2023 Education Weekly study, 85% of teachers reported that online tools improved student engagement.' Explanation: 'This statistic shows that when students use online databases, they find more relevant information faster, leading to better learning outcomes.' Finally, closing sentence: 'Thus, access to digital research tools is a key benefit of technology in education.'

err-conclusion-new-info

Students often think the conclusion is a place to add extra points they forgot, not realizing it must only restate and summarize without introducing new information.

Explain: The conclusion of an expository essay must restate the thesis in different words, summarize the three main points from the body paragraphs, and end with a final thought or reflection. It is strictly forbidden to introduce any new information, arguments, or evidence. New information confuses readers and weakens the essay's closure because it raises new topics that are not explained.

Example: Correct conclusion: 'In conclusion, technology tools have transformed student learning by improving access to information, enabling collaboration, and developing critical thinking skills. While these benefits are significant, students must use technology responsibly to make the most of these opportunities.' This restates thesis, summarizes three points, and gives a final thought—no new info. Incorrect conclusion: 'In conclusion, technology tools have transformed student learning. Another benefit is that they reduce paper waste, which helps the environment.' The sentence about reducing paper waste is new information not mentioned in the body, so it is not allowed.

err-evidence-not-specific

The student thinks that general opinions or unsupported claims are acceptable as evidence in expository writing, because they haven't learned to distinguish vague assertions from specific, verifiable information.

Explain: In expository writing, evidence must be specific and support your points clearly. Acceptable evidence includes facts, examples, statistics, expert opinions, and logical reasoning. Vague statements like 'many people think' or 'some studies show' are too general. Instead, use concrete details: name a source, a number, a person, or a real example.

Example: Instead of saying 'Expository writing is different from narrative,' use a specific fact from the lesson: 'Expository writing is factual and structured to inform, while narrative writing tells a story with characters and plot.' This gives a clear comparison.
